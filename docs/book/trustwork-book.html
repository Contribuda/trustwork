Part I · Why we exist

1Mission, philosophy and values

Trustwork exists so that trust in building can be seen: who built what, how well, for whom, and with whose money. It is an open engine for a gathering point of the construction industry, run as hubs by the people of each country and city, joined in one mesh, and growing ring by ring into a civic engine, because the built environment is where public money becomes visible. One record of evidence, owned by the people who earned it, under rules everyone can read.

The mission in one page¶

Builders have always had to earn trust, and every age has found a new way to make that trust legible. The masons of the cathedral cities cut a mark into each stone they dressed, so that the master could see whose work held and whose did not. The guild kept the roll of who had served their years and who had been struck off. The bill of quantities let a client compare the prices of strangers on one sheet instead of trusting a single man's word. The building inspector came after fires and collapses, as a person with the right to look and the duty to refuse. The licence register told the public which contractors the state had examined. Each of these made trust a little more visible. None of them made it portable, provable, or the property of the person who had earned it. The mark stayed in the stone. The roll stayed with the guild. The inspector's report stayed in a drawer. The licence said that someone had passed an examination once, not what they had built since.

Meanwhile the industry grew more fragmented than any other. A facade is designed by one firm, fixed by a second, clad by a third, financed by a fourth and maintained for decades by someone who never met any of them. Knowledge lives in the heads of site foremen and in folders of scanned acceptance reports. Reputation travels by telephone between people who already know each other. And where most building is done by the people who will live in it, most builders have no record at all: construction employs about seven per cent of the world's working population and is one of the three sectors with the largest share of informal work, according to UNESCO-UNEVOC and the International Labour Organization. The skill is real. The record is missing. The missing record is what keeps a mason uninsured, a family unable to borrow for its own house, and a town unable to say whether the drain it paid for was ever built. And that house is, for most families, the largest investment they will ever make, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

The missing link is not another directory, another rating site or another model. Directories list those who paid. Stars inflate until they say nothing. Models can summarise what is known, but they cannot make anything true. What is missing is a shared, honest record of who did what, under rules everyone can see: a quote frozen the moment it was sent, a delivery accepted by the person who received it, a certificate anyone can check by number, a defect recorded and then recorded as remedied, a hidden work photographed before the concrete covered it, and a reputation that is nothing more than the sum of such facts, explained, with the right to appeal, and never for sale.

Trustwork is that record and the place around it. It begins with the construction industry, because construction is where trust is most fragmented and most costly, and because the engine is designed for exactly that work: plans that become requests to people, quotes that cannot be quietly changed, agreements both sides can prove identical, evidence from the site, acceptance by the counterparty, reputation from evidence only. Then it grows outward along a line that was there from the start. A school, a clinic, a road, a drain, a market roof, a staircase on a hillside: these are where a budget stops being a number and becomes something a resident can stand in front of. If the engine can record how a private house was built, it can record how a public work was decided, tendered, built and confirmed. That is the civic mission, and it is not a change of direction. It is the next ring of the same wall.

So the engine has a second task beside the first. It is to help the state and society work together in the places that need it most: to let residents decide what is built with their money, to keep budgets and contracts open, to teach a society how its built environment is made and paid for, and to raise the culture of building and the reputation of everyone who does it well.

The places that need it most are those with informal building, weak transparency, public works that stall or vanish, housing that families build for themselves without plans or credit, and recovery after disasters; and, always, places where a lawful operator can work and the people who use the record are safe to use it. Part VIII goes country by country. The need is measured, not assumed, and the engine goes only where its own rules can hold.

Evidence is the brick. Acceptance is the mortar. Reputation is the wall.

What we are, said plainly¶

  • An open engine. Free software under the AGPL-3.0-only licence, created and maintained by Contribuda together with everyone who contributes. Anyone can read it, run it, change it and check it. Nobody has to trust us; they can trust the code and the record. It costs nothing from us, no licence fee and no implementation fee, ever; chapter 7 says why, and who earns what.
  • A gathering point for the people who build. On a hub you read sourced news and a weekly magazine, meet at events, learn with certificates anyone can check by number, are found through a profile that is yours for life, find partners through matching that explains itself, do business with frozen quotes and provable agreements, and build a reputation from what actually happened.
  • A hub anyone can run. An association, a publisher, a chamber, a university, a training body, an entrepreneur or a community runs the engine for a country, a region or a city, under its own law and with its own data, from a country pack whose every fact has a source.
  • One worldwide mesh. Hubs complement each other; none subordinates another. Only public projections federate by default. Deal data travels only between the nodes of the parties. Private data never leaves its node.
  • A civic engine, growing from construction. A resident's idea, a vote, a line in an open budget, an open tender, a build with field evidence, a confirmation by residents and inspectors, and a record of who delivered, explained and appealable. This ring is vision today, and this book says so wherever it appears: it is a direction of this book, not yet designed in the engine's handbook.
  • One agent per person. Every participant has one representative agent that works for them under mandates they can read, change and revoke; a company may have one standing representative with a named accountable person. There is no swarm to manage. Chat text never authorises anything.

Philosophy: evidence is the brick¶

The engine's central question is not "how much do we trust this company?" but "what is the smallest fact we can prove?". A delivery accepted by the person who received it. A certificate checked against the issuer by number. A quote frozen with a digest the moment it left the seller. A defect recorded, and later recorded as remedied. A photograph of a drain bed, dated and placed, taken before it was covered. Each of these is small enough to be checked by one person in one minute and large enough to mean something. That is a brick. Everything else in Trustwork is built from bricks like it, and nothing in Trustwork is allowed to look like a wall when it is only paint.

Three ideas follow, and they run through every chapter of this book.

Truth is labelled. A page built from a public register, a page a company has claimed and a page whose claim was verified look different, and always will; so do a resident's estimate, a professional's costing and a sealed quote. A reader should always be able to tell what kind of brick they are looking at. Unknown is shown as unknown.

Nothing is summed into a score. Reputation in Trustwork is explained, not totalled: which facts, how many, over what period, by which method version, with what excluded and why, and how to appeal. There is no public score until a method with a minimum sample has been released, and a profile without one says so. Chapter 15 shows why: public ratings inflate because nobody wants to harm the person they are rating, and a number that cannot harm cannot inform.

The wall remembers. Facts are appended, never overwritten. A correction is a new version with a visible note. An appeal is decided by a different person from the one who decided first. A project that was voted for and never built keeps its page, with the reason. A record that can forget on request is not a record; a record that cannot be corrected is not honest. Trustwork is built to do both: remember everything and correct in the open.

Values¶

These are the sentences we judge our work by. They are short because they are meant to be remembered, and each is a reason to refuse a feature that breaks it.

  1. People first. Relief, agency and confidence for the person come before efficiency for anyone else. One human, one lifelong identity; your profile and your work survive a change of employer.
  2. Reputation cannot be bought. No payment, sponsorship, plan or staff action changes a ranking or a record. A paid check buys the check, not its result.
  3. Private stays private. A buyer's budget, a seller's floor, a negotiation thread, a family's house plan and a resident's identity behind a ballot are visible to no one without participation rights, including our own staff, search, exports, other hubs and agents.
  4. Agents work for you, not on you. An agent acts only within structured mandates you can read, change and revoke. Money and commitments need a budget and your confirmation.
  5. No fakes. No invented users, badges, ratings, reviews, traffic or results. Demo data is synthetic and says so.
  6. Every language, every country. Everything a reader sees follows their language, including dates, currencies and demo content. Country differences are data with sources, never code.
  7. Free to learn, for everyone. The lessons a family and a builder need to build safely and to check a stage are free, in every language; the certificate is the record. What many homes teach is published, with consent, as a public good and never sold.
  8. Open and independent. The engine is free software. Every hub owns its data, its infrastructure and its identity. Federation connects hubs; it never subordinates them.
  9. Lawful everywhere, kindly. Sanctions and export controls apply to every deployment, with no exception for staff or for the project's owner. Where we must refuse, we refuse in the visitor's own language and with warm words.
  10. Neutral between parties, never neutral about evidence. We serve any lawful government, municipality or civic body and side with none, and we publish what the evidence shows whether it flatters or not.
  11. Honest about the future. Vision is told as vision and built is told as built, in different places. The one chapter of this book that speaks only of today is marked, and it is short.

Four lines have a sharper edge in the civic ring, and we state them here so that no later chapter can soften them. Never a score of residents by the state, and nothing that could become one: a resident is never the object of a record. Never a social-credit system, under that name or any other. Trustwork never holds money. And reputation cannot be bought, by a contractor, by a company, or by a government. Chapter 15 sets out how each line is enforced.

Who the book is for¶

For everyone who builds the engine, so that they share one picture of what it is for. For every hub owner and operator, who will be asked by a mayor, a banker, an association or a journalist what this thing is and why it can be trusted. For lenders, public bodies, contributors and their agents, who want to understand it deeply enough to build with it. Naming an organisation in this book records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

It is not a status report; chapter 18 says briefly what is built today. It is the book of the idea, of the system as it will be, and of the road there. Read it in order the first time; after that, each part stands on its own.

2A home that remembers

For most families the house is not one asset among several. It is the asset: the largest thing they will ever buy or build, and the thing they leave. It is built over years, lived in for generations, and it accumulates mistakes that nobody writes down, so each owner learns them again at their own cost. This chapter is the human centre of the book: a house record that continues after the build and passes with the home, learning from many homes that is free for everyone, and protection for the families who build with their own hands. The house record, the owner's adviser and the observatory are directions of this book, not yet designed in the engine's handbook.

Trustwork's part is practical. A house cannot remember by itself, a family cannot afford to learn every lesson alone, and nobody can insure or finance what has never been recorded. So the engine gives families the means to keep their house's memory, lessons that cost nothing to anyone who builds, the protection that today only buyers of professionally built homes enjoy, and, from many houses read together with consent, the statistics that let an insurer cover a house and a lender finance one.

The largest thing a family owns¶

The OECD, in a study of 2022, puts it in one line: housing is "households' largest lifetime investment and the majority of their wealth". Across OECD countries, owner-occupied housing is on average half of household wealth and more than sixty per cent of the middle class's. In the euro area, by the European Central Bank's household survey of 2021, 61.7 per cent of households own the home they live in, and its median value, 191,600 euros, is larger than the median household's entire assets. In the United States the Federal Reserve's survey of 2022 found the median homeowner worth 396,200 dollars and the median renter 10,400. Ownership is the line between two wealth worlds.

It is also what families leave. The British tax authority writes that estates under a million pounds "are likely to consist mainly of residential property and cash", and in a number of OECD countries most estates pay no inheritance tax at all, largely because of relief for close relatives and for the family home. The house passes, and the state steps aside to let it.

Where Trustwork is most needed it is harder. "Land and housing are the most important assets of the poor," the World Bank says, and the poor build them in stages, from savings and remittances, mostly without a drawing, a professional or a record. In Peru, by the Housing Ministry's own account, about seventy per cent of homes are self-built. The self-built house is savings, pension and security in one object, and it is the one large thing a family owns that nobody records.

What a house forgets¶

A house is built once and repaired for a century. The footing is poured in a season, the damp-proof course goes in on a morning, the roof is tied down or it is not; then the walls close and what was done well or badly disappears from sight. The leak appears three winters later, far from where the water enters. The crack opens over a decade, and nobody remembers that the ground was never tested. A house keeps no memory of its mistakes unless someone keeps it for it.

Yet the lessons are not mysteries. Buildings fail in the same ways everywhere, and mostly from water: in France, where claims under compulsory defect insurance have been counted since the 1980s, sixty-one per cent of insured defects in 2022 to 2024 were failures of water-tightness; in Japan, under a warranty scheme that ran from 1980 to 2008, water ingress was ninety per cent of all defects. After water comes structure. About eighty per cent of the deaths in the Kobe earthquake of 1995 were caused by collapsing houses, most of them built before the seismic code of 1981. The year a house was built largely decided whether it would fall on its family, and that year was written nowhere the family could read.

A house that remembers is a house that teaches.

One house, three generations¶

Told as the system will run it, in a hillside district of a Peruvian city. The people are roles, not persons; no figure is given because none is known.

One plot, one record, three generations
  1. The first year
    a young couple and a neighbourhood mason · a plot on the slope

    They launch the house on the hub, in Spanish. Their agent turns it into phases, trades, permits and budget ranges, and shows, before the first stage is paid, a short lesson on a footing on a slope. The mason takes the free course that goes with it; the certificate is the first the mason has ever held. An owner's adviser, a retired builder with a mandate from the couple, confirms the footing before it is covered. Three records, three authors, one stage.

  2. The twenty-fourth year
    the daughter · the same house, now hers

    The build file closed long ago; the house record went on, through a second storey and a damp stain under the stair recorded as a defect and then as a remedy. Her parents pass the house to her by deed, and the record passes with it, whole, with the mandates over it. She reads what her father never told her. When a licensed insurer covers the house, she grants it a time-limited read of the fields it needs. The insurer sees the record. It does not own it.

  3. The thirty-first year
    a buyer and the daughter · the deed

    The buyer reads thirty years of entries, each with the name of the person who confirmed it. The record changes hands at the deed, and the seller cannot edit the past. Because the family consented long ago, the next young couple on the slope read in their own language how a footing on this ground was done, and what went wrong when it was not.

The house record¶

Chapter 10 assembles the engine's record of a build into a file a licensed lender or insurer can read. But a build file ends at completion, and a house does not. The house record is what this chapter adds. The build file is its first chapter; then come the maintenance plan with its dated inspections, every repair and renovation with its quote and acceptance, the sale and the inheritance. A project becomes a house at completion, and the house keeps its record for as long as it stands.

Four places have already decided that a house should remember. Japan went furthest: a national council founded in 2010 sets the rules of its house history, a dwelling can receive a house ID that is inherited across owners, and by 2018 3.57 million dwellings had a history on file; a law in force since 2009 obliges the owners of certified long-life houses to keep maintenance records, and in the fiscal year 2024 it covered 39.3 per cent of new detached houses. The council's stated purpose is a move "from the age of building houses to the age of protecting, nurturing and handing them on". Flanders made its Woningpas free for every homeowner in 2018, one digital file per dwelling, more than three million of them by the operator's own count. France, by a law of 2021, requires a housing information record for every new home permitted from 2023, kept by the owner in an open format and handed to the buyer at the notarial deed. And the European Union, in the 2024 recast of its buildings directive, defined the digital building logbook in law for the first time.

One place shows the other side. England and Wales required sellers to supply a Home Information Pack from 2007 and suspended the duty in 2010, before repealing it, on research that more than ninety per cent of buyers wanted information about a home's condition and fewer than one in six trusted it when the seller provided it; experienced buyers preferred "an informal check by a tradesperson they already knew". A record is worth what its verification is worth. So every entry in a Trustwork house record shows who recorded it and who confirmed it, with the date and the digest of the evidence; an entry nobody confirmed is shown as the owner's word. A reader sees evidence levels, not a brochure, and no house score.

Learning from many homes¶

A house that remembers teaches its own family. Many houses that remember, read together with consent, teach a country. Every working defect observatory pairs a money event with a record event. France's compulsory decennial insurance produces claims, and since the 1980s the claims have fed a national database of defects by element and cause, used to plan prevention and to measure whether it worked. Denmark created a building defects fund for subsidised housing by law in 1986, financed by one per cent of construction cost, with inspections at one and five years and a legal duty to spread what it learns; by its own account, serious failures fell from more than a quarter of new buildings in the early 1990s to under eight per cent. And Alabama built a programme around a standard called FORTIFIED: an independent evaluator designates each house, a public register lists the designations, insurers must discount the wind premium for a designated home, as reported, and a state programme pays grants towards the work. In a study of more than forty thousand insured coastal properties after Hurricane Sally in 2020, the designated homes claimed between 55 and 74 per cent less often. A standard, a verified record of each house, a discount tied to the record and money at the moment of the work: every part of that mechanism is something a hub can hold.

So each hub, as chapter 10 describes, publishes aggregates from the projects whose families consented, with small cells suppressed and re-identification tested. This chapter adds the observatory: the same consented aggregates read over the life of the house and not only its build, so that a hub can see which roofs leak after ten years and which footings crack on which ground, and whether its own courses made a difference. For many of the countries in this book it could be the first defect statistics ever published for houses that families build themselves. Insurers can price what they can count; lenders can lend against what they can see; the next family builds knowing what the families before it learned. The family's own record is never the statistic.

Free, and good, for everyone¶

This chapter's promise has a word in it that costs money: free. The people who build most of the world's homes cannot pay for courses, and the evidence on what teaches them is clear. After Typhoon Haiyan in 2013 the government and the humanitarian shelter agencies in the Philippines agreed eight "Build Back Safer" messages for families and carpenters. The independent assessment of 2016 found that households had learned "by watching people they saw as the most skilled local carpenters"; that training for the wider community "was reported only to be effective when materials were distributed to participants in conjunction with the training"; and that, in one agency's words, "many can quote the key messages, price is the main barrier". One carpenter, well versed in the safer methods, said what every builder knows: "ultimately I build what people ask me to build." The owner gives the order, so the owner has to be the learner too. And people will learn in large numbers when the lesson is in their own language, practical and within their means: a Spanish-language course on building your own house safely, from Mexico's national university, had 159,540 learners enrolled as of November 2026.

So the rule, which chapter 14 carries further: the lessons a family and a builder need to build safely and to check a stage are free for everyone, in every engine language, and the certificate, checkable by number, is the record. A hub or a training body may offer other courses for a fee, as the learning design allows; the lessons that keep a house standing are never behind one. The lesson arrives where the evidence says it works: on the plan step itself, in the family's language, before the stage is paid, so that a family about to accept a footing is shown what to look at, and the mason about to pour it is offered the course and the certificate that follows. And the trusted local builder, the teacher people actually learn from, gains a record as a mentor whose students' certificates carry their name.

Protected¶

Where a family can buy protection, it rests on five pillars: a registered builder; a warranty or defect insurance that outlives the builder; inspected stages; protected deposits; and a body to take a dispute to. Japan learned what happens when one pillar is hollow. Its law of 2000 gave every new house a ten-year warranty for its structure and against water ingress; then a scandal of falsified structural calculations bankrupted a seller, and the owners "had to take out additional loans to buy a new unit on top of having already bought the one which was built based on the fake structure statement". Since 2009 every housing business in Japan must hold defect insurance or a deposit. A warranty was a promise; the law made it a funded one.

Now read the five pillars from the hillside. Every one of them presupposes a professional builder, a contract, a record of stages and an institution that can be found afterwards. Kenya's construction authority has registered contractors since 2011, and its regulations exempt "a skilled construction worker or construction site supervisor" from registration as a contractor, which is exactly the person who builds most Kenyan homes; such workers can be accredited, but the protections tied to a registered contractor do not reach the family that hires one. Denmark's compulsory building-damage insurance exempts housing that the consumers build themselves; Arizona's recovery fund pays the victims of licensed contractors only. The family that builds with a neighbourhood mason, pays cash by the week and keeps the plan in its head has none of the five pillars, in Nairobi and in Phoenix alike. This is not a judgement of the mason. It is a description of the record that was never made.

Trustwork cannot pass a warranty law and does not pretend to. What it gives is the part of protection that comes from a record, to the families who have nothing else. The plan carries the protection facts from the country pack at the step where the family chooses a builder: which register to check, what warranty applies, where a dispute goes; and where none exists, it says so plainly. Hidden works are accepted with dated, placed photographs before they are covered. An independent inspector and an owner's adviser, a person on the family's side who knows building, act as participants under a mandate from the family: they inspect stages, record defects and confirm acceptances, their reputation grows from evidence like anyone's, and they are paid by the family or by a programme, never by the builder. Money moves only through licensed providers the hub configures, against accepted evidence, and Trustwork never holds it. A dispute goes to the counterparty with the record in front of both, and then wherever the country's law sends it. And at the end there is a house record that exists, which is more than most families in the world have ever had.

The lines¶

The record belongs to the family: not to the hub, not to the builder, not to Contribuda, and not to any lender or insurer that has read it. It passes with the home by an explicit act at sale or inheritance, the seller cannot edit the past, and it can be exported in an open format so that nobody is locked in. Nothing in it is shared without the family's consent, and the hub's staff cannot read it except through the one door every private record has: a scoped, time-limited case grant approved by a second person, which the family can see. There is no house score and no family score. No resale premium is promised: no study known to us measures one. Lenders and insurers read with consent, for a stated period and a stated set of fields; they never own the record. Aggregates are published under an open licence and never sold. And no entry is ever marked verified without a named confirmer.

What stays human¶

Choosing the mason. Standing in the trench. Accepting a stage. Deciding what to share, and with whom. Teaching a daughter where the water comes in. Handing over the keys and the record. The agent drafts, reminds and compiles; it never approves, never accepts, never consents and never inherits. A house is a family's memory in brick. The engine only keeps the notes.

The record belongs to the family. The lessons belong to everyone.

3The missions we set ourselves

A mission says what the project is for, and why. There are seven. The first three belong to the construction industry, where the engine is designed to work first. The next three are the civic ring that grows out of it. The last is about the engine itself: that it runs anywhere and belongs to no one. For each we say why it is needed, what it looks like five years on, how we will know without ever watching an individual, and what stays human. The evidence for each lives in the chapter that treats it fully; here it is only named. The years follow the arc in Part VII; they are its shape, not dates.

1. Make every builder visible¶

Why. Most of the people who build the world have no record of what they can do. Informal work is 57.8 per cent of all employment worldwide, more than two billion people, by the ILO's count for 2024, and construction is one of the three sectors where it is most common. The skill is real and the certificate is missing. Chapter 14 shows what changes when that record is supplied.

Five years on. Every worker, professional and company on a hub has a lifelong profile that belongs to them and not to their employer, a portfolio backed by evidence and confirmations tied to real work, certificates checkable by number, and a passport that travels between hubs and across borders. In at least two countries, the hub is the place where an accredited body assesses an informal worker's prior learning and records it once. A young contractor in a regional city is found by the client who needs them, for reasons the matching explains.

How we will know. By counts, never by faces: certificates issued and checked, profiles that survived a change of employer, confirmations tied to real transactions, and, in consented research, whether participants feel more in control of their working life. We measure the record's reach, never a person.

What stays human. The assessment of a skill by an accredited assessor. The decision to publish a profile, by recorded, revocable consent. The confirmation of work by the counterparty who received it. Nobody is made visible against their will, and no agent confirms anything on anyone's behalf.

2. Make every build clearer and safer¶

Why. Where building is informal and inspection weak, buildings fall. Kenya's construction authority found 10,791 of 14,895 sampled buildings unsafe to occupy in a 2022 audit, as the press reported; Nigeria's count of collapses and the causes regulators name are in chapter 11, and every one of those causes is a missing record.

Five years on. Anyone who launches a project, a house on a lot in San Diego or a room added to a family home in Lima, has a real plan: phases, trades, permits and inspections, budget ranges, risks and the decisions ahead. Plan items become consultations with experts and tasks for trades, and hidden works are accepted stage by stage with dated, placed photographs before they are covered. Finished projects become shared knowledge with the owner's consent, so that a self-builder in Accra can read, in their own language, how someone else solved the same wall. Manufacturers' knowledge sits on the plan steps where it fits, never because anyone paid. When the build ends, its record continues as the family's house record (chapter 2), and the lesson for each step stays free (chapter 14).

How we will know. Plans approved and completed, consultations accepted, stage acceptances recorded with evidence, defects recorded and remedied, and each hub's open statistics on cost, completion time and defect rates by stage and building type, aggregated from consenting projects. Whether buildings fall less often is for the professional bodies that count collapses to say; we will give them better records.

What stays human. The plan is approved by its owner. The advice is given by a licensed professional under their own name. A stage is accepted by a person who was there. An inspector's act is an inspector's. The agent prepares, reminds, matches and reports; it never pours concrete and never signs for it.

3. Make every private build bankable and insurable¶

Why. Private construction has, almost nowhere, any tool for lending, because there is no standard; the diagnosis holds wherever one looks. The reason is the object, not the borrower: an unfinished house with no standard plan, no inspected stages and no record of its builder cannot be valued, lent against or insured. Chapter 10 makes the case in full.

Five years on. Every Trustwork build produces, as a by-product of the work being recorded, a standard, evidenced build file: the plan and its versions, permits, the named professionals, frozen quotes, inspection gates, field evidence, acceptances, changes and defects, and the contractor's record. Trustwork publishes the file's format as an open standard, so that many lenders and insurers can read one shape. With the borrower's consent, a licensed lender releases money stage by stage against accepted evidence and an independent inspector's act; an insurer prices from evidence rather than from guesswork. A family borrows and insures its own house as it rises.

How we will know. Build files completed to the standard; lenders and insurers that read it, counted as institutions, never as households; stage releases made on accepted evidence; and, over time, the defect and overrun statistics of financed builds against unfinanced ones. We will publish what did not work too.

What stays human, and what stays outside Trustwork. Trustwork never lends, never holds money and never decides who is creditworthy; licensed lenders, insurers and inspectors act under their own law, and Trustwork supplies the evidenced file, with the borrower's consent, and nothing else (chapter 10).

4. Let residents decide¶

Why. Participatory budgeting began in Porto Alegre in 1989 and has spread to thousands of cities. The projects residents choose are overwhelmingly built things, and its weakest link is always delivery: the vote is decided in a day, and the building takes years. Chapter 12 gives the evidence.

Five years on. Any hub can enable the civic ring under its country's law, with a consenting municipality or civic body. Ideas are co-designed and costed by a named professional before the ballot; any vote is held by a neutral keeper, the municipality or an electoral institute, one person one vote, with no link from ballot to person that any role, including ours, can follow; co-financing, the mark of initiative budgeting, is optional and capped, so that the poorest neighbourhoods are served first. Where the law does not allow it, or the civic space is closed, the construction hub runs alone, and we say so.

How we will know. Projects proposed, costed, voted, funded, contracted, built and confirmed, as a pipeline a resident can read; the share of winners accepted within the promised time; the share dropped, each with a stated reason; turnout as a share of all residents. We will never know who voted for what, and we build so that we cannot.

What stays human. The idea, the discussion, the vote, the council's budget, the inspector's act, the committee's confirmation. The engine carries the record from one human act to the next; it decides nothing and replaces no council, auditor or court.

5. Follow public money into concrete¶

Why. Governments publish more than they listen: the world's average budget transparency score has hovered around forty-five out of a hundred for a decade, and its average for public participation is seventeen, by the Open Budget Survey of 2025. And open data about money is not evidence about buildings. The Philippine flood-control scandal of 2025 showed it: every notice had been posted, and the works did not exist (chapter 11). Photographed evidence of public works exists, and India has kept it for a decade; what does not exist is a shared, portable record of it, joined to the budget line and the contract (chapter 13).

Five years on. Every public work in a hub's territory has a public-works record, from the budget line and the contract to the stages with field evidence, the inspector's act and residents' confirmations, with what remains unknown shown as unknown. Every institution that commissions works has a delivery record of what it promised against what it built, with the right of reply. A resident can see what their vote became.

How we will know. Public works with a complete record; resident confirmations compared against a professional measurement, because monitoring without a measurement and a consequence does little; responses by institutions to observations, and their time; and the gap, published, between what was promised and what stands.

What stays human. The inspector's measurement. The resident's observation, made safely. The institution's reply. The journalist's piece. We never rate an official as a person; people in public roles appear only as signatories of documents that are already public.

6. Raise the culture of building¶

Why. A society that understands its built environment and its budgets gets better buildings and keeps more of its money. "Culture of building" has a European definition and a published measure, governance first (chapter 14). Culture travels through people who are paid and respected for building well, not through posters.

Five years on. Every hub reads a magazine with named sources and accurate dates, in which a weekly piece follows one budget line into one building. Events are conferences, site visits and public hearings alike. Courses teach a mason the next trade, a committee member how to witness the acceptance of a hidden work, a journalist how to read a municipal budget, with certificates mapped, in at least one country, to a national qualifications framework.

How we will know. Courses completed and certificates checked; events held, as totals; magazine corrections, published; and, in consented research, whether readers say they understand their budget and their building better. We do not count what a person read.

What stays human. Teaching. Editing. Deciding what is worth a site visit. Writing the sentence that explains why the drain matters to the people who will never read a budget. Agents draft, summarise and remind; the by-line is a person's, and so is the correction.

7. Run anywhere, belong to no one¶

Why. An engine that helps the state and society cannot be owned by a company, captured by a government or dependent on one donor. Open civic projects live on diversified income and die on one funder: the Sunlight Foundation closed in 2020, Code for America's brigades lost their sponsor in 2023, the Open Collective Foundation dissolved in 2024, and USAID stopped implementing foreign assistance on 1 July 2025, taking much of the money that paid for open-government work. Decidim and OpenStreetMap survived by widening their base. Trustwork is already free software under the AGPL-3.0-only licence, and a careful reading of the Digital Public Goods Standard's nine indicators finds its gaps to be documentation and policy, not architecture.

Five years on. Hubs in ten or more countries, run by local operators under one charter that carries the invariants and the list of what we will never build; an operators' association independent of any one funder; Contribuda as maintainer on the terms of chapter 7; the engine recognised as a digital public good, or the gap list published; income to hubs from commissions, events, courses and services, never from ranking and never from data; and a public failure log.

How we will know. Independent operators; country packs maintained in their own country; an association budget published every year; a second implementation of the protocol; a charter every hub has signed.

What stays human. The decision to open a hub and to take legal responsibility for it. Counsel's review of each country's law. The association's vote. The maintainer's refusal to build what the charter forbids, even when a government asks.

The seven together¶

Read as one, the missions are a single sentence. Make every builder visible so that every build can be clearer and safer; make every build evidenced so that a bank and an insurer can trust it; let residents decide what to build with common money and follow that money into concrete with the same record; teach a society to read its buildings and its budgets; and keep the engine that does this open and owned by the people who run it. The civic ring is the same wall, one course higher.

What we promise is a record. What we hope for is a culture.

4Trust is built like a building

A wall is not bought. It is laid, one course at a time, each brick bedded in mortar on the course below, and anyone may walk up to it, look, tap it, and see whether it was done well. This is the central idea of Trustwork, and it shapes the engine, its mark and its civic ring: trust is built the way a building is built, from small accepted facts, and it can be inspected, repaired, and remembered.

Course by course¶

Every trade knows that a wall is only as good as its first course. In Trustwork the first course is evidence, the small accepted facts of the first chapter: each a brick, small enough to be checked by one person in a minute, hard enough to bear weight.

Acceptance is the mortar. A brick lying loose on the ground is a claim. It becomes part of the wall only when the counterparty, the person who received the work or the inspector who measured it, beds it in with a recorded decision. In the engine this is a state machine, not a metaphor: a claim is made, evidence is attached, and the claim is confirmed or disputed by someone with the standing to do so. A photograph is not acceptance. A model's summary is not acceptance. Only a person's recorded decision is, and it carries their name.

A reputation is the wall. It is nothing more than the courses laid so far, visible to anyone who cares to look: how many, over what period, of what kind, by which method they were counted, with what excluded and why. Nobody can buy a wall. You can only lay it. Nobody can lay it for you in a night. And a wall has three properties that a score does not. It can be inspected: every brick is still there to be looked at, with its evidence. It can be repaired: a bad course is not painted over but taken out in the open, with a note saying what was wrong and what replaced it, and the appeal is heard by someone other than the person who laid the judgement. And it remembers: a course laid ten years ago under a different employer is still yours, because the wall belongs to the person who built it.

A score is a photograph of a wall from far away; it tells you there is a wall and nothing about the bricks.

The mark, read¶

Trustwork's mark is three blue squares. It has no official meaning; what follows is one reading of it. Set on a two-by-two grid, the three squares are three courses already laid and one place still open. We read the laid squares as the three who build with trust today: the person who builds, the company that delivers, and the public that commissions with common money. We read the open square as the resident, the community, the next participant, the next hub, the next craft. It is not ours to fill. If the engine ever drew that fourth square itself, by scoring the resident or by deciding for the community, it would have stopped being what it is. The gap is the promise.

The loop of public trust¶

Lay the courses in a city instead of on a site and the wall becomes a loop. Public money becomes visible in the built environment, and every step from an idea to a finished public work is a brick that someone can accept or dispute. The loop below is the civic ring of the engine. It is a direction of this book, not yet designed in the engine's handbook; nothing in it is built; it is shown here because it is the idea the rest of the book serves.

Three things about the loop matter more than its shape. First, the evidence weighs as much as the vote: votes are fast, concrete is slow, and the programmes that hold put a verified act between the contractor and the money, as Makueni County in Kenya does by paying a contractor only after a committee of residents has approved the work. Second, confirmation needs a measurement to check against and a consequence attached, so the residents' confirmation sits beside an engineer's bill of quantities and an inspector's act, and it triggers something: a payment signal, a defect record. Chapter 12 gives the evidence for both. Third, the loop records institutions and companies, never residents. The ballot is unlinkable to the person by any role, including ours.

One year, step by step¶

This chapter follows one year in one municipality, because a year is the rhythm of a public work. The place is a mid-sized municipality in the Dominican Republic, and the choice is deliberate. Dominican law has required every municipality to run a participatory budget since 2007, with a works committee for each project, and every tender is published as open data; chapter 20 explains why the arc places the first full loop there.

Everything that follows is told as the system will run it. No municipality has agreed to anything; the people are roles, not persons; no figure is given because none is known. The vote is the municipality's, held under its law; the hub supports the record around it.

A barrio, a drain, a budget year
  1. January
    a resident · a barrio on the edge of town

    The street floods every rainy season where the storm drain was never finished. The resident has photographed it from the doorstep for years. This time their agent, under a mandate they gave it, knows from the country pack that the participatory-budget calendar opens this month. It drafts the idea in their words, attaches the dated photographs, and asks whether to submit. They change a sentence, and submit.

  2. February
    neighbours and the planning office · the community assembly

    The assembly meets in the school, with the municipality's planners present. The drain and the broken culvert under the same street are merged into one idea; the hub's page records each version and who proposed it, from the assembly's own minutes. Nobody votes yet. Nobody is scored.

  3. March
    a civil engineer · registered with the national college, paid by the municipality

    The idea becomes a consultation. The engineer walks the street, measures, and returns a note: what the drain needs, a cost range, and what could not be seen without opening the ground. It is labelled as a professional estimate with the engineer's name and method, and the engineer's record will later show how the real price landed against the range.

  4. April
    the section assembly and the open council · the vote

    The costed ideas are voted on under the municipality's own rules, one person one vote. The hub runs no ballot and holds no roll; it shows the result as a sourced page from the municipality, with the counts and the method, and nothing about who voted. The drain wins.

  5. May
    the council and the barrio · the budget line

    The approved plan enters the municipal budget, as the law requires, and the drain's page links to the budget line. The barrio elects its community works committee: five neighbours, shown on the page by role, who will watch that the work follows the plan and the money.

  6. June
    the procurement office · the open tender

    The tender is published through the national procurement office and imported as a sourced page on the drain's record. Four contractors answer. The winning bid becomes an agreement both sides can prove identical.

  7. July
    the contractor · a small firm from the next town

    The firm's page was sourced from procurement data months ago; now its owner claims it, with evidence. The company's agent, under a standing mandate with a named accountable person, lays out the stages from the agreement and the engineer's note, each with the evidence it will need before acceptance.

  8. August
    the foreman and two committee members · the drain bed, before it is covered

    Before the pipe is laid, the foreman records the bed with dated, placed photographs against the drawing, and two committee members stand in the trench and attach their own observation. The municipality's inspector measures the next morning and signs the act. Three records, three authors, one stage. Then the concrete covers it, and the record is all that remains of what was underneath.

  9. September
    the inspector and the committee · a defect

    The culvert's headwall is not to the drawing. The inspector records a defect, the committee agrees, and the firm's owner confirms a remedy under their own name. Three weeks later the remedy is accepted. The defect stays on the record, and so does its remedy. Nothing is painted over.

  10. October
    the works committee · the confirmation that releases money

    The surface is finished before the rains. The inspector's final act, the contractor's completion statement and the committee's confirmation, checked against the engineer's bill of quantities, sit as three records on one page. The hub sends one signal to the municipality's finance system that the stage is accepted, and the municipality pays through its own channels. Trustwork has held no money at any point.

  11. November
    the hub's editorial office · lessons

    The magazine follows the budget line into the drain, every source named and the defect included. The works committee becomes the maintenance committee, as the law provides, and its members take a short course on witnessing hidden works. The contractor's record shows one public work, on time, one defect remedied; the municipality's delivery record shows one project promised, budgeted, built and accepted, with dates.

  12. December
    the resident · the same doorstep

    It rains, and the street does not flood. The resident photographs it anyway and attaches it to the drain's record as a resident observation. Their agent mentions that the calendar opens again in January, and that the stair to the upper street has been on their list for years.

Each month of that year rests on a design choice: the public project, not the vote, is the unit; costing is a paid consultation; tenders are sealed and agreements frozen; every stage carries three records by separate authors; Trustwork sends a payment signal and never moves money; records are kept for companies and institutions, never for residents; the law comes from the country pack; and the person who looks is protected. Chapters 12, 13 and 15 set out each choice.

The loop is not electronic voting, not a government's scoring of its residents, and not a replacement for councils, auditors, inspectors or courts. Chapter 15 sets out these lines.

Nobody can buy a wall. You can only lay it.

5The future we are building for

Four views shape every decision in Trustwork: who will build the homes the next decades need and how; how people and their agents will work together in an industry and a city; what the state and society will expect of a record like ours, and what they must never be allowed to do with it; and what the engine must become in the next five years so that it is ready when the world arrives there.

Who will build the next homes¶

The homes the world needs in the coming decades will mostly be built by the people who live in them, in cities that are growing faster than their plans. We do not claim a global count; the sourced figures are enough. In Peru about seventy per cent of homes are self-built, by the Housing Ministry's own account. In Nairobi, about sixty per cent of residents are reported to live in informal settlements, and the city builds a fraction of the homes it needs each year. The same picture holds in Mexico, Colombia, Manila, Accra, Lagos and Kigali; chapter 20 gives the figures country by country.

This is the world Trustwork's plan-to-people was designed for. A family that will build its own house incrementally, as money arrives, does not need a marketplace; it needs a plan it can understand, advice from a professional it can afford for an hour, a mason whose record it can check, and a way to prove at each stage that the work was done, so that one day a lender or an insurer will take the risk alongside it. The programmes that changed self-building at scale had this shape, and none of them left a portable record that the family owned. The engine's job is to make that record a by-product of building well, in the family's own language, on a phone.

The other side of the same future is safety. Kenya's engineers are asking for a national building register so that anyone can check who designed and who approved a building. Florida answered a collapse that killed ninety-eight people with inspections at fixed ages and records posted online. The Dominican Republic discovered in April 2025 that it had no inspection code in law. Each asks for the same object: a public page per building with who designed, who built, who approved, who inspected and when. That page is Trustwork's sourced page by another name, and the engine can offer it to every country long before any vote is involved.

People and agents¶

The routine of an industry is moving to machines: finding, matching, comparing, scheduling, drafting, reminding, summarising, reconciling. What remains with people, and matters more than before, is judgement and responsibility: the decision to build, the acceptance of a stage, the professional's signature, the licensed act, the movement of money, the word given to a third party. Trustwork's model of the two is simple and strict. Each person has one representative agent, acting only under mandates they can read, change and revoke, and a company may have one standing representative with a named accountable person (chapter 8). It never commits on chat text; money and commitments need a confirmed mandate, a budget and the person's own confirmation.

There is no swarm, no panel of bots, no agent that acts for nobody. The future we expect is not agents replacing people in construction; concrete does not pour itself and a stage does not accept itself. It is an industry in which the paperwork that used to make trust expensive is done by agents under mandate, and the acts that make trust real are done by people under their own name.

In the civic ring the line is the same and sharper. A resident's agent can watch a public-works page, prepare an observation from the resident's own photographs, draft a question to the procuring office under the resident's name, and remind them of a hearing. A municipality's standing representative can answer observations. Nothing is sent without a person, and no agent votes, confirms, inspects or decides. The world is moving in the same direction where it moves well: cities that let a machine pre-check a building permit keep the decision with the reviewer, and Seattle's evaluation of 2026 found the pre-check caught most of what reviewers caught while reviewers kept the last word; Singapore announced in 2026 a registry of the AI agents its public service runs, so that each is known and accountable. People's expectations are plain and fair: in the OECD's trust survey of 2026, more than four in ten thought AI could make government serve them better, and fewer believed governments would keep it safe. An engine that puts agents in public processes must show its safeguards before it shows its savings.

What stays human

Decisions. Acceptance. Licensed acts. Money. The assessment of a skill. The inspector's measurement. The resident's vote. The council's budget. The editor's by-line. The appeal, decided by a different person. The frontier of what an agent can prepare moves every year; the list of what only a person can do in Trustwork does not.

The state and society¶

Trust in national government is a minority position across the OECD, thirty-nine per cent in 2024 and forty in 2026, and the largest gap in the data is between people who feel they have a say and people who do not: sixty-nine per cent of the first trust their government, twenty-two per cent of the second, in the 2024 round. A loop that closes, from a resident's idea to a finished work the resident can confirm, is a "have a say" machine, and it is the one thing in Trustwork that touches trust in institutions directly.

The rails are being laid. The United Nations' Global Digital Compact of September 2024 names open-source software, data and standards as digital public goods and commits to investing in them and in digital public infrastructure by 2030. Dozens of countries are building shared identity, payment and data-exchange layers; open contracting has a standard in which nine of the fourteen countries of this book and Ukraine already publish nationally, and one more through a city; participation platforms are free software recognised as digital public goods; a signed credential is a web standard. Trustwork does not build identity or payments; it plugs into them through adapters, and it intends to stand beside those platforms as a recognised digital public good itself.

The risks are as real as the rails, and they decide where the engine may go. Surveillance. A state that can read a resident's observations, votes and complaints as a profile has a social-credit system, whatever it calls it. Residents are never the object of a record; the ballot is unlinkable; a civic role carries no reputation. Capture. Open government can be lost as fast as it is gained: the United States, which co-founded the Open Government Partnership, withdrew from it by a letter of 15 January 2026, and Georgia, ranked first in the world for budget transparency in 2023, was permanently suspended from the same partnership on 13 April 2026 over restrictions on civic space, including its laws on foreign influence. So civic-space standing is a gate, not a score: a hub enables the civic ring only where a lawful operator can work and where the people who use the record are safe, and the gate is re-checked every year. Where it is shut, the construction hub runs alone, and chapter 20 says so. Exclusion. A digital-only vote measures the connected; a co-financing rule that rewards the neighbourhoods that can pay excludes the ones that cannot; a monitoring design that exposes the monitor gets people hurt. So paper and assisted channels count alike, co-financing is optional and capped, the poorest areas are served first, and the person who looks is protected by design.

Neutrality follows from all of this: Trustwork serves evidence, not parties, and chapter 15 says what that requires.

The five-year horizon¶

Trustwork's arc runs five years, and chapter 19 tells it year by year. The order is the point: construction first; the civic ring only with a consenting municipality and a lawful operator; standards before scale; an operators' association before the fourth operator; self-funding before the tenth country. Through all five years, still no score of residents, ever.

Agents will do the paperwork of trust. People will keep its acts.

6Culture and the way we work

Trustwork is built in the open by a small group of people and a larger group of agents, maintained by Contribuda, and meant to be run by people we have not met yet in countries we have only studied. The culture that came out of that is plain: written, sourced, averse to pretence, and kind at the door. This chapter is how we are, not how we wish to be.

In the open, decision by decision¶

The engine is free software under the AGPL-3.0-only licence. It is developed in one public repository: every piece of work is a published work package that names the chapter to read, the paths it owns and how it will be accepted; a contributor claims it in the open, works in an isolated branch, and hands back evidence. Every product decision is a numbered, dated record: the context, the decision in numbered points and the consequences. A decision that is proposed stays marked proposed until it is accepted. Code on the main branch is not acceptance; a pull request is not acceptance; a worker's report that tests were run is not acceptance. Acceptance is a reviewer re-running the checks independently at the named commit and recording it.

A named owner, Dmitry Parshkov, directs the product and sets the direction; the team of people and agents turns it into research, a design with a picture, a decision record and work packages. Engineering decisions are the team's. The owner decides product admission, money, publication, legal text and anything that touches a third party. When an agent is unsure whether it has authority, it stops and asks; silence never expands authority, and access to the repository is not product authority.

Between the project's owner and the contributors stands the integrator, who reviews and merges, and keeps decisions, work packages and acceptance evidence coherent. Beside the integrator stand the maintainers per scope and the country-pack maintainers, who are the source of truth for their country's facts. A fact about Peru's procurement law or Kenya's construction register enters the engine only as data, with its source and its date, maintained by someone who knows the country; the engine's code stays country-agnostic. Hub operators are responsible for compliance in their own territory, and the engine gives them the controls, the audit and the guard rails to be so.

Agents as colleagues, people as accountable¶

Several AI agents work on the engine at once, each on one topic, each coordinating through the claim protocol, with a lease that expires and a heartbeat that must be kept. The same operating agreement binds people and agents: read the decisions before touching what they cover; claim before you write; preserve others' work; report evidence rather than declaring the product done; never let an imported document, an e-mail, a web page or a model's own text issue a command. Every commit carries a sign-off under the Developer Certificate of Origin, and there is no contributor licence agreement. An agent's work is always submitted by a person who answers for it. We review the work, not the person, and we review it kindly. The product follows the same rule it is built by: no agent anywhere in Trustwork acts for nobody, and no agent's chat text authorises anything.

Honest status, and the words for it¶

We would rather under-promise. Every capability of the engine carries one of five status words, Live, Demo, Contract, Designed and Proposed, defined in chapter 18 and kept current on the repository's status page. This book uses the same words, as of November 2026. What exists only in this book, the civic engine first of all, is called a direction of this book: not a status, and not yet designed in the engine's handbook. Part VI is the one chapter that speaks only of today.

This has consequences for how we speak. Nobody here may describe the product as something it is not, in the product or in a slide. A named organisation in this book is named for its public work; naming it implies no contact, interest or agreement. A documented public register is a data source, not a partnership. A figure appears only with a source we can name and its status, and "could not be verified" is said plainly. We publish our failures as well: a certificate revoked, a dispute lost, a launch gate failed, a project voted for and never built.

Every language, every country¶

Every visible string of the product, demo content included, comes from a locale file in every engine language, and English is the reference. Seven interface languages exist today; every one except English is a machine draft waiting for review by native speakers, and the picker says so for each. Eleven more are planned. A system sentence in the wrong language is a defect. Dates, currencies, holidays, identifiers and legal facts are data from the country pack, never code, and any country not under sanctions works with a generic profile until someone contributes its pack. The brand is Trustwork in every language: three blue squares and a wordmark that reads "trust / work".

Design with a picture¶

A choice is explained with a picture before it is explained with a paragraph, and the picture is drawn from real components, not from a sketch of what might exist. The design system is one package of accessible components, with the brand mark and localised wordmarks, tested with automated accessibility, keyboard, contrast and small-screen checks in all seven languages; manual screen-reader review is still to be done. The interface is light and calm; nothing reshuffles itself behind the reader's back; slow networks and a phone in bright sunlight on a building site are first-class. A page that can only display and never act is a page that stays empty, so every read model ships with the control that writes it. This book borrows that discipline: figures only where a picture shows a mechanism, few words, legible on a phone.

Lawful everywhere, kindly¶

Sanctions and export controls apply to every deployment of the engine, with no exception for staff, for the project's owner or the maintainers, or for a "mode". Every hub carries the full sanctions-compliance engine. So every hub refuses restricted places at every door, screens people and companies against current lists before any commitment, holds money-related acts until both parties are clear, and never offers a restricted place as a territory. The restricted places are listed in the engine's data and in its notice, and they are never hosts or markets in this book. Where we must refuse a person because of where they are, the refusal page speaks warmly and plainly, in their own language.

Nothing in the engine is legal advice; every legal point is a question for counsel in each country, and the hub operator's counsel reviews each legal document version before it takes effect.

What we refuse¶

Some features are refused in advance, so that no partner, operator or government need ask twice: pay-to-rank, staff controls over scores, employee surveillance, a "log in as user" switch, a generic inbox, a control panel for a swarm of bots, bulk messages to private persons who never agreed to hear from us, and fake social proof; and in the civic ring, any score of residents. Every hub starts with no system-initiated outreach at all, and may go further only under a policy its counsel has approved, activated with a second person, to organisation and role addresses. Chapter 15 sets out the lines behind them. The product lines are already in the specification and its tests, and all of them belong in the charter this book proposes for every hub.

We would rather be believed slowly than admired quickly.

7Open and free, on purpose

The engine is free software, and it is free of charge: no licence fee and no implementation fee, ever, from Contribuda to anyone. This is not a phase before a price list. It is a decision about what kind of thing Trustwork is and whom it serves. This chapter says why we are open, why we are free, who earns what in a hub, what Contribuda does and takes, and what we will never take.

Why open¶

A record of who built what, how well and for whom is only worth keeping if the people in it can check the hand that keeps it. That is the first reason. Trustwork asks a mason to put their working life into it, a company to let its deliveries be confirmed by its customers, and, in the civic ring, a town to let its public works be seen stage by stage. None of them should have to take our word for how the engine freezes a quote, computes a reputation or refuses a staff member access to a private budget. They can read the code, and so can an auditor, a journalist, a ministry's security team or a rival. Trust that cannot be inspected is just a brand.

The second reason is that nobody should be locked in. A hub operator who chooses Trustwork for their country must be able to leave with their data, run the engine unbranded, change it, and hire anyone they like to do so. The licence guarantees all of that. A record that a vendor's business decision can switch off is not a record at all.

The third reason is that the public bodies we hope to serve are themselves moving this way, and saying why. The European Union's Interoperable Europe Act, in force since April 2024, requires public bodies choosing interoperability solutions to prioritise those without restrictive licensing terms, such as open source, where they are equivalent in functionality, total cost, user-centricity, cybersecurity or other objective criteria. It also requires them to share, on request, the solutions behind cross-border public services, with their documentation and, where available, their source code. India made open source the preferred option for government software in 2015, to be compared on strategic control and lifetime cost among other things. France's law of 2016 made the source code of software used by public bodies a communicable administrative document. Brazil has treated free software as public policy since 2003. Germany founded a centre for digital sovereignty in 2022; Schleswig-Holstein decided in 2024 to move about thirty thousand workstations off proprietary office software; Denmark's digital ministry and the city of Lyon decided in 2025 to do the same, as reported, and each used the same word: sovereignty. The European Commission's 2021 study of open source named three benefits for the public sector: lower total cost of ownership, less dependence on single vendors, more digital autonomy. It gave no saving figure, and neither do we. A public body that puts its open budget, its tenders and its residents' votes into an engine wants to own the engine's fate. Open is how it can.

The fourth reason is time. A hub should be able to outlive us. The open civic platforms that survived their founding city or their first sponsor did so because the code was free and someone else could pick it up: a Dutch foundation keeps the Madrid platform maintained after the city's politics changed; an institute founded by the Estonian and Finnish governments develops the core of Estonia's data exchange layer, open source since 2016. If Contribuda vanished tomorrow, every hub would keep running, every record would stay readable, and anyone could continue the work.

The fifth reason is that the best knowledge about Peru's procurement law or a Kenyan county's construction register is not in our heads. Country packs, adapters, translations and compliance fixes come from people who know their country, and only if the engine is theirs to improve. The licence we chose, the AGPL, adds one rule to this: whoever runs a modified engine as a service must publish the modifications. A hub that improves the engine improves it for every hub. That rule is why the country packs flow back rather than fork away.

Nobody has to trust us. They can read the code and check the record.

Why free of charge¶

Open and free of charge are different decisions. Much open software comes with an enterprise edition, a hosting fee or a mandatory implementation contract. Trustwork has none, for three reasons.

Public tenders. When a municipality or a ministry buys a platform, the implementation budget is often a scored criterion, and a licence fee is a line that recurs every year for as long as the system lives. An engine with no licence fee and no implementation fee from its maintainer changes what that line can be. The budget becomes the operator's own costs, spent in the country: servers, people who speak the language, counsel who know the law, the adaptation of templates and packs. Nothing leaves for a vendor abroad, and nothing recurs for the right to keep using what was already paid for. This is how such tenders are usually scored, an observation and not a measured saving; it is also the plain logic of the EU rule above, where total cost is one of the criteria an open solution is compared on. What a public body procures, then, is hosting, an operator's services and adaptation, from any bidder it chooses; the engine itself is never on the invoice.

The places that need it most cannot pay a vendor. This book goes where informal building, weak transparency and stalled public works are worst. A county in Kenya, a province in Peru, a town in Ghana may have a procurement officer, an association of contractors and a university, and no budget at all for a software licence from another continent. If the engine carried a price, it would end up only where it is least needed.

A public good. The United Nations Secretary-General's 2020 roadmap for digital cooperation called for a concerted global effort to invest in digital public goods: open software, open data, open standards. The Global Digital Compact of 2024 commits governments to investing in them by 2030. The Digital Public Goods Standard, with its nine indicators from open licensing to doing no harm by design, is the bar such software is measured against, and chapter 3 says where Trustwork stands on it. The campaign that put the idea in a sentence has been signed, by its own count, by more than two hundred organisations: if it is public money, it should be public code. A record of public trust that was itself for sale would be a contradiction in terms.

Who earns what¶

A free licence is not free to run. The platforms we admire teach this. The foundation that keeps the Madrid platform alive has no developers of its own and, by the European Commission's own case study, struggles financially; Munich pays it a yearly contribution; Scotland's local authorities pooled hosting for thirty-two councils to lower the barrier, and still lack the capacity to customise. Barcelona's platform, by 2022, was paying two workers from two grants. Hosting, people, adaptation and support cost money every year, whoever wrote the code. The question is not whether a hub costs something. It is who pays, and who keeps what it earns.

The hub belongs to its owner in the country it serves, and so does its income. That owner is a person, a company or the state on the hub's side: an association, a chamber, a publisher, a university, an entrepreneur, a cooperative, a municipality or a ministry. The owner sets the hub's prices as hub settings (chapter 8): commissions on consultations and tasks, marketplace fees on events and courses, capacity subscriptions, agent work beyond the free floor, verification at cost. Every price is a published version, the same for everyone in its category, activated by a second person, shown to both sides before acceptance, and never an influence on ranking, matching, recommendations or reputation. Reading, a profile, publishing and answering a request for quotes stay free for every participant on every hub.

Everything a hub earns stays with the hub; what is earned inside a country benefits that country. Contribuda takes no share of a hub's commissions, no share of a domestic deal, no fee per participant, no cut of an event ticket or a course. The income a hub makes from the consultations of its estimators, the courses of its training centres and the events of its association is what pays for its servers, its editor-in-chief, its compliance officer, its translations and its counsel. A hub that earns well can hire; one that earns little can run lean with its agent team; neither sends anything to the maintainer. This is the sustainability model, and it is the one found behind every open civic platform that lasted: earned income from the people who benefit is the only income that grows with use.

It also keeps the maintainer honest. A company that took a share of every hub's commissions would have a reason to want commissions high and the free floor low. We have no such reason, and no hub owner need suspect us of one.

What Contribuda does, and what it takes part in¶

Contribuda creates and maintains the engine and gives it away under the licence. It writes and reviews the decision records, the handbook and the work packages; it keeps the mesh protocol, the design system and the country-pack tooling coherent. It is the decided operator of the San Diego hub, the reference hub where every new method is shown first; today that hub is a demo on synthetic data, not a deployment, and its ownership is to pass to a local operator through an open competition (chapter 23). What an operator needs to launch and run a hub comes with the engine, free like the engine: the launch playbook, the console and the operating company of agents. Contribuda is designed to run the public hub directory, the map and list of every listed hub in the world, which any operator may also run for a region and which no hub needs in order to peer (Proposed; to be confirmed). Nobody needs Contribuda to run a hub. A hub owner who chooses Contribuda's own platform as a tool, or wants its help, becomes a client of Contribuda on Contribuda's commercial terms and rules, exactly as if the engine had been written by someone else: a separate, optional service, never a fee for the engine or its implementation, and one the hub owner may leave at any moment (chapter 23).

What Contribuda does take part in is the international layer. When a participant on a hub in one country does business with a participant on a hub in another, when a payment for that deal moves through licensed providers across a border, and when a person's or a company's reputation is presented and verified across the mesh rather than at home, Contribuda is part of it. How, exactly, is to be decided. What is already fixed are the rules any design must keep. Whatever Contribuda's part is, it is disclosed to both parties before acceptance, like every other fee in the engine. It never changes ranking, matching or reputation. It never touches a domestic deal: two parties on hubs in the same country owe nothing to anyone but their own hub, if that. Trustwork still never holds money; whatever is charged moves through licensed providers like everything else. And both parties are screened against sanctions lists on both sides before the first commercial message, as the accepted cross-border rules already require.

The accepted design of cross-border commerce today says that each hub charges only its own participant, with both hubs' fee versions disclosed and recorded at acceptance, and that any revenue sharing between operators lies outside the protocol and must appear on both price pages. The commitment to an international layer adds a third party to that picture, and the two are to be reconciled. Whether Contribuda's international part is a disclosed fee version of its own, a share agreed with operators on their price pages, or something else, is to be decided by Dmitry Parshkov, as the project's owner, in a decision record, with counsel, and that record will reconcile it with the exchange layer. Until then this chapter promises the constraints and not the mechanism. What the San Diego hub charges before its ownership passes to a local operator, if anything, is likewise to be decided (chapter 23). We would rather name the remaining gaps than paper over them.

What we never take¶

So that no operator, partner or public body need ask twice, the list is short and it is final. Contribuda takes no licence fee for the engine, from anyone, ever. It takes no implementation fee: the launch playbook and tools come with the free engine, and a hub that wants nothing more owes nothing. It takes no share of domestic commissions, domestic deals or any other income a hub earns inside its country. It takes no payment for ranking, placement, matching or verification results, because reputation cannot be bought and that rule binds us first. And it takes no data: public facts travel in the mesh with attribution; profiles are never exported in bulk or used to train models; private data never leaves its node, including to us. If a sentence anywhere in Trustwork contradicts this list, the sentence is the error.

The idea as a whole¶

Open, so that trust can be checked and nobody is locked in. Free of charge, so that a public tender can be won on the operator's own costs and a hub can open where no vendor would go. Owned and monetised in the country, so that the people who run the hub keep what pays for it. The maintainer present only where a deal, a payment or a reputation crosses a border, in a form to be decided, under rules everyone can read before they agree. Mission seven called this running anywhere and belonging to no one; this is the money behind the sentence.

The engine is a gift. What a hub earns at home stays at home.

Part III · The engine

8A gathering point for the people who build

Before anyone can build a reputation, there has to be a place where the evidence lands and where people come back to read it. Trustwork is that place for the construction industry: a hub people return to because it is useful, where you read, meet, learn, are found, find partners, do business, build a reputation from what actually happened, and delegate the routine to one agent that works for you. This chapter describes the engine as it will be, in one piece, without status badges; chapter 18 says what exists.

Why a gathering point comes first¶

A record of trust is only as good as the number of people who write to it and read from it. Every industry has tried the shortcut: a register, a directory, a rating site. A register says who is licensed; nobody visits it until something has gone wrong. A directory lists companies; the companies are listed because they paid, and the clients stop believing it. A rating site collects stars; the stars inflate until they say nothing, and chapter 15 explains why. None of these is a place anyone wants to be. They are files, and a file that nobody opens cannot carry trust.

Construction has the opposite problem too: it has gathering points, but they do not keep records. The trade fair, the association dinner, the manufacturer's training day, the site visit, the phone call to a foreman who worked with that subcontractor once. Trust travels through these, slowly and only between people who already know each other. The knowledge they produce evaporates. A good young contractor in a regional city can stay invisible for years to the client who needs exactly them, and a client who has been let down once has no way to warn the next one except gossip.

So the engine starts from usefulness, not from the record. It gives the industry the things it already goes looking for every week, in one place, in its own language, and lets the record grow out of them. Every article read, course finished, event attended, quote sent, delivery accepted and defect remedied is a small, dated, sourced fact. Evidence is the brick. The gathering point is where the bricks are laid.

A file nobody opens cannot carry trust.

Eight verbs¶

The engine is described best by what a person does on it. There are eight verbs, and each of them has to be worth coming back for on its own.

Read. A hub publishes topical news with named sources and accurate dates, practical articles from practitioners, and a curated weekly magazine with a numbered issue and an editor's letter, read on a phone or downloaded as a laid-out PDF. A released issue never changes; a correction is a new edition with a visible note. No issue claims readers it has not measured, and imported items keep the licence they came with.

Meet. The industry meets in person: conferences, exhibitions, site visits, a supplier's open day, a training for installers, an evening meetup of young engineers. A hub lists the events of its territory and lets anyone its policy allows host their own, with tickets whose price is frozen once sold, check-in that works offline, materials published afterwards, and a certificate of attendance when the organiser issues one. Attendees who opt in can request meetings in slots the organiser defines; nobody sees who declined them. Sponsors are labelled and never affect a listing's position, and events are deliberately not rated.

Learn. Courses are short, practical and tied to real work: hidden-works acceptance on a ventilated facade, reading an insulation test report, winter concreting under the local code. A course has lessons, practice tasks and an assessment with a pass threshold. A certificate is a small public record that anyone can check by its number: the course and version, the date, the issuer, and its status, valid, expired or revoked with a reason. It says that this person completed this course and passed its assessment; it is not a state qualification or a licence, and a hub shows an accreditation only when it has recorded a source for one. Learning progress is private; there is no employer view of it.

Be found. Every person has a profile that belongs to them for life and survives a change of employer. Every company has a page with its people, projects, catalogue and evidence-backed reputation. Pages come in three states that always look different: a sourced page, created from an open source and labelled as such; a claimed page, which someone at the company has taken over; and a verified page, whose claim the hub checked against a register with a recorded method and date. A sourced page carries no follower counts, no response times, no ratings, nothing that imitates activity. Missing data is shown as unknown, never as failure.

Find partners. Matching explains why a suggested partner fits: the licence category, the region, the trade, the capacity, the evidence on file. Positions are never sold. An introduction is a request the other side accepts or declines in private, within a quota that stops anyone from spraying the directory. Open needs from real projects are published to those who opted in to that category and region, at the same moment for everyone.

Do business. A vague need becomes a clear request for quotes. Every sent version of a request and of a quote is immutable and carries a digest, so neither side can later dispute what was offered. A buyer may seal the round so that no quote is readable until the deadline, which protects suppliers from bid shopping; the seal cannot be lifted early and a late bid cannot undercut opened ones. An agreement binds exact versions, and both sides can prove their copies identical. Then execution is recorded: deliveries, work records, staged acceptance with photographs and a short act for each hidden stage, defects and their remediation, and the obligations each side still owes. The engine never holds anyone's money; payments move only through licensed providers the hub configures, and commissions are hub settings published as versions.

Build reputation. Reputation is a toolkit each participant uses, not a verdict the platform passes. Every snapshot names its method version and sample sizes, shows what was excluded and why, and says how to appeal. There is no public score until a method with minimum samples has been released, and nothing about it is for sale. Chapter 15 has the whole argument.

Delegate. Each person has one agent, their representative in the industry, labelled as an agent in every message and acting only under mandates they can read, change and revoke. The section below says how.

From plan to people¶

The flagship journey ties the verbs together. Anyone launches a project, for example a house on a lot outside a city. Their agent turns the idea into a plan: phases, tasks, the expertise and trades each step needs, materials, permits and inspections, budget ranges, a timeline, risks, and the decisions ahead. The owner approves a plan version. Plan items then become requests to people: a consultation with an expert (an advice session, a review of drawings or quotes, a site visit) and scoped tasks for contractors and trades. The agents of both sides do the routine work, matching, quoting, scheduling, negotiating within their mandates, tracking progress and reporting, while people decide. Each accepted task feeds the execution record, and the record feeds reputation.

When the project is done, and only with the owner's consent, it becomes shared knowledge: a diary, lessons learned, checklists, answers to the questions the next owner will ask. Experts earn reputation and income from the consultations they give. Budgets, addresses and negotiations stay private unless the owner publishes a redacted version. This is how an industry that keeps its knowledge in foremen's heads begins to keep it in a place a stranger can read.

Knowledge on every step¶

Most of what a builder needs to know at a given step already exists: in a manufacturer's installation guide, a supplier's technical article, a training course, a standard. The hub's editorial office builds sourced pages for manufacturers and suppliers from their public sites, within each source's licence, and indexes their catalogues, guides, training and events. For each step of a plan the system recommends the materials, articles, courses, events and consultants that fit, and explains why. Manufacturers appear as knowledge partners, on the steps where they help, because they fit, never because they paid. If a hub switches on sponsored content at all, it is labelled and kept out of ranking. The programmes that changed self-building in the developing world worked because a manufacturer's knowledge reached a family as advice from a person they trusted; chapter 14 tells that story. This is the open version of the same idea.

One agent, one registry, every door¶

Everything a person can do in the interface, their agent can do for them, and the reverse: full two-way parity. All modules register their operations in one capability registry that the agent runtime, the interface and a person's own AI chat connected over the Model Context Protocol (MCP) all use. A parity report in the build shows any gap. So you can connect your own Claude or ChatGPT to your one Trustwork agent and talk to the engine from where you already work, with consent you can withdraw. That connection is another hand of the same agent, under the same mandates, labels and kill switches; it can propose, and it can act only inside mandates you confirmed on the first-party surface. Chat text never authorises anything. If you type "go ahead", the agent turns it into a proposed mandate change that you confirm in a structured form, or it does not act.

Agents of different participants talk through structured envelopes, request, propose, counter, clarify, accept, decline, each referencing exact object versions with digests. The free-text note in an envelope is read like an e-mail: it can inform, never command. A seller's floor and a buyer's ceiling never leave their own node. Agents of competing sellers never see each other's prices, and the platform never relays one seller's quote to another.

What stays human¶

A plan version is approved by its owner. A quote above the mandate's ceiling, and every agreement, is accepted by a person. Field evidence and the confirmation of execution are human acts that can never be pre-authorised. Licensed acts, an engineer's stamp, an inspector's report, a payment, stay with the people and institutions the law names. Agents prepare, compare, chase and remind; they never pose as humans and never confirm what they did not witness.

Why this can work where directories did not¶

Three of the values in chapter 1 make the difference, and each is written into the engine rather than into a policy page: truth is labelled, private stays private, and no fakes. A hub that opens with real content and real companies clearly labelled, one event a month, a few respected local engineers writing, and useful matching from real needs, grows a community slowly and keeps it. One that fakes its first thousand members teaches everyone to leave.

The eight verbs are not features on a list. They are the reasons a person opens the hub on a Tuesday, and the record of trust is what accumulates while they do.

People come for what is useful. The record is what stays.

9Hubs, operators and one mesh

Trust is local and law is local, so the engine does not run as one website owned by one company. It runs as hubs: one for a country, a region or a city, each operated by people who answer for that place under its law, each a small company of agents and people with an accountable human for every function. One mesh connects them so that a hub in one city and a hub in another country complement each other without either owning the other. This chapter says how a hub is made, who runs it, what a country pack is, what the mesh carries and what it never carries, and how the civic hub, a direction of this book, fits the same shape.

Why hubs¶

Trust is local. In construction the hand that keeps a record is local: the association that has run the regional conference for thirty years, the trade publisher everyone reads, the university that trained half the engineers in the province. The law is local too. Who may call themselves an engineer, how an electronic signature binds, which payment providers are licensed: all of it changes at every border and often at every state line.

A single global platform would have to flatten all of this, and in flattening it would become a stranger everywhere. So the engine is something people run for a place they care about. A node is one installation: a database, a web origin, a signing key, an operator. A hub is a community for a territory hosted on a node. One node can host a country hub and several city hubs that share infrastructure and staff, or two different operators can run a country hub and a city hub on separate nodes and let the mesh join them. Territories are declared as data and are descriptive, never exclusive. A city hub and a country hub both cover the city; what happens there appears on both, each with its home hub named. Nobody owns a city.

Who runs a hub¶

A hub operator is a legal entity that brings three things the engine cannot: local knowledge, legal responsibility and people. The hub owner, the person, company or public body that owns the hub and its income, is often the operator itself; where they differ, the hub owner appoints the operator, and the operator answers for the territory's law. An operator may be an industry association, a chamber of commerce, a trade publisher, a university or training body, a cooperative, an entrepreneur or a community of engineers. The operator answers for compliance with the law of its territory, from privacy and data residency to consumer protection, electronic signatures, taxes and sanctions; the engine gives it the controls, the audit, the templates and the guard rails, and no legal advice. A hub can never declare a restricted place as its territory. Whoever runs a hub can do it alone: the engine, the handbook and the console are free software, and nobody needs Contribuda, which created the engine and maintains it, to run one. Contribuda takes no fee for the engine or its implementation; an operator who wants its tools or its help becomes its client on Contribuda's own commercial terms, as a separate, optional service, and may leave at any moment (chapter 23).

A hub is a small company¶

An operator must be able to launch a whole operating company in minutes and add people as the hub grows. The accepted design describes the company as twenty-three standing functions: leadership, strategy, editorial, marketing, outreach and partnerships, community, sales, support, trust and safety, compliance, finance, legal operations, people operations, quality and standards, culture and ethics, research, events and learning operations, localisation, data stewardship, IT and operations, procurement for the hub itself, analytics, and federation and network relations. Each function has agent roles that do its routine work and human positions that a person can fill later. On day one the operator is the owner of every function and the accountable person for every agent role.

The rules that keep this honest cannot be switched off. Every agent role starts at observing or drafting; raising one above drafting needs a second person and a budget cap, and the level drops by itself, never rises, when a quality floor is crossed, a kill switch is pulled, the model changes or the accountable person leaves. Decisions about staff, money, contracts, legal judgement, recognition and publication are unreachable for agents at every level. Agents never count as people for governance: not as launch-gate staff, not as a second approver, not as a reviewer. They have functional names and abstract icons, no portraits, and label every draft. The minimum real hub is therefore two people and the agent team: a real model provider, a real e-mail connection and the soft launch each need a second person.

Growth is a hand-over, not a replacement. When a function's queue runs over target for days, the console shows a hire signal with its reason, never a demand. The new person shadows the agent's queue, then reviews it, then takes it over, with every change approved and, for finance, compliance, trust, people and legal, approved by a second person. The shadow period's practice decisions are shown to the new person alone: no surveillance of staff, no comparison of a named person with an agent, no per-person productivity figure anywhere in the console. That is a condition of being a Trustwork hub.

A country's facts as data¶

The engine's code knows nothing about any country. Everything that differs lives in a country pack: registration identifier formats and the registers that issue them, regions and places, currencies, languages and scripts, holidays, licensing of trades and professions, codes and standards, tax and invoicing rules, electronic signature law, payment rails, data residency notes, procurement portals. Every fact carries a source and a date. A country-pack maintainer is the source of truth for their country's facts and is named in the governance of the engine. Where a pack is missing, a country not under sanctions works with a generic profile until someone contributes one. Packs exist in depth for the United States, the reference country, and for a handful of other countries; an EU layer carries what member states inherit. The civic engine extends the same idea: a country's participatory-budget law, its committee roles and its whistle-blower protection become pack facts.

Launch gates and the second person¶

A hub moves from draft to preparing to a soft launch by invitation to public, and the engine refuses each step until every gate is green: the operator's legal entity recorded and screened against sanctions lists; legal texts adapted with the operator's counsel; the data-residency decision recorded; two staff with distinct roles and second factors; an editor-in-chief and an editorial policy; community rules and every template in every hub language; a backup restored once, with evidence; an accessibility check. A gate that passed blocks again as soon as its precondition stops holding. Soft launch fills the hub with real content and real companies that claimed their pages. No fake activity is ever seeded.

Inside a running hub, sensitive configuration, money settings, non-fake providers and higher agent autonomy are activated by a second person. Nobody, including hub staff and the engine's own maintainers, can read private data without a scoped, time-limited case grant approved by a second person, and every such grant is on the audit record. There is no "log in as user" switch, and there never will be.

One mesh¶

Every node may join one worldwide mesh, with no central owner. The figure shows the shape: each node keeps its own database, operator and law; public projections flow between all of them; deal data flows only between the two nodes whose participants are party to the deal; and private data never leaves the node where it was written.

Seven principles govern the mesh, and the protocol enforces them rather than asking for good behaviour. Nodes are independent; federation connects and never subordinates. Public by default, private never. One writer per object: the node where something was created is its home, and everyone else holds a read-only replica with provenance. Links, never merges: two records about the same company are linked only when a person decides. Authority stays home: reputation is computed only by the subject's home node and shown elsewhere with its method version and samples; verification badges say which hub granted them; moderation is local. Corrections and deletions travel: a correction is a new revision everyone receives, a deletion is a signed tombstone everyone must apply within the agreed period, and a node that does not honour tombstones is defederated. Lawful everywhere: a node that declares a restricted place is refused at the handshake, and operators are screened before peering.

Peering is bilateral and graded. Two nodes can syndicate each other's public feeds, trust each other's verifications, route deals so that both nodes hold provably identical agreements, and accept each other's sign-in. Raising peering to deal routing or identity needs a second approver. Either side can end peering at any time; defederation stops new deliveries, never rewrites history, and lets running deals finish under their accepted terms.

Public facts about companies travel as open data with attribution; public person profiles may be redisplayed inside the mesh only, never exported in bulk or used to train models, because people chose to be visible, not to be harvested; individual reviews do not travel at all.

Governance¶

The engine is created and maintained by Contribuda and developed in the open, as chapter 6 describes. Its governance names an owner, who decides direction, each decision recorded and dated; a maintaining organisation; an integrator, who reviews and records acceptance; maintainers per scope; country-pack maintainers, who are the source of truth for their country's facts; hub operators, responsible for local compliance; and contributors with their agents, each person accountable for what their agent submits. The licence guarantees the exit: anyone can run the engine unbranded, and a hub whose operator changes its mind can leave the mesh and keep its data. What the licence does not give is a voice, and the open civic projects that outlived their founding city or their first sponsor all gave their operators one. An association of hub operators that holds a charter made from the engine's invariants and the list of things it will never build, and advises on its direction, is proposed here for the years ahead; the Trustwork name and mark remain Dmitry Parshkov's, as the repository's notice records. Contribuda would remain the maintainer, taking no fee from any hub for the engine; its part in the international layer, and only there, is to be decided (chapter 7). Nothing like the association exists yet.

The civic hub¶

The civic engine of Part IV needs an operator too, and here the shape of a hub is tested rather than changed. A municipality, a civic body or a university may run a civic hub for a city: alongside an industry hub run by an association or a publisher, or inside it as a second hub on the same node, or peered with it from a separate node. It carries the city's participatory-budget cycles, its public-works records and its institution delivery records; the industry hub carries the contractors, suppliers, experts and trades the civic loop needs. The two complement each other the way a country hub and a city hub already do: a public-works record on the civic hub links to the contractor's page on the industry hub; a resident's idea becomes a costed consultation with an estimator who is a participant of the industry hub; the tender is published to suppliers who opted in on both.

Running a civic hub adds launch gates of its own, set out in chapter 15. Any vote is held by a neutral keeper, and identity is proven outside the hub wherever the country allows. A public body gets what every operator gets, the engine, the console, the templates and the mesh. It does not get a view of private data, control of rankings, or a way to read a resident's votes, observations or complaints as a profile. Those are not settings; they are refusals. The hub operating company is an accepted design in the engine's handbook; a municipality running a civic hub is a step beyond it, found only in this book: its design is to be written as a decision record, and until then every sentence in this section is a hypothesis.

Every hub keeps its own law. The mesh keeps its word.

10A build a bank can trust

A family that builds its own house almost nowhere can borrow for it, and almost nowhere can insure it while it rises. This chapter says why, what has worked in the places that solved parts of the problem, and how Trustwork will turn the record it is designed to keep of a build into a file a licensed lender and a licensed insurer can read. Trustwork never lends, never holds money and never advises on credit. It supplies the standard and the evidence. The records this chapter builds on exist in the engine; the build file itself is a direction of this book, not yet in the repository's designs.

Where a private build is financed at all, there is a standard record of the plan, money released in stages against an inspection, a warranty or defect insurance tied to those inspections, and a title that can carry a lien. Where any of the four is missing, the family pays cash, builds in bursts as money arrives, and carries every risk alone.

The problem is the object, not the borrower¶

Most housing in the developing world is built by its owners, in stages, from savings, remittances and small loans. The World Bank's stocktaking of housing in Sub-Saharan Africa says that "most housing is built incrementally" and that in 2011 only three per cent of the region's population was eligible for a conventional mortgage. Kenya, a country of more than fifty million people, had 30,015 residential mortgages outstanding in 2023, and 284,472 microfinance loans. Roughly ninety per cent of Ghana's housing stock is produced informally, by a 2011 estimate. In Nigeria 71.4 per cent of landlords held no title in a national survey of 2018. UN-Habitat's World Cities Report of 2026 concludes that "conventional housing finance systems have generally failed to engage with the reality of incremental upgrading." Nor is the problem confined to poor countries: in the United States, 176,932 single-family homes were started in 2024 on the owner's own land, 17.5 per cent of all single-family starts, and lending for them exists only through a specialised product with its own rules.

Lenders refuse not because the borrower is poor but because the object is unreadable. Read any working instrument from the inside and the same gaps appear, each a missing record rather than a missing person.

  • No valuation of an unfinished asset. A lender needs an "as completed" value and a way to re-check it as the building rises.
  • No standard plan and budget. Stage payments, against interim valuations or a fixed schedule of costs, need a plan broken into stages.
  • No reliable builder. Where no contractor record exists, nobody can be prequalified.
  • No inspection of stages. Without an inspector, the lender can pay nothing until completion, which the owner cannot pre-finance, or pay in advance and hope.
  • Change without a record. A loan may rise "only to cover documented increased construction costs".
  • Title. The lien is the collateral; without a registrable title there is no mortgage, only a personal loan.
  • Informal income. Where most work is informal, the answer is rarely a twenty-year mortgage but a sequence of small loans, each secured by the stage it finances, which again needs the stage recorded.

Insurers refuse for the same reasons and one more: nobody records defects. A private build with no history of who built what and what was fixed has no actuarial history. France solved this by law, making defect insurance compulsory for builder and owner in 1978 and producing decades of claims data; Britain through a warranty provider that inspects key stages during the build and then insures the house for ten years. Both depend on a record kept while the house was being built, not reconstructed afterwards.

What worked elsewhere¶

The instruments differ in law and language and agree in shape. In the United States a construction-to-permanent loan covers the build and the mortgage that follows: the appraisal is of the house "as completed", the lender inspects "prior to issuing draws" and holds back undrawn funds, and the loan converts only on a completion report, lien waivers and a certificate of occupancy. In Britain a self-build mortgage pays at named stages, "wall plate level", "wind and watertight", "first fix and plastering", after the lender's valuer has seen the work, and the lender expects a ten-year warranty whose provider inspected the build. Germany wrote the schedule into law for homes bought from a developer: up to seven instalments, thirty per cent after earthworks begin, forty per cent of the balance at the shell, five per cent on completion. France made insurance the licence to build: a constructor must hold ten-year liability cover before opening the site, and if no insurer will quote, a tariff office sets the premium.

The countries Trustwork looks at first have pieces of the same machine. Peru pays a family a housing bond of 32,100 soles to build on its own registered, lien-free plot, through a registered technical entity that signs the contract, submits plans and budget, triggers the disbursement and has 120 days to finish: the state standardised the role, and nobody has yet standardised the record. Mexico's housing fund lets a worker build on land they own with money released in stages, as the press describes it; the Philippines' housing fund releases construction loans in tranches after inspection, as a law firm's commentary describes it. Kenya has a mortgage refinance company that sets eligibility criteria for the lenders it refinances, and a deep housing microfinance sector lending small, short sums for a room or a roof. Uzbekistan built a mortgage market in five years by building its records first, a refinancing company, a housing price index and a mortgage database, and the company had supported more than twenty thousand mortgages by January 2025.

One precedent matters more than the rest, because it shows a standard turning into a bank product. The International Finance Corporation's EDGE certificate asks for twenty per cent less energy, water and embodied carbon than a local baseline, audited by a third party. By February 2023 it had certified 357,000 housing units, in a programme active in eighty-nine countries; Colombia went from no certified new build in 2017 to more than twenty-seven per cent of new build by 2023; a South African bank launched the country's first green mortgage on 807 certified homes. The certificate did not lend. It made the object legible, and lenders priced what they could read. The Open Contracting Data Standard did the same for public contracts. Nobody has done it for the private build itself.

A bank does not need to trust the builder. It needs to read the building.

The bankable build file¶

A build file is a consented, versioned projection of one project for one reader: a lender, an insurer, a guarantor. It contains only the fields that reader needs, each traceable to a record the engine already keeps; it is not a new database but a projection with an audience the owner adds. Most of what it draws on is already built in the engine's API or designed; what is new is the lender as an audience, the inspector who signs inside it, and the open standard that lets every lender read it the same way. Money released by evidence¶

The sequence is simple and every step of it is human. The owner approves a plan version with stage gates; the hub's stage template suggests the gates and the typical share of cost at each. The owner adds a licensed lender, and where the agreement uses one a licensed escrow provider, as a reader of the build file. The lender decides in its own systems. Work proceeds. The contractor files a work record with evidence, the inspector files an act, the owner files an acceptance. When all three exist for a stage, the engine composes a disbursement instruction: the agreement digest, the stage, the amount the agreement names, the three record references, the file version. The parties sign it; the licensed provider executes it; an unknown outcome goes to reconciliation, never to a blind retry. Lien waivers, retainage and deposit limits follow the country pack.

Trustwork is the clerk, never the cashier. That is a recorded decision and a tested rule of the engine: it never holds, pools or moves participants' funds. Where no licensed provider exists, the engine records obligations and evidence while money moves outside it, and the file is still worth having to a lender who pays by its own transfer.

Inspectors, valuers and insurers¶

The missing participant in every instrument above is the person who stands in the trench. An inspector on Trustwork is a person or firm with a verified credential, a mandate on this project, a narrow capability (read the shared stage and attach an act; never edit the plan, accept work or see a budget the owner has not shared) and a reputation of their own: acts filed on time, acts later contradicted by a confirmed defect, appeals heard. Their agent may schedule visits and draft the act; the person signs it. A valuer works the same way. Peru's registered technical entities, the community construction management teams of a federation of the urban poor in Durban, and the American lender's draw inspector are the three archetypes; each country pack says which licence the role needs.

An insurer added as a reader sees what a British warranty provider sees: who built, which stages were inspected and by whom, which defects were found and fixed. Its certificate, builder's risk during the works, latent defects after them, catastrophe cover where the hazard is wind, water or ground, becomes a credential with an expiry, checked like a licence, and over time the hub's open statistics give it the frequency data it lacks. Trustwork never ranks insurers, never takes a commission on a policy, and never says "buy this".

A hub's open statistics¶

A loan officer approving a draw for "foundations complete" needs to know whether that stage in this town usually costs what the budget says; an insurer needs the frequency of roof defects by building type. So each hub publishes, per period, region, building type and stage, the range of cost per square metre and of duration, the share of stages passed at first inspection, the defect rate by category and the frequency and cause of change. Only from projects whose owners consented to contribute; only cells above a minimum sample, checked against re-identification in small towns; never a project, a person, a company or an address; published with the method version under an open licence, and offered to the national statistics office as a contribution, not a replacement. This is the bridge to the civic ring: the same cells that tell a lender what a roof should cost tell a town what a school roof should cost, and a budget line outside the hub's range is a question worth asking in public.

An open Build File Data Standard¶

One lender reading one hub's export is a pilot. Many lenders reading one format is a market. Trustwork will publish a Build File Data Standard: a schema and a vocabulary for the sections above, with stable keys across versions, crosswalks to open building classifications, a mapping to the open contracting standard where a build is public, fixtures in every engine language, and a validator. Its licence will be open, so that any lender, insurer, inspector's application or national programme can read and write it without Trustwork. Trustwork owns none of the data, only the stewardship of the schema, which it can hand to a neutral body when one exists. The standard is what makes "many lenders read one format" true, as EDGE made "many banks read one certificate" true.

How we will act¶

Step by step, in the arc's order, and everywhere with the same rule: Trustwork is the record, never the regime. Naming an organisation in this chapter records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

First. Publish the first draft of the open standard, and add to the engine the parts the table shows as missing: the inspector and valuer roles, the stage closure record, the disbursement instruction and the consent trail for a reader. Today there are no work packages for them.

Then pilot where a staged, inspected disbursement already exists in law or practice, so the hub supplies the record to a regime that already wants it: Peru, where registered technical entities already sign the contract and trigger the bond, and where Spanish, an engine language still awaiting native review, is the reference hub's second language; Mexico and the Philippines, whose housing funds already release money in stages; Kenya, where a refinance company sets criteria for many lenders and housing microfinance is deep; and the San Diego hub, where an owner building an accessory dwelling unit under a construction-to-permanent loan already lives with draw inspections. Nigeria and Ghana come second: the need is greatest and the title base weakest, so they begin with evidence and statistics, not finance. Uzbekistan, Rwanda and the Dominican Republic follow as their programmes are studied.

Readers, not partners. A refinance company matters most: if the build file enters its eligibility criteria, every lender it refinances reads it. Housing microfinance institutions with incremental products, national housing funds that already disburse in stages, the development finance institutions behind them and inspection bodies follow; insurers are described by kind. Contact is inbound: a licensed institution that wants to read the standard asks. Chapter 21 describes them.

The lines we keep¶

The law around housing finance is close on every side, and the design stays on the information-only side of it until counsel says otherwise. Trustwork never lends and never holds money; that is a recorded decision and a tested refusal in the payment layer. It never gives credit or insurance advice: the agent may explain what a build file contains and what lenders generally ask for, and may never recommend a lender, a product or a sum. It never acts as a credit intermediary: the European mortgage credit directive defines one as a person who "presents or offers credit agreements" or "assists consumers by undertaking preparatory work", and excludes those "merely introducing"; Trustwork hands the owner a file, and the owner hands it to whom they choose. It never distributes insurance: the European distribution directive counts a ranked comparison with a way to buy as distribution and excludes "the provision of information on an incidental basis"; so a hub may list licensed insurers without ranking, remuneration or a button, or not at all. And it takes no remuneration from lenders or insurers for consumer housing finance, in any country: United States law forbids "any fee, kickback or other thing of value" for referring settlement-service business, and Contribuda takes no remuneration from lenders or insurers at all. Business finance is a different matter for a hub, and the engine's product specification draws the line: a hub may list licensed trade-credit and project-finance partners for companies, with any referral fee disclosed before a referral is made; consumer lending only through partners licensed for it; and payment never changes ranking, matching, reputation or what a family is shown.

Three more lines. Trustwork never produces a credit score or a "bankability score"; it produces a file, and the lender decides. Consent is specific and revocable; revocation stops the next read and cannot unsend what a lender lawfully received. And a build file may never become a condition a public subsidy programme imposes on families who do not use Trustwork; the standard is open precisely so that it never has to be.

Whether a build-file export offered to licensed lenders is "merely introducing" or an arranging activity under that country's law; how that country treats a disclosed referral fee from a licensed business lender, since for consumer housing finance there is none; the licensing of the disbursement provider; electronic signatures on acts and instructions; inspectors' liability; the data-protection basis for each shared field and each published aggregate. What stays human¶

Approving a plan version. Accepting a stage. Signing an act or a valuation. The credit decision, in the lender's own systems; underwriting, in the insurer's. Consenting to share, and to contribute to statistics. Agents compile the draft file for the owner's review, schedule the inspector's visit and prepare the instruction for human signature. They never approve, inspect, decide, sign or consent.

How we will know¶

Projects with a complete build file, and no stage marked inspected without an inspector's record. The share of stages with an independent act before a tranche moved. Cost variance and defect rates per stage. Lenders and insurers that read the standard, counted as institutions; refinance companies that cite it. Zero funds held, zero fees from lenders or insurers for consumer credit, zero findings of unlicensed activity. And what owners say at completion, in consented research: whether they borrowed, and at which stage. We will publish what did not work.

Part IV · The civic engine

11Why construction is the doorway

Public money becomes visible when it becomes a building. A school roof, a clinic, a drainage channel, a staircase up a hillside, a market shed, a road: these are where a budget line turns into something a resident can touch, photograph and judge. They are also where the most public money is lost and where the most people work without any record of their skill. This chapter makes the case, with the best available figures and their status, that the civic engine is the next ring of the same engine that is being built for the construction industry, and not a second product.

Where public money becomes visible¶

When residents are asked what to do with a share of a city's budget, they overwhelmingly ask for things to be built. In one national programme for which a breakdown was published, Uzbekistan's Initiative Budget in 2022, thirty-two per cent of the funded projects were internal roads, thirteen per cent school repairs and equipment, twelve per cent street lighting, eight per cent water and drainage and six per cent hospital renovation, as the press reported. Porto Alegre's participatory budget of the 1990s is remembered for infrastructure: the most quoted figures, from secondary sources with differing baselines, are water connections rising from three quarters of residences to almost all of them in a decade, and sewer coverage nearly doubling. Paris's open dataset of winning projects, New York's council-district projects and Madrid's showcased cases are footbridges, playgrounds, lifts, school yards and street works. No single reliable figure exists for the share of public budgets worldwide that becomes buildings and infrastructure, and this book does not invent one. The pattern is enough: the participatory part of a budget is a construction programme by another name.

That is the first reason construction is the doorway. A platform that wants to help residents decide, and then to show them what their decision became, has to understand plans, permits, bills of quantities, tenders, site stages, acceptance and defects. Those are not civic concepts. They are the daily concepts of the industry Trustwork was built for.

What is lost, and how well we know it¶

The figure most often quoted is that ten to thirty per cent of investment in publicly funded construction is lost to mismanagement and corruption. It deserves its status: it is an estimate made by CoST, the Infrastructure Transparency Initiative, in 2012, described by its own former chair as a business-as-usual scenario rather than a measurement, and repeated since by the OECD and the Open Government Partnership. The better-sourced number is the IMF's model of 2015, which puts about thirty per cent of the potential value of public investment lost to inefficiency, not only to theft. The academic anchor, a World Bank working paper of 2007, gives no monetary figure at all and makes a sharper point: the largest cost of corruption in construction is not the money that disappears but the poor-quality infrastructure that gets built, with weak returns and no funded maintenance. The OECD's foreign-bribery report of 2014 adds that fifty-seven per cent of the bribery cases it analysed involved public contracts.

Then there is the case that put a number on it in 2025. The Philippines examined 9,855 flood-control projects worth 545.64 billion pesos contracted between July 2022 and May 2025. By October 2025 the public-works department reported 421 projects that did not exist among about 8,000 inspected: roughly one in twenty by count. The finance department put losses at up to 118.5 billion pesos for 2023 to 2025, against 545.64 billion pesos contracted over the whole period. Nine licensed construction companies, which their part-owner told a Senate hearing belonged to one family, had bid against each other; the licensing board, which had kept renewing their licences, revoked them on 1 September 2025, a decision the companies said they would appeal. Those figures are from the press reporting official statements, and the independent commission, which closed in March 2026 after handing its findings to prosecutors, had published no final report as of November 2026. The losses land inside the CoST range. They also make the point the World Bank paper made in 2007: every record that was supposed to protect the public existed, the procurement notices, the project lists, the licences, the audits, and none of them had tied the money to the concrete.

Loss is not only theft. Peru's audit office counted 2,428 stalled public works worth 44.3 billion soles on 30 June 2025, seven in ten of them municipal. A stalled work is a promise with a photograph of nothing. Kenya's counties spent fifty-seven per cent of their development budgets in the 2024/25 year, against seventy-eight per cent of their budgets overall; only twelve of forty-seven counties passed seventy per cent, and one reported no development spending at all. Execution, not allocation, is where a budget is kept or broken, and execution in a development budget means building.

The builders nobody records¶

Construction is one of the three sectors with the largest share of informal employment, as chapter 1 said. In the countries where the engine is especially needed, most houses are built by their owners and by masons, carpenters and electricians who have no certificate, no insurance and no record of a single job they have finished well. Where that is true, buildings fall. Nigeria's Building Collapse Prevention Guild, a professional body, counted 653 collapses and 1,616 deaths between 1974 and May 2025, more than half of the incidents in its 2024 report in Lagos alone. The causes named by the engineering regulator, the standards body and the Lagos authorities are the same every time: unqualified builders, substandard materials, departures from the design, weak supervision, no soil test, no stage certification. Every one of those is a missing record before it is an engineering fault: who built it, what was delivered, whether a stage was accepted and by whom.

This is the second reason. The civic loop ends with reputation, and in the places that need it most, the people whose reputation is at stake have never had a record to build one from. A worker's lifelong profile, a certificate anyone can check by its number, a portfolio backed by acceptance acts: these are the first rung of formality, and they are the heart of the engine's design.

Participation stops at the vote¶

Governments have learned to publish budgets and have not learned to listen: the Open Budget Survey's average for public participation is seventeen out of a hundred, as chapter 13 shows. And where they do listen, through participatory budgeting, the listening stops when the vote closes. The leading participation platforms, free software governed by associations and listed as digital public goods, all end at a status label set by the administration: "in study", "in progress", "completed". None of them holds the tender, the contract, the site photographs, the acceptance act or the contractor's record. Chapter 12 shows that delivery is the weakest link of participatory budgeting everywhere, and that the reasons it fails are procurement and construction reasons: feasibility, costing, contracting, supervision, acceptance, payment.

So the gap that every civic programme leaves is exactly the shape of what a construction engine does. That is the third reason, and the decisive one.

The next ring, not a second product¶

It would be easier to describe the civic engine as a new product for governments. It would also be wrong. A civic tool built apart from the industry has no estimators to cost an idea, no contractors to bid, no inspectors with a record and no way to know whether the roof it reports as finished has in fact been finished. An industry tool built apart from the public has no reason to publish what the public paid for. Trustwork grows the civic ring out of the industry core because the core is where the evidence is made: on site, by the people who build, at the moment a stage is accepted. The resident's idea at the start of the loop and the resident's confirmation near its end are two new stations on a track the engine is built to run.

None of this makes Trustwork a voting system for elections, a view for the state of its residents, or a replacement for councils, auditors, inspectors or courts; chapters 4 and 15 say so and how it is enforced.

Where it is especially needed is where all three reasons are strongest at once: where most building is informal, where public works are where the money goes missing, and where residents are already being asked to vote on budgets that nobody follows into concrete. Chapter 20 names those places. This chapter only had to show that the door is already in the wall.

The budget line and the building are the same promise, seen from two ends.

12Residents decide: participatory and initiative budgets

Since 1989 cities have let residents decide a share of what gets built. The idea has spread worldwide, has saved children's lives where it lasted, and has failed, again and again, at the same place: between the vote and the building. This chapter takes the best of what the world has learned, including the failures, and says how Trustwork's participatory-budget module will work. The module is a direction of this book, not yet in the engine's designs.

What the idea is, and what travelled¶

Participatory budgeting began in Porto Alegre in 1989. Residents met in neighbourhood assemblies, ranked their needs, elected delegates and decided a share of the city's capital budget; the city built what they decided. The World Resources Institute's study credits the programme with including the poor in decisions, breaking clientelism and redistributing infrastructure, and names four conditions: structured participation, adequate money, political commitment, and a government that implements what was decided. Commitment declined, and the city suspended the process in 2017. By 2019 the Participatory Budgeting World Atlas counted more than eleven thousand experiences worldwide, a quarter of them in South America.

What travelled was not quite the idea. Baiocchi and Ganuza distinguish a communicative dimension, the meetings and the deliberation, from an empowerment dimension, the binding link from meeting to decision, and find that only the first spread in the idea's global expansion. Most of the world's participatory budgets are consultations that a council may ignore. The ones that work bind the budget.

Two families now exist, and the book uses both names. Participatory budgeting in its classic form lets residents allocate a share of the public capital budget by deliberation and vote; the state pays and builds. Initiative budgeting adds co-financing: residents and local businesses pay a part of the project, often in kind, in labour, materials or land, and the project is framed as a joint venture of community and state. The name comes from a World Bank programme of the late 2000s that financed micro-projects proposed by local communities on condition that they contributed, and it travelled through the Bank's advice to Central Asia and the Caucasus; Uzbekistan's national programme carries the name today. The distinction that matters is the same for both: does the vote bind the budget, and does anyone follow the money into the building?

What it reliably achieves¶

The strongest causal evidence is Brazilian. Across municipalities from 1990 to 2004, those that adopted participatory budgeting spent a larger share of their budgets on health and sanitation, and infant mortality fell, with the effect strongest in municipalities that had run the programme for more than four years. A second study of Brazil's largest cities over twenty years finds the same, strengthening the longer the programme ran. In Peru, where a 2003 law made participatory budgeting mandatory for every regional and local government, a 2024 study finds increased pro-poor spending and better quality of life, attributed not to strong civil society but to a competent finance ministry that reduced the information gap between residents and officials.

What it does not reliably achieve is as well documented: fiscal discipline, large infrastructure, persistence across political cycles, equal participation by the poorest, and, above all, implementation.

Where it fails: between the vote and the building¶

Read the right-hand column again and one pattern covers almost every row. Votes are fast; concrete is slow; and the administration that must deliver is not the one that promised. The Philippines ran a national Bottom-up Budgeting programme from 2012 to 2016 for tens of thousands of small projects chosen with civil society; of 59,684 projects planned for 2013 to 2015, 16,576 were completed, 27.8 per cent as reported, and 7,928 were dropped, with 8.5 billion pesos tied up in them; the next government excluded it from the 2017 budget. In Uzbekistan a civic monitor found single-glazed doors where double were specified, used transformers bought as new, and projects whose money never arrived, as reported; its recommendations read like a specification: public review of design documents, strict deadlines for contracting, better costing, and a handover act signed by the facility head and public representatives.

The failures have a shape, and each has a safeguard with evidence behind it. Capture and rubber-stamping need binding rules and published feasibility criteria. Clientelism, the thing Porto Alegre set out to break and the thing a deputy's lane reintroduces, needs allocation only by rule, with every discretionary award labelled as such. Vote buying and coerced voting need a ballot verified once and stored unlinked, an aggregate anomaly audit, and a consequence that lands on the project, not only on the votes; Uzbekistan's decision in 2025 to disqualify coerced projects and call compelled voting forced labour did more than any login screen. Low turnout needs paper and assisted channels, young voters and many languages; New York's vote was three-quarters on paper. Exclusion by co-financing, documented in sixteen Kyrgyz villages in 2011, needs contributions to be optional, capped, and never a condition for the poorest neighbourhoods to be served. Tokenism needs a share of the real capital budget fixed in law. Cost surprises need professional costing before the ballot, which every mature programme now inserts and most do badly: the proposer guesses, the real price arrives at tender, the project stalls. Political cycles kill programmes written in decrees; the ones that survived were written in law, measured in public and left a record.

The rule from Makueni, and the condition from Indonesia¶

Two findings decide the design. In Makueni County the contractor is paid only after a committee of residents, elected in a public forum for that project, has approved the work. A resident's signature sits between the builder and the money. That is the strongest counter-example to the pattern above, and the Dominican law's works committees and Mexico City's oversight committees reach for the same thing without the payment link.

The condition comes from a randomised study of about six hundred village roads in Indonesia. Raising the probability of a professional audit from four per cent to certainty cut missing expenditure by about eight percentage points and paid for itself. Grassroots monitoring on its own had little average effect: it reduced missing wages, which villagers could see, and not missing materials, which they could not, and it worked only where elites could not capture the meeting. Engineers had to measure the road to know what was missing. The lesson is not that residents cannot verify. It is that a resident's confirmation needs a professional measurement to compare against and a consequence attached to the finding.

Confirmation without consequence is tokenism with photographs.

How Trustwork's module will work¶

The module is not yet designed in the engine, and it is meant to be what the platforms above are not: not a better ballot, but the thing that carries a project after the ballot. Where a city already votes on Decidim or Consul, both free software under the GNU Affero licence, like the engine, and both listed as digital public goods, Trustwork does not ask it to switch. The vote stays where it is; the winning project is handed to the hub and lives there to acceptance. Where a city has no platform, the module offers the vote too, on the rules below, and the vote always has a neutral keeper: the municipality, its electoral institute or an independent civic body operates it, as Mexico City's institute does, and the operator of an industry hub never runs a vote.

The build is the unit, not the vote. The civic object is a public project with plan versions, the same object a homeowner's house is, with a public projection. It carries every step from the idea to the acceptance, the imported procurement notice included.

Costing is a consultation. A proposed idea becomes a scoped, paid consultation for a local estimator, engineer or architect on the hub, who returns a feasibility note and a cost range with a method under their own name, and earns a record from how the final quotes land against it.

The ballot is data, not code. The module supports approval voting, knapsack voting, the Method of Equal Shares, district envelopes and a need-weighted floor, configured from the country pack and the city's rules. Identity is proven outside the hub wherever the country allows it, by a national electronic identity, a bank identity or an in-person desk named in the pack; the ballot is stored unlinked, so that no role, staff included, can ever learn how a person voted. The roll is auditable in aggregate, one vote per verified person per cycle, with anomaly reports published to the city and the public, and paper and assisted votes count under the same roll. The Polish ombudsman's test is the design rule: prevent double voting while collecting the least personal data. A project whose votes are shown to be bought or coerced can be removed by the keeper under a published rule, not only its votes.

Co-financing is a disclosed term. Resident and business contributions are recorded as obligations, in kind at a published rate, optional, capped, and never a condition for the poorest areas to be served. A business paying half of a road to its own gate is shown as what it is: a procurement decision with a civic label.

The tender is sealed. Quotes are frozen once sent and unreadable until the deadline, and the winner's agreement is one both sides can prove identical: the answer to Madrid's "turned out not to be feasible" and to the Uzbek projects that were never contracted.

A resident act between the contractor and the money. Each milestone closes with the inspector's act, dated and placed photographs, and a confirmation by the named members of a project committee elected in a public forum, compared against the bill of quantities and the inspector's act, so that it is an attestation of an observed fact, not a vote of thanks. The hub then signals the municipality's own finance system that the stage is accepted. Trustwork never holds the money.

The dropped project has a page. A winner that cannot be built gets a stated reason, a date, the role responsible, and a substitute chosen by the same rule that chose it. The Philippines dropped 7,928 projects and Madrid discarded 182; here a dropped project is a record, not a silence.

Open data, and the law as data. Every project's stages publish as a feed the city can place on its own portal, mapped to the Open Contracting Data Standard, as Paris's stage dataset and Makueni's tracking portal already do in their own forms. Each country's thresholds, committee roles and deadlines are sourced facts in its pack.

Records for contractors and institutions, never for residents. A contractor's public-works record feeds its reputation; the department that commissioned the work gets a delivery record. No resident gains or loses anything from any civic act, ever, and the schema has no place to put it.

What stays human¶

Agents draft ideas from a photograph and a sentence, find hearing dates, assemble a cost range for an estimator to sign and remind. None of them votes, costs, signs, accepts, confirms or releases money. The idea is a resident's. The cost is a professional's name on a number. The vote is one person's, once. The acceptance is an inspector's act and a committee's confirmation. And the choice to adopt any of this is a lawful municipality's, made in public. A platform cannot make a government keep its promises. It can make the promise, the price, the build and the confirmation one record that nobody can quietly edit, and leave the rest to the people the record was made for.

Let residents decide, and then show them what their decision became.

13Following public money into concrete

The world has built standards for publishing budgets and contracts, programmes for disclosing infrastructure projects, and registers of millions of photographed public works. Each sees one stretch of the road. The loop closes only when a budget line, a contract, a photograph of the finished work and a confirmation by someone who can be held to it sit on one page, with every source named. This chapter says what exists, what it taught, and what Trustwork will do with it. The public-works record and the institution's delivery record are a direction of this book; the import of open procurement data is designed in the engine and not yet built.

Open budgets: publishing more than listening¶

The Open Budget Survey of the International Budget Partnership is the only independent, comparative measure of budget transparency, participation and oversight. Its 2025 round, the tenth, covered eighty-two countries and documents through the end of 2024; the global average for transparency was forty-five out of a hundred, where sixty-one counts as sufficient, and the average for public participation seventeen, up from fifteen in the 2023 round that covered 125 countries. Of the fourteen countries of this book that the survey covers, counting the 2023 round for those absent from 2025, the Dominican Republic listens best, with a participation score of fifty, and Nigeria least, with seven. Participation, in the survey's sense, means formal, inclusive, recorded ways for the public to take part in formulation, approval, execution and audit. Almost nowhere does it exist at the execution stage, which is the stage where a building is or is not built.

The principles that say it should exist are old: the Global Initiative for Fiscal Transparency's high-level principles, recognised by the United Nations General Assembly in December 2012, assert the public's right to direct participation in fiscal policy. The practice that followed is mostly documents and execution portals such as Brazil's, which has published spending since 2004, and Ukraine's, launched in 2015. A portal shows that money moved. It does not show what it became.

Open contracting: the standard that worked¶

The Open Contracting Data Standard is a common data model for publishing contracting information at every stage, planning, tender, award, contract and implementation, with documents attached and everything about one process linked by one identifier. Its infrastructure extension, built by the Open Contracting Partnership and CoST and released under an open licence, adds the project level: one project, many contracts, one timeline. The partnership's registry listed 136 datasets as of November 2026. Nine of the fourteen countries of this book publish in the standard nationally (Colombia, Peru, Kenya, the Dominican Republic, Rwanda, Nigeria, Mexico, Ghana and Georgia), as does Ukraine, and the United States through one city; the Philippines, Armenia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan do not yet, though the Philippines has committed to.

The evidence that openness changes behaviour is real, and most of it comes from the people who promote it. Ukraine's system is the best documented: competitive procurement grew from 616.8 billion hryvnia in 2023 to 841 billion in 2024, under full-scale war, and the average number of bids per tender rose from 2.03 to 2.38, by the economy ministry's own figures. Its technical lesson is that all data is open by default and civil society gets the same interface as the state. Colombia reports more bidders and fewer direct contracts where its second-generation system is used; Paraguay reports contract amendments falling from nineteen per cent to three. A fair reading is that open contracting reliably increases visibility and often competition; its effect on corruption and on the quality of the built asset depends on who uses the data and whether anyone can act on it. Ukraine shows that too: in the first year of its community monitoring layer more than three thousand violations were reported and five fines followed; years later, with the monitors feeding the state audit service and publishing the outcome of each complaint, the anti-corruption agency credits them with tens of millions of hryvnia saved in a single month. The difference was not the data. It was a named authority with a duty to respond and a published result per finding.

Infrastructure transparency: the forty data points¶

CoST, the Infrastructure Transparency Initiative, was launched in 2012 with World Bank support after a three-year pilot in eight countries. Its approach has four parts that Trustwork takes as a specification: a group of government, industry and civil society working together; disclosure of forty data points across a project's life; an independent assurance review that checks the disclosed data and translates its gaps into plain language; and social accountability, meaning that media, civil society and academia use the findings. Its Infrastructure Transparency Index scores a country's procuring entities on disclosure, assurance and practice: Uganda rose from 20.8 to 32.26 per cent between 2021 and 2024; Ghana scored 35.76 in December 2025, a result the country's business press headlined as "not encouraging". The index is the closest existing thing to a delivery record for public institutions, and it is in beta almost everywhere; an evaluation in 2026 found that nine of CoST's nineteen active members had run a structured scrutiny process in the previous five years.

Evidence of the concrete¶

Photographing public works with a date and a location is not a new idea. India has done it at a scale nothing else approaches: since an agreement of June 2016 between the rural development ministry, the space agency's remote sensing centre and the national informatics centre, every asset completed under the rural employment guarantee is geo-tagged with two photographs, moderated by a state officer and shown on a public map; the centre's count is about thirty million assets. Peru's audit office runs a register of every public work with progress and photographs; it is the register that counted the stalled works of chapter 11, and officials who fail to update it face sanction. The Inter-American Development Bank's investment map, which shows project progress to the public and whose pilots report faster physical and financial execution, was made open source in May 2025 and runs in fourteen countries.

The Philippine flood-control scandal (chapter 11) showed the difference. The registers that failed were registers of paper: a licence renewed, a notice posted. The records that worked were records of evidence: an inspector on site, a resident with a phone, an auditor with a map.

A portal shows that money moved. Evidence shows what it became.

Social audit: what makes resident verification credible¶

Resident verification has a long record and a clear verdict. In India every state must have a social audit unit independent of the agency that spends the money; Andhra Pradesh created the first in 2009, trained village auditors to verify muster rolls and works door to door, and held public hearings at which the implementing officials had to sit. The audits detected more each round. Their effect on behaviour was weak, because follow-up and punishment were weak: in 2021/22, of the money flagged nationally, fifteen per cent was recovered. Indonesia's complaint system has taken more than a million and a half reports and is judged ineffective against abuse of authority because institutions do not answer. Nigeria's open-contracting portal, promised in 2016, had eight compliant ministries in 2018. A channel without an accountable responder is a suggestion box.

Across the cases that worked, five things recur. An independent, funded body convenes the audit. Field evidence carries its provenance: location, timestamp, two photographs, who took them. Each finding gets a public hearing or a public outcome. A named authority has a duty to respond within a time limit. And the follow-up is published: resolved, not resolved, why. The Indonesian roads study of chapter 12 adds the sixth: a professional measurement to compare against. Scorecard trials in Ugandan clinics point the same way: monitoring alone is unproven; monitoring with a measurement and a consequence is not.

What Trustwork will do, said exactly¶

Three layers, with their status. The first exists in the engine's designs for the industry and is reused. The second and third are new to the civic engine and not yet designed in it. The first civic product of the San Diego hub is to be this evidence layer, a "works of the city" built from open permits and capital lines, and not a vote.

Sourced pages from open procurement data (designed, not built). The engine's discovery design already treats a procurement portal as a source, shows a public tender as a "public procurement notice" on the needs board and never as a member's need, and builds project pages only from public notices, permits and awards, linked to companies by their registration identifiers, never naming homeowners or private addresses. The procurement design names the Open Contracting Data Standard as its exchange format; adapters for the launch countries' portals are an open work package. Every such page is labelled "from an open source" with the portal, the notice, the licence and the date; nothing on it is a Trustwork judgement. The civic engine adds scope: each award becomes a sourced contract page beneath the supplier's company page, and each infrastructure project a sourced public-works page with its contracts beneath it.

A public-works record with field evidence and resident confirmation (not yet designed). A public-works page gains a timeline: milestones from the source system, the contractor's own execution records if the contractor is a participant, and the client's or inspector's confirmation as the authorised decision. Residents add observations: dated, geo-referenced photographs of the asset, two at minimum, in the manner of India's register, with the observer's identity held by the hub's trust reviewer and never published. Each observation passes through public states, received, acknowledged, confirmed or disputed, resolved, as Ukraine's monitors publish theirs, and what to look at comes from CoST's assurance practice. A confirmed observation that contradicts a reported milestone changes no score by itself; it opens a case for a person, and the case and its outcome are on the page.

An institution's delivery record (not yet designed). For a municipality, a ministry or an agency, a page that answers one question with public data only: what was promised, budgeted, built and accepted, and when. Budget planned against executed by year, from the open budget portal. Projects promised, completed and stalled, from the national works register and from completions the hub has confirmed. Audit findings, from the supreme audit institution. Participation events held. Kenya's county absorption rates, Peru's stalled-works count and Costa Rica's transparency index by institution are the kind of input, each with its source and date. Chapter 15 says why this is a record and not a rating, and why no official is ever its subject.

Civic agents, under the same mandates. A resident's agent, a contractor's agent and a municipality's standing representative work on these pages under the same registry and mandates (chapters 5 and 8); nothing is sent without a person.

The people who look¶

Monitoring is dangerous, and a design that cannot protect the monitor is not a design. The Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative's tracker counts eighty-two people killed and 166 assaulted in India since 2005 for using their right to information; the figures are from a secondary report of the tracker. The Philippines has no national whistle-blower protection law and relies on an ad-hoc witness programme; its Senate recommended one in 2026. The European Union protects whistle-blowers by a directive of 2019 and, since 2024, protects public participation against abusive lawsuits in cross-border cases. The difference between those legal environments must be a fact in the country pack, with its source, and it must change how a hub behaves.

So the public-works record is built around the observer's safety. An observation is pseudonymous by default: the hub's trust reviewer knows who made it and the page does not. It carries the location of the asset and never the location of the reporter. Where protection is weak, the pack forces pseudonymity, the operator may embargo observations until the inspector responds, and a resident may submit through an institution with standing, a legally recognised oversight committee, an auditor or a monitoring organisation, so that no individual is exposed. Publishing "the contractor failed" is an allegation; publishing "the acceptance act of this date records defects; remediation not confirmed as of this date; the contractor's response follows" is a sourced fact with a reply, and that is the only form a hub publishes. Officials' names are ingested only as they appear in the public source, never enriched, never made into a profile. And the operator has a written duty to suspend a campaign that endangers people, which no setting can remove. None of this is legal advice; each country's defamation, data and protection law is a question for the operator's counsel, recorded in the pack.

One page on which the money and the building meet, with a person who can be held to each: that is what Trustwork proposes to build, from parts the industry already needs.

Open data about money is not evidence about buildings. One page, every source named, is.

A mason without a certificate and a town without a budget-literate public are two faces of one gap: the skill and the money are real, and the record and the understanding are missing. This chapter is about closing that gap from both sides, so that the people who build can prove what they know, and the people who pay can read what they are paying for. It takes what the world has learned about training self-builders, recognising informal skill, building safely after disasters and naming a culture of building, and says what Trustwork's learning, events and magazine become in the civic engine. The civic parts are a direction of this book, not yet in the engine's designs.

The skill is real; the record is missing¶

Construction is one of the sectors with the most informal work, and the International Labour Organization's work on the recognition of prior learning shows what that means for one person. In Jordan, construction workers, most of them Syrian refugees and Jordanians, were assessed against industry standards and received certificates that formally acknowledged what they already knew; one programme document counts 2,600 of them. Kenya finished a national policy on recognising prior learning after a pilot and planned to roll it out to trades including carpenters, masons and plumbers. Malawi piloted it and had assessed and certified more than five hundred artisans by August 2026. The ILO's office in Sri Lanka observed in 2017 that migrant construction workers return home with transferable skills and no certificate, and asked for an accessible, transparent assessment system. The pattern is the same in every case: the skill exists, the record does not, and the missing record is what keeps the worker informal, uninsured and underpaid.

The engine's answer exists in design and as a demo. A certificate on a hub is a public record anyone can check by its number, and it says plainly what it means: this person completed this course and passed its assessment, not a state qualification or a licence. The civic engine adds the next step, not yet designed in the engine: a recognition pathway, in which an assessor accredited by a training authority or a professional body, recorded with a source, assesses a worker's prior learning on the hub, and the hub issues a certificate that names the assessor and the standard. The certificate goes into the worker's reputation passport, a signed, revocable, holder-controlled credential in the W3C Verifiable Credentials format, a web standard since 2019 and in its 2.0 version since May 2025, compatible with Open Badges and mappable to Europe's learning model, so that it travels with the worker between employers, hubs and borders and cannot be lost or forged. No ministry or professional body recognises Trustwork certificates today; the book promises the standard, not the recognition.

How self-building changed where it changed¶

The programmes that changed how poor families build did not start from inspection. They started from advice, small credit and respected local people. A cement manufacturer's programme in Mexico and Latin America has, since 1998, combined materials on credit, technical advice and a savings schedule for families building incrementally, delivered through more than two hundred promoters from the communities themselves, most of them women; the company reports more than 650,000 families served by the end of 2022, a figure that is its own. An engineering non-profit working in Nepal after the 2015 earthquakes reports more than 24,000 houses built or strengthened, more than 52,000 homeowners who came to its technical support centres, more than 8,000 government engineers and construction professionals trained, and a public campaign of forum theatre and films that reached hundreds of thousands; the government approved and published its confined-masonry house design in 2017. Those figures are the organisation's own, and this book cites no independent evaluation of safety outcomes. Nepal's reconstruction itself was homeowner-driven by law: owners chose from approved designs, managed their own builders, and received a grant of 300,000 rupees in three tranches, released against inspected construction stages by engineers posted to municipalities; the reconstruction authority reported ninety-two per cent of private housing rebuilt by the end of its term in 2021.

Read together, the three say one thing. Culture travelled through people who were paid and respected for carrying it, a promoter, a mason-trainer, a municipal engineer, and it was tied to money released against evidence. That is the shape of Trustwork's plan-to-people journey: a self-builder gets a real plan, consultations with experts who earn income and reputation for giving them, and staged acceptance of each stage with a short act and photographs. The missing piece in the places the engine is needed most is the respected local master, and the engine gives that person a role, a record and a living: the mentor whose consultations are counted by outcome and whose students' certificates carry their name. Those lessons stay free and arrive on the plan step itself, for the reasons chapter 2 gives.

Where buildings fall¶

The collapses counted in Nigeria, and the causes the regulators name every time, appear in chapter 11. Investigations there often end in "unknown factors", and no audited breakdown of causes exists; each named cause is a record that was never made. The guild that keeps the count is a professional body trying to teach a society not to let its buildings fall. A hub in such a place is a learning institution before it is a marketplace: courses on stage certification and soil testing taught by the engineers who investigate the collapses, certificates for the workers who take them, a magazine that reports every collapse with sources, and execution records that make the missing record ordinary.

A culture of building, with a name¶

"Culture of building" risks sounding like a slogan, and it has a definition. In January 2018 the European ministers of culture adopted the Davos Declaration, which treats building as a cultural act and the whole built environment, infrastructure and public space included, as culture. In 2021 the Swiss Federal Office of Culture published a quality system that makes the idea measurable: eight criteria, each with an assessment questionnaire, and a quality statement required on all eight. The criteria are governance, functionality, environment, economy, diversity, context, sense of place and beauty. Social, cultural and emotional qualities stand on an equal footing with the technical, environmental and economic ones. The word for this is Baukultur, and the book keeps the German word because no English one carries the same weight.

Two things make this more than a European curiosity. The first criterion is governance: how a place is decided, by whom, and how openly. That ties the culture of building directly to the loop of public trust: a participatory project decided in public, costed by a named professional, built with evidence and confirmed by residents scores on governance before a brick is laid. And the eight criteria give a hub's magazine, events and courses an existing vocabulary to organise around, and a public-works page an optional short statement on each criterion, written by the proposers and the professionals who costed it, never scored. The system is European in origin, and a hub adopts it only if its operator chooses to: a tool to adapt, never a standard to impose.

Budget literacy and the youngest voters¶

A public that understands its budget is the precondition of the civic loop. The instruments are modest and proven. A citizens' budget is the budget in plain language, and the Open Budget Survey has scored its publication for years. Budget schools, short courses for residents and journalists on reading a municipal budget, are run by budget groups and universities in many countries; the practice is well attested and its outcomes uncounted, so the book describes it and does not measure it. The cheapest civic education there is turns out to be school and youth participatory budgeting: Paris lets residents vote from the age of seven; New York from eleven, regardless of immigration status; Boston has run a youth budget of a million dollars a year since 2014; Portugal's schools budget has run since 2017; a Council of Europe school programme in two Georgian cities reached more than nine hundred students, teachers and administrators. A twelve-year-old who has argued for a school yard, seen it costed, voted, and watched it be built, or not, has learned what a budget is better than any lesson could teach.

The engine's first ring is itself a literacy device: a reader who sees a named source and a visible correction every week learns what to expect from news, and what to ask of a budget.

What learning, events and the magazine become¶

The civic engine does not add a school to the hub. It gives the hub's existing learning, events and magazine a public audience and a public subject.

  • Courses for the people who confirm

    How to read a bill of quantities. What a stalled work looks like. How to photograph for evidence. How to witness a hidden-works acceptance. Short, practical, with a certificate checkable by number, for the members of a project committee, for residents who observe, for journalists.

  • Courses for the people who build

    Stage certification, soil testing, the local code's winter concreting, installing a manufacturer's system, taught by the manufacturer and labelled as such. How to bid on the national procurement portal, for the small contractor who has never won public work; the supplier bid desk is designed for exactly this.

  • Recognition of prior learning

    Not yet designed, and recognised by nobody yet; the page says so until it is.

  • Events that are hearings

    A public hearing on a budget is an event with a programme and a record; a visit to a half-built public work is a site visit. Registration, materials and attendance certificates work as they do for any event.

  • The magazine follows the money

    One sourced story per issue that follows a budget line into a building. A "what was promised, what was built" edition at the end of each participatory cycle, built from the public-works records, with the institution's reply. Every number sourced; every correction an edition.

  • Youth cycles on the same module

    A school's participatory budget runs on the same module with small sums, so that the first budget a person ever votes on is one whose result they can see built.

What stays human¶

Learning is the part of the engine where agents help least and people matter most, and the design keeps it that way. An agent can suggest a course from a plan step, draft a quiz from a standard, remind a learner, and tell a contractor which certificate a tender requires. It cannot assess prior learning; an accredited person does, under their name. It cannot teach a mason to feel when a mix is wrong; a master does, on site, and the hub gives the master a record. It cannot decide whether a course deserves accreditation; a professional body does, and the hub shows the decision with its source. And it cannot make a society want to build well. What a hub can do is make the people who already build well visible, paid and copied, and put the budget in front of residents in words they can read, early enough to argue. A society that knows how to build is one that has been let into the conversation about what gets built, and has learned, from its own projects, what the answers cost.

Teach the craft to the hands that build, and the budget to the people who pay.

15Reputation for everyone, and the lines we never cross

Reputation is the reason the engine exists and the easiest thing to get wrong. A star rating anyone can buy, inflate or weaponise is worse than none. This chapter sets out how Trustwork builds reputation from evidence for participants, how it records the delivery of public institutions without ever rating an official, and then the lines it never crosses. The civic extensions are a direction of this book, not yet designed in the repository; the rules are not.

Why stars fail¶

Two failures are well documented. The first is inflation. A study of five online marketplaces in a 2022 marketing journal found average ratings rising substantially over the years and, where the data allowed the test, much of the rise coming from raters applying looser standards rather than from better service. The mechanism is human: raters do not want to harm the person they are rating, and since the capacity to harm is what makes a rating informative, public ratings drift towards uselessness. Where feedback was private, raters stayed candid. The second failure is purchase. Fake reviews became a market large enough that the United States Federal Trade Commission banned them by rule from 21 October 2024, with civil penalties of up to 53,088 dollars per violation (the 2025 inflation-adjusted figure), model-generated reviews and bought social-media indicators included. For a reference hub in the United States, "no fakes" is law, not only principle.

Official registers fail in their own ways. The United States federal past-performance system requires an assessment of every contract above a threshold; an inspector general's review found eighty-four per cent of assessments short of the requirements for ratings or narratives, and the federal bid-protest forum will not hear a direct challenge to a rating. A performance record without narrative evidence and an appeal route degrades into unused percentages. And a register that cannot see common ownership, as the Philippine licensing board found in 2025 when it revoked the licences of nine related contractors, is a captured register. Debarment lists, by contrast, work as facts: the development banks' public lists, bound since 2010 by an agreement that a debarment by one bank is enforced by the others, are reliable, binary and slow. Trustwork shows a debarment as what it is, a sourced fact about a company with a date, a ground and a source, never as its own judgement.

Reputation from evidence¶

The engine's rule is that a claim becomes a fact only through evidence and a recorded decision. "Delivered four hundred square metres of panels on the twelfth" is a claim. Delivery notes, acceptance acts, dated photographs and test reports are evidence, each with its source, time and hash. Confirmation is a recorded decision by the counterparty, or by a trust reviewer in a dispute; importing a document is not confirmation. A dispute excludes the observation until it is resolved, and nothing is rewritten.

From such facts the home hub computes a reputation snapshot under a published method. It reports dimensions separately, never as one number: timing, quality, payment discipline, cooperation and subjective reviews, each with the number of observations it rests on. It names its method version, counts what was excluded and why, shows missing data as unknown rather than zero, and says how to appeal. Timing is measured against the plan as it was agreed at the time, so a client's change of scope is never counted as a supplier's delay. Until a method with a minimum sample has been released, a profile says "not enough data", and nothing is ranked publicly. Weights, windows and minimum samples are to be decided.

Nothing can buy any of it. No payment, plan, sponsorship, commission or staff action changes a score, a badge, a credential or a position; staff act only with evidence or through a reviewed method release, and every such act is attributed in the explanation. Review rings and confirmation swaps open a case for a person, never an automatic sanction. And authority stays home: only the subject's home node computes, and no other node may recompute or build a cross-node ranking.

A toolkit for everyone¶

Reputation is not the platform watching people. Every participant builds and confirms their own, with tools. Credentials: licences, insurance and certificates recorded as "checked on this date by this hub", with the documents never shown. A portfolio of completed work linked to execution records and marked "client confirmed" only when the client confirmed it, otherwise "self-reported", shown only with the project owner's consent and never with an address. Counterparty confirmations tied to a real agreement, task or consultation, asked once with one reminder and never with an incentive. Contributions counted by outcome, never by volume. Coaching from the person's own agent, limited to legitimate actions: it will tell you two finished jobs are waiting for confirmation; it will never suggest a review swap. And the reputation passport: a portable bundle of signed, revocable credentials issued by the home hub, which the participant owns, chooses from and shows anywhere, inside or outside Trustwork. A worker who moves to another hub or country carries their record; the receiving hub shows it as "verified by that hub" and never re-issues it.

Disputes and appeals are part of the toolkit. A participant can dispute an observation with evidence, answer a fair negative review in public, and appeal a decision to a different person than the one who decided; a fair negative observation can be answered and never deleted. Reviews are tied to a real interaction and do not travel between hubs, because defamation law differs by country and a review belongs with its evidence.

Institutions: a delivery record, not a rating¶

Public bodies are not sellers, and a rating of a ministry is a political instrument. What the evidence supports, and what the civic engine is designed for, is an institution's delivery record: did the project the public voted for get a budget line, a tender, a contract, a build and an acceptance, on the promised date, at the promised cost, and was the documentation published? It is built only from public, sourced data and the completions a hub has confirmed (chapter 13). A missing figure is shown as missing. There is no composite score, no ranking of institutions and no league table. The institution has a right of reply on the page, with its own evidence. And the record is a time series that does not reset at an election, so that it belongs to the institution and not to whoever runs it this term. The book uses the words "delivery record" for institutions and reserves "reputation" for participants who opted in.

Officials: signatories, never subjects¶

An official's record is not a Trustwork object. People in public roles appear on a hub only as the signatories of documents that are already public, an award notice, an acceptance act, a budget decision, exactly as the source shows them, never enriched, never gathered into a profile, never used for outreach. A procurement officer who awards a tender to a company that later fails is not a line in anyone's record; the award and the failure are two sourced facts about a company and an institution, each with a date. The engine's discovery design already forbids collecting court or debt records of natural persons and building profiles of people in public roles; the civic engine inherits the rule and adds a test: no public projection may contain a derived score for a natural person.

The lines¶

These are the refusals. They are not settings a hub can change, and a hub that wants them changed is not a Trustwork hub.

  1. No score of residents by the state, ever. A resident's votes, proposals, observations and complaints are never gathered into a record about that resident. The ballot is unlinkable by any role, staff included. Participation is never a signal about a person. The schema has no place to put one, and a test will fail if one appears.

  2. No social credit. The thing we refuse deserves an accurate description. The system most people mean is not one national score of citizens; it is an umbrella, planned from 2014, over a financial credit system, a corporate compliance apparatus with blacklists and joint punishment across agencies, a national list of court-judgment defaulters with real consequences such as travel restrictions, and local pilots that computed personal scores in a few dozen cities; a draft national law published in 2022 remained unenacted as of November 2026, and a 2025 directive set out twenty-three measures towards unified rules and credit repair. Its real faults are the ones we design against: it is state-run and opaque; a consequence crosses domains silently, so that a court debt becomes a travel ban; appeal is weak; and legal sanction is blended with moral labelling.

    Trustwork forbids each: no state computes anything; a fact in commerce never becomes a civic penalty; disputed observations never count; appeals go to a different person; every snapshot explains itself; nobody is labelled, only facts are shown. The European Union has banned AI social scoring since 2 February 2025; Trustwork banned public scores of people before any law required it, and keeps the rule in every country.

  3. No surveillance. No employee productivity dashboard, no employer view of learning, no per-person activity, sentiment or engagement measure for staff or members; cohorts under ten are suppressed; no open-tracking pixels; no "log in as user". An observation carries the location of the asset and never of the observer.
  4. No pay-to-rank. No featured placement tied to money, no sponsored position in matching or recommendations, no staff control that sets a score. Sponsorship, where a hub allows it at all, is labelled and kept out of ranking.
  5. No ranking of people in public office. Institutions are measured by delivery; people by their own consented record. There is no page for an official, no index of mayors, no score of inspectors. A person in a public role who is also a participant, an engineer who inspects and also consults, has one profile, their own, built from the roles they chose.
  6. No cross-domain consequence by default. A fact about a company's late payment on a private job does not bar it from a public tender by any act of the engine; the procuring entity decides under its own law, with the record in front of it. A fact in one hub does not become a sanction in another; authority stays home.
  7. Residents are never the object of a record. Only works, contracts, budgets and the companies and institutions that deliver them are. The engine's discovery design already refuses to collect private individuals as consumers or homeowners; the civic engine extends the refusal to residents as voters, proposers and observers.

One line looks forward. The European Union's high-risk obligations for stand-alone AI systems in areas such as access to essential public services are now due in December 2027, after a 2026 amendment moved the dates. An agent that ranks bidders for a public tender, or a reputation snapshot used in awarding public work, may fall under those rules where the law applies. The engine's design, explanation on every snapshot, a human decision at every award, logging and appeal routes, is written as a conformity story now, before the date. Whether and how the rules apply in any country is a question for counsel.

Institutions are measured by what they deliver. People are measured by nobody but themselves.

Neutral between parties, never neutral about evidence¶

A hub serves evidence, not parties. The same methods apply to every administration, and the delivery record is a time series that does not reset at an election, so a new government inherits its predecessor's stalled works on the page and gets credit for finishing them. A hub publishes no endorsements and no rankings of politicians, observes electoral silence periods where the law requires, and works with any lawful government, municipality or civic body under the same rules. Neutrality does not mean silence about facts: an acceptance act that records defects is published whether or not it embarrasses someone, and the operator accepts that at the launch gate. Neutrality means that the embarrassment is distributed by the evidence and not by the operator.

Protecting the people who look¶

Monitoring can get people hurt; chapter 13 sets out how a hub protects its observers. The country pack says, with a source, how well the law protects whistle-blowers, and the design tightens where it does not. A hub also protects the people it writes about: a contractor or an institution can attach a reply and evidence to any page, a dispute freezes the disputed observation from public display until it is resolved, and a correction travels to every peer as a new revision.

When a hub must refuse¶

Some places and some requests close the door. A hub can never be run from or for a restricted place; the engine refuses at every entry point and the mesh at the handshake. Beyond sanctions, a civic hub adds launch gates of its own: a lawful public body or civic organisation as operator; any vote held by a neutral keeper; a ballot no role can link to a person; no resident record in the schema; acceptance that evidence is published whether or not it flatters. One more gate applies: the civic ring switches on only where the territory's current civic-space rating and open-government standing, recorded in the country pack with date and source, meet a threshold to be set by a recorded decision, re-checked every year (chapter 20). Where civic space is closed, a civic hub is not offered, however welcome an industry hub might be, because a loop of public trust cannot run where the public cannot speak. A government that asks a hub for its voters' identities, for a list of who observed what, for the removal of an acceptance act that records defects, or for a ranking of residents by their civic conduct, is asking the hub to stop being one. The operator refuses; under compulsion the hub suspends, read-only with a banner; and if it cannot continue it closes the way every hub closes: participants notified, their data exported to them, the public record handed to a successor hub where one exists, tombstones sent to every peer. The judgement is made in the open, country by country, in chapter 20.

Everything in this chapter comes down to one asymmetry, and the book asks every hub operator to remember it. The powerful are measured by what they deliver, in public, with sources, and they may reply. The rest of us are measured by nobody, and we may, if we choose, build a record of our own work that belongs to us and that nobody can buy.

Reputation cannot be bought. It also cannot be imposed.

Part V · The system as it will be

16The experience, person by person

This is the system as it will be, told from the chair of each person who uses it: what they come for, what their one agent does for them under mandates they can read, and what stays theirs to decide. It is written as the product, not as a status list; chapter 18 says which of this exists. Where the civic ring or the financing ring appears, it is a direction of this book, not yet built, and said to be.

One door, one agent, one record¶

Everyone arrives the same way: one account, one lifelong identity, one representative agent that acts only under mandates confirmed on the hub's own form, never from a sentence typed in a chat. A person is often several things at once, a site engineer who writes articles, mentors newcomers and renovates their own flat, so "homeowner" or "professional" is a lens for suggestions, never a role that grants anything. Rights come from participation grants, company authority, staff roles and mandates, and from nothing else. What holds for everyone is listed at the end; here are the people.

The people who build¶

  • The self-builder

    Comes for a real plan for the house, the room or the roof they are about to build with their own money: phases, trades, permits, budget ranges with their basis, risks and decisions, in plain language; then the people the plan needs; later, borrowing and insuring stage by stage; and after completion a house record, the family's own, that continues through every repair and passes with the home to a buyer or an heir.

    The agent drafts the plan from a conversation and the country pack, proposes consultations and tasks, reminds them before a hidden work is covered, compiles the build file when they choose to show it to a lender, and at the end proposes what to share with the next family.

    Theirs to decide: approving every plan version, accepting every stage, choosing who sees which item of the plan, whether to borrow at all and from whom, and whether anything of the project is ever shared. The agent never approves, never accepts and never shares.

  • The worker

    Comes for a record of what they can do that belongs to them and not to an employer: a profile, finished work confirmed by the client, certificates checkable by number and, in time, a passport that travels to the next hub. For many it is the first rung of formality.

    The agent keeps the profile accurate, asks the client once for a confirmation and reminds once more, never with an incentive, and finds the course or the assessment of prior learning that leads to the next trade.

    Theirs to decide: what is published, with recorded and revocable consent; which jobs to answer; whether to take the assessment. Nobody is made visible against their will.

  • The professional

    Comes for the engineer's, architect's or estimator's place in a project: the drawings reviewed, the hidden work measured, the cost range given before a vote, the licence shown as checked on a date by the hub. And for being found by the client who needs them, for reasons the matching explains.

    The agent answers requests that fit, drafts proposals that are frozen the moment they are sent, keeps the calendar, prepares the feasibility note's template with the method the professional chose, and watches licence and insurance expiry.

    Theirs to decide: every professional judgement, under their own name and licence. The engine labels what the professional wrote as a professional estimate, different in appearance from a resident's idea and from any quote.

  • The expert and mentor

    Comes for consultations that pay and count: an advice session, a review of quotes, a site visit, a mentoring series for a newcomer.

    The agent keeps a versioned offering with the full price and the hub's commission shown before booking, schedules within window capacity, and brings the deliverable to the client for acceptance.

    Theirs to decide: what advice to give, and to whom. The hub's commission is the same for every expert in a category and never changes who is recommended.

  • The contractor

    Comes for work found fairly, quotes that cannot be undercut after the hour, an agreement both sides can prove identical, stage acceptances that protect them as much as the client, and a reputation from accepted work that explains itself and cannot be bought.

    The agent, or the company's one standing representative with a named accountable person, lays out the stages from the agreement, records work with dated, placed photographs, drafts the pay application against confirmed work records and the agreement's digest, proposes a remediation plan when a defect is recorded, and keeps the company's page claimed and current.

    Theirs to decide: the price, the bid, the remediation, and the public response to a fair negative observation, which they may dispute and never remove. Timing reputation measures them against the plan as it was agreed at the time, so a client's change is never their delay.

  • The supplier and manufacturer

    Comes for requests for quotes answered with frozen prices, a catalogue with immutable versions, confirmed deliveries, and the place on a plan step where their knowledge helps a builder choose and install correctly, because it fits, never because they paid.

    The agent watches the needs board for what the company sells, drafts quotes within mandates, records deliveries with evidence, and keeps installation guides and training labelled as the manufacturer's own.

    Theirs to decide: every quote and every promise. They can never buy a recommendation, a ranking or a position; a hub's open statistics will show how their systems perform by stage and defect category, and that is the only advertising the engine runs.

The people who commission with common money¶

  • The resident

    Comes for the street that floods, the stair that is missing, the roof that leaks: an idea, a place to discuss it, a costed note before the vote, and afterwards the record of what their vote became, with the budget line, the tender, the contractor, the photographs and the acceptance. The vote itself belongs to the municipality or a neutral keeper; the hub keeps the record around it.

    The agent knows from the country pack when the calendar opens and what right a resident is exercising when they attach a photograph, drafts the idea in their words, and offers to attach an observation to a public work's record, as "received" until the institution answers.

    Theirs to decide: whether to propose, whether to attend, how to vote, whether to observe, and whether their name appears at all. A resident is never the object of a record, and observations are pseudonymous by default.

  • The municipal official

    Comes for one record of what was promised, budgeted, contracted, built and accepted that does not reset at an election, with assemblies and committees configured from the law and the right of reply.

    The agent, the municipality's representative with a named accountable person, imports the approved budget's capital lines and links each to its project page, drafts replies to observations from the record, and prepares the yearly delivery report for a person to sign.

    Theirs to decide: the budget, the award, the reply, the payment through the municipality's own channels when the hub signals that a stage is accepted. Officials appear only as signatories of documents that are already public; the engine never rates an official as a person, and it never runs the vote.

  • The procurement officer

    Comes for a tender whose quotes are frozen once sent and sealed until the deadline, so that nobody undercuts late; approval routes where the requester never decides; and a private supplier base that nothing leaks from.

    The agent drafts the request from the engineer's note, checks the compliance matrix with citations, and lays out the comparison with stale versions excluded. Where the tender runs in the state's own system, the hub imports the notice as a sourced page and never rewrites it.

    Theirs to decide: the award, in their own session with step-up for money, and never by silence. Approval by expiry does not exist.

  • The auditor

    Comes for a public-works record that holds, side by side, the contractor's statement, the inspector's act and the residents' confirmation compared against a measurement, with a bill of quantities and a payment signal that can be traced to all three. An audit office's citizen-monitor programme finds here the structured place its monitors lacked.

    The agent reads what is public and what the institution shared, and drafts the gap between what the papers say and what the site shows.

    Theirs to decide: the finding. The engine takes none of an auditor's, a court's or a council's decisions; it gives each a record to work from.

The people who finance and insure¶

These three roles are introduced in chapter 10. Each is a reader the project owner adds or a participant with a narrow capability; none of them is Trustwork, and Trustwork is none of them.

  • The independent inspector

    Comes for a place where their act counts as what it is: a third record, separate from the builder's statement and the owner's acceptance, that closes a stage and lets a tranche move. And for a record of their own: acts filed on time, acts later contradicted by a confirmed defect, appeals heard.

    The agent schedules the visit when the builder's evidence arrives, drafts the act from the stage template, and files nothing until the person signs.

    Theirs to decide: what they saw. They read the shared stage and attach an act; they never edit the plan, never accept work and never see a budget the owner has not shared. Their liability is their professional law's, not the platform's.

  • The lender's credit officer

    Comes for a build file they can read in one format whichever hub it comes from: the approved plan, the estimate against the hub's ranges, the permits, the named professionals, the contractor's explained record, the stage gates, and afterwards every closed gate's three records. And for a hub's open statistics on what a stage costs and how long it takes in this region.

    The agent, on the lender's side, is the lender's own affair. On the owner's side, the agent compiles the file for the owner's review and sends it only when the owner adds the lender as a reader.

    Theirs to decide: the credit decision, in the lender's own systems and under its own licence. The engine records "submitted" and "decided" without reasons and composes the disbursement instruction only when a gate is closed; the lender or its escrow provider executes it. The lines of chapter 10 apply, among them that the engine never lends or holds money and that no payment changes what a family is shown.

  • The insurer's underwriter

    Comes for what a warranty provider sees during a build: who built, which stages were inspected by whom, which defects were found and fixed, what the structure is and where it stands, and a resilience rating if one exists. Over time, for defect frequencies by stage and building type from the hub's open statistics.

    The agent on the owner's side attaches the certificate of cover as a credential with an expiry, checked like a licence, on the project and, with consent, on the contractor's passport.

    Theirs to decide: the premium and the terms. The hub never ranks insurers or sells a policy (chapter 10).

The people who tell, teach and gather¶

  • The editor and the journalist

    Comes for a desk where every piece names its sources, corrections are new versions with a visible note, a second editor reviews, and the weekly magazine can follow one budget line into one building.

    The agent gathers sourced items, drafts summaries labelled as such, checks the source permissions before publication, and never publishes alone; a disclosed piece is released only under a named responsible person.

    Theirs to decide: what to publish, what to correct, and the sentence that explains why the drain matters. The by-line is a person's, and so is the correction.

  • The teacher and the training centre

    Comes for short practical courses with certificates checkable by number; the course for committee members on reading a bill of quantities and witnessing a hidden work; the course for a mason on the next trade; and, where an accredited body takes part, the assessment of prior learning recorded once and portably.

    The agent drafts course outlines from shared project knowledge, schedules assessments, issues the certificate the teacher approved, and keeps the accreditation shown only with its recorded source.

    Theirs to decide: who passed. A platform certificate is not a state qualification or licence, and the engine says so; a manufacturer's course is labelled as the manufacturer's.

  • The event organiser

    Comes for a conference, a site visit, a trade evening or a public hearing with registration, tickets through a licensed provider, check-in and partner meetings.

    The agent drafts the programme, handles registration within the mandate, and proposes partner meetings that both sides opt into.

    Theirs to decide: the programme, the price and who speaks. Attendance is counted as totals; nobody learns who sat where.

The people who run the hub¶

  • The hub operator

    Comes for running Trustwork for a country, a region or a city as a small company that takes legal responsibility, launches a team of operator agents across the twenty-three functions of a hub operating company, and adds people as the hub grows.

    The agents of the operating company work under operator grants, each with a named accountable person: the chief of staff prepares the daily brief, the editorial roles draft, support and trust-and-safety roles triage, finance drafts the monthly close with the operator's own accountant, compliance watches the gates. None of them passes a launch gate, enables a real provider, sets a score or reads private data.

    Theirs to decide: the country pack's facts with their sources, commissions as versioned and disclosed settings, the legal documents counsel approved, and the refusal to build what the charter forbids, even when a government asks.

What holds for everyone¶

What holds for everyone are the values of chapter 1: one lifelong identity; one agent under mandates the person can read, change and revoke, with the stop control one click away; private stays private; nothing for sale, no paid leads, and commissions that never change who is shown; sourced, claimed and verified always distinct; every automated limit explained and appealable to a different person; no resident ever scored; restricted places never served.

The agent prepares. The person decides. The record remembers.

17Scenes from the hubs

Ten short scenes from the places chapter 20 looks at, each told as the system will run it. The year of a Dominican drain was told in chapter 4, and a school roof from budget line to signature in chapter 13; these are the others. The people are roles, never persons; the programmes, laws and figures are the countries' own, with their sources in chapter 28; no municipality, lender, insurer or agency has agreed to anything. San Diego: a second home on the lot, and a lender's draws¶

A homeowner in a neighbourhood east of the city centre wants to build an accessory dwelling unit behind the house. The reference hub in San Diego, in English first and Spanish second, gives their agent the country pack's facts: the city's permit process, the state licence board where a contractor's licence is checked on a date, the tiered limits the council adopted for such units in 2025. The plan comes back as phases with permits and inspections as items, a budget range with its basis, and the decisions ahead; the owner approves it. Three licensed contractors answer the task request; the quotes are frozen the moment they are sent, and the agreement is one both sides can prove identical. The owner's lender offers a construction loan that converts to a mortgage, with an appraisal "as completed", inspections before each draw and a completion report. The owner adds the lender as a reader of the build file. At every stage the contractor's record, the city inspector's result and the owner's acceptance are three records; the lender's own draw inspector, a participant with a narrow mandate, adds a fourth. When a gate closes, the engine composes the instruction and the lender's escrow provider releases the draw. Trustwork touched no money and recommended no lender; it was the record the lender already wanted and the owner already needed.

San Diego: the works of the city¶

The same hub carries a read-only layer the city did not have to build. San Diego publishes more than a hundred datasets daily, from every service request to a budget dashboard back to 2021. The hub's data steward, under the operator's grant, imports the capital improvement programme as sourced pages, each with its budget line, its contract where the procurement data names one, and the contractor's sourced page beside it. A paving crew's foreman, a participant, attaches dated photographs of a base course before it is covered; a city inspector, invited as a reference institution, confirms or does not. Nothing is voted. Nothing is scored. A resident who reports a pothole through the city's own channel sees, for the first time, the project page where that street's resurfacing sits, with the gap between the dates on paper and the state of the asphalt. The arc places this layer first, before any ballot.

A Peruvian district: a house on its own lot, stage by stage¶

Peru's housing bond and its registered technical entities (chapter 10) standardised the role; this scene adds the record. The lender is a role, not a named institution.

One plot, one bond, one loan, six gates
  1. March
    the family · a district on the northern coast

    Their lot is registered and free of liens, the programme's first condition. Their agent checks the country pack for the current call and the register of technical entities, and drafts a two-room house with a bathroom, to be extended later. The family changes the kitchen and approves the version.

  2. April
    the registered technical entity · an engineer and a builder

    The entity has a verified page; its registration with the housing fund is a credential checked on a date. It signs the contract with the family, an agreement both sides can prove identical, and attaches the plans and the itemised budget. The hub's template for a house of this type suggests six gates; the entity adjusts them and the family approves.

  3. May
    a housing microfinance lender · a reader

    The bond covers the core of the house; for the rest the family wants a small construction loan. They add the lender as a reader of the build file: the approved plan, the budget against the hub's ranges, the plot's check, the entity's credential and record, the gates. The lender decides in its own systems; the hub records "submitted" and "decided" and nothing of why.

  4. June
    the builder, an independent inspector and the family · the foundation

    Before the floor slab covers the footings, the builder records them with dated, placed photographs against the drawing. A licensed civil engineer from the district, a participant with the capability to inspect and no other, measures and signs an act. The family accepts. Three records by three persons close the first gate, and the engine composes an instruction for the lender's first tranche, which the lender's licensed provider releases. The bond moves under the programme's own rules, outside Trustwork, as it always did.

  5. July
    the inspector · the roof slab

    The reinforcement does not match the drawing in one bay. The inspector records a defect; the builder's remediation is recorded and, a week later, accepted. The gate stays open until it is. The defect stays on the record afterwards, and so does its remedy.

    the family · the finished house

    The last gate closes within the entity's 120 days. The completion record closes the file's version; the lender's last tranche moves; the entity's record shows one house delivered on time with one defect remedied; the inspector's record shows six acts, none contradicted. The family's agent asks whether they would like to contribute the project's cost and duration, and nothing else, to the hub's statistics for houses of this type in this region. They say yes. Next year's family will see a range that includes theirs, and no name.

A Mexican city: a self-builder with a workers' fund credit¶

Mexico's housing fund lets a worker use their credit to build on land they own, on condition the land is regularised, with the money released in stages as the work advances, as the press describes it. A worker in a city in the central highlands has a regularised lot and a master builder they trust. Their agent drafts the plan in Spanish from the hub's template for a house of this type, lists the permits the municipality requires, and names the stages the fund's releases will follow. The master builder is a participant with a profile and a portfolio, three houses confirmed by their owners, and no licence to show; the hub says so plainly, and shows beside it the course completed at a chamber's training institute, checkable by number. The fund is not on the hub; the owner shares the build file with it the way Peru's family did, by adding a reader, and if the fund pays by its own transfer the file is still the record of every stage. The manufacturer appears on the plan steps where its knowledge helps, labelled as its own.

Outside Nairobi: a room, a roof and a sequence of small loans¶

In Kenya, where mortgages are few and housing microfinance is deep (chapter 10), a six-year study of housing microfinance there found tens of thousands of households borrowing small, short sums to build a room or a roof. A family on the edge of the city builds that way. Their plan has three phases that may take years: a second room, a proper roof, a water connection. A mason accredited by the national construction authority takes the first task, the accreditation on the mason's profile, checked on a date. A housing microfinance lender, added as a reader, finances the room as one short loan tied to that stage; the roof will be another loan, another stage, another gate. The hub's open statistics show the usual cost of such a room in this county, as useful to the family as to the lender. And when a fourteen-storey building fell in the city at the start of 2026 and engineers asked for a national register of who designed, built and approved every building, the hub's building page was already that shape. Kenya is on the FATF list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring (as reported), which licensed payment providers weigh; until providers are connected, the engine records obligations and evidence while money moves outside it.

A Philippine province: flood control, on paper and on the ground¶

After the flood-control scandal of 2025 (chapter 11), a presidential portal opened in August 2025 to show every project's location, contractor, cost and completion date. A hub for one province takes that public list and builds a sourced page for every dike, wall and pumping station in its territory, and for every contractor named, from the licensing board's register, each labelled from its source. The province's resident auditor from the national audit commission, whose citizen participatory audit earns the country a full score for participation at the audit stage, is invited as a reference institution. Residents attach evidence: a photograph of the river bank where a wall is recorded as complete; a photograph of the wall where it exists. The observations are "received" until the public works office answers from the record. Evidence first, judgement later: the hub publishes the gap between the date on paper and the state on the ground, and leaves the finding to the auditor and the courts.

Sekondi-Takoradi: a market roof, disclosed before anyone asked¶

The metropolitan assembly on Ghana's western coast joined the infrastructure transparency initiative in 2019 and persuaded its neighbours to publish on its disclosure portal; by 2026 all fourteen districts of the region take part in independent review. A Ghana hub run by a regional operator builds on that work rather than replacing it. The assembly's common-fund works, a new roof over a market among them, are imported as sourced project pages from the disclosure portal, each carrying the forty data points the initiative asks for and the independent reviewer's findings. The hub adds what the portal does not hold: the contractor's claimed page, the stages with the foreman's photographs of the trusses before the sheeting, the assembly engineer's act, and a place for the market women's association to confirm that the roof is there and the stalls stay dry. The hub's weekly magazine publishes the review in the initiative's own format, sourced and dated, with the project pages linked and no opinion added. The country's national budget transparency score fell by twenty-four points in the latest survey; the region's disclosure did not.

Medellín: a contractor's page, from sourced to verified¶

Colombia's national procurement system publishes every contract daily in the open contracting standard, and its law gives citizen oversight committees the right to watch any public contract. The hub for the city imports the works of two comunas funded through the city's participatory budget, which has run since 2004, and the contracts behind them. A roofing contractor finds its own page on the hub, built from the procurement data months ago and labelled as sourced. Its owner claims it; the claim is verified against the company register and the engineers' licensing council, and the page changes appearance, because sourced, claimed and verified always look different. Now the company links its execution records to its public works: baselines, stages, the bill of quantities. A veeduría from the comuna, with the legal standing the country pack records, attaches its observations to one stage, compared against the city engineer's measurement. A year later the company's public-works record shows four contracts, three on time against the agreed baseline, one with a client-caused change that the record names as such, and two defects remedied; its reputation snapshot explains all of it with its method version and sample count and no score. Nobody in the comuna is the object of any record.

Kigali: a training centre, and institutions measured by delivery¶

A training centre in the city puts its short courses on the Rwanda hub, run by a training body: masonry, formwork, plumbing. Certificates are checkable by number and say what they are, a platform certificate and not a state qualification, until the national vocational training board, taking part on the hub, assesses prior learning and the hub records that assessment with its source. The national building-permit system, Kubaka, launched in 2025, becomes a sourced page per permit; the public procurement authority's daily open data becomes a sourced page per tender. The civic ring here is deliberately narrow, and the hub says so in every language it serves: the districts' public performance contracts on public works are mirrored as commitments with execution evidence, so that an institution's delivery record can be read by anyone; no resident has a reputation, no vote runs on the hub, and no official is scored as a person. Every public counterpart passes the sanctions engine before a page is published.

An Armenian community: a capital plan put out for comment¶

Armenia publishes every draft legal act for public comment, with the authority's answer to each comment, on a site its residents already use. An enlarged community outside the capital, with an elected council and a budget of its own, lets the hub's civic ring import its capital budget and procurement as sourced pages and run next year's works list through the same kind of comment round: each proposed work with its cost range from a named engineer, each comment answered in public by the council's representative, an agent with a named accountable person. The council decides under its own law, and if a vote is held it is the council's or the community's, held outside the hub with identity proven outside it. Then the works are built with staged evidence, and the community's delivery record shows, at the end of the year, what was listed, what was commented, what was decided and what stands.

What the scenes share¶

In every scene the hub imported what was already public before it asked anyone for anything. In every scene the stage was closed by records from different persons, and money, where it moved, moved outside Trustwork through a licensed provider or a public body's own channels. In every scene the institution and the company gained a record and no resident did. And in every scene the hub did not do the thing a reader might expect a platform to do: it did not run the vote, did not lend, did not rank, did not score, and did not decide. It kept the record, and the record was enough.

Different countries, different laws, one habit: import what is public, record what is done, and let people decide.

The kinds of place that come next: a province with a backlog of paralysed works, under an audit office's citizen-monitor frame and with a municipal association as operator; a city with a participatory budget of twenty years' standing, a university and oversight committees with legal standing; a region whose infrastructure disclosures already flow to an independent reviewer; a county that pays contractors only after residents approve. Chapter 20 says where each of these conditions exists today, and nothing is promised to any of them. Recognition as a digital public good, or the published list of what was missing. Certificates mapped to recognition of prior learning in at least one country, with a national training authority as the assessor. Each hub's open statistics, cost ranges, completion times and defect rates by stage and building type, consented, aggregated and never exposing anyone. The first insurer pricing from evidence. The hub operating company's functions staffed by agents and people in at least five hubs. An annual delivery report for each participating municipality, written from records, with the municipality's reply beside it. Baukultur statements on public-works pages where an operator adopts the eight criteria.

The rings. The civic ring in several countries; the financing ring in a few; the learning ring recognised outside the hub for the first time.

Year four, 2030: a federation that funds itself¶

The world. The United Nations' Global Digital Compact, adopted in September 2024, set 2030 for its commitment to invest in digital public goods and infrastructure, and the year will review it. Europe's public authorities must bring their older high-risk systems into compliance by August 2030. AI agents in public procurement work under conformity rules. The first generation of self-builders who learned on hubs finish their houses.

Trustwork. The operators' association financially independent of any one funder, from membership fees, services and grants, holding the charter and advising on the use of the name, which remains Dmitry Parshkov's mark; Contribuda as maintainer (chapter 7). A second, independent implementation of the federation protocol by someone who is not Contribuda. The reputation passport accepted by at least one outside verifier, a lender, an insurer or a licensing board. More kinds of civic loop: a province whose national project list and audit commission are already public; a community that already puts its draft acts out for comment; a borough whose vote is already run by an independent electoral institute, when a willing one and access to its data exist. Income to hubs from commissions as settings, events, courses and services, never from ranking and never from data, and all of it belonging to the hub's owner in its country. A public failure log: projects voted and not built, disputes, revoked certificates, launch gates failed, because the credibility of the loop depends on showing what did not work.

The rings. The federation itself becomes a ring: an engine whose voice belongs to the people who run it.

Year five, 2031: an engine others run, where it is especially needed¶

The world. Post-disaster reconstruction and the upgrading of informal settlements increasingly run through homeowner-driven models with staged digital payments, the shape Nepal gave them after 2015, generalised. National permit systems exposing machine-readable gateways that accept staged field evidence at completion. Trust surveys measuring whether AI in government kept its safeguards.

Trustwork. Hubs in ten or more countries run by local operators under one charter. The civic ring in several municipalities per country where the law allows, and paused where it does not. The build file read by many lenders and insurers, so that a family can borrow and insure its own house stage by stage. Certificates recognised for informal workers in at least two countries. A shared library of project knowledge, with owners' consent, in every hub language, so that a self-builder in Lima or Accra reads how someone else solved the same wall. A federated delivery record that lets a resident see what their vote became, wherever the work was built. And still: no score of residents, ever; no money held; no ranking for pay; no watching of workers.

The rings. All of them, in the places where they are especially needed, run by people who live there.

Where the civic ring waits¶

Where civic space is closed, the construction hub runs alone and the civic ring waits. Chapter 20 names the four places and the yearly review, and says why this is a consequence of our lines, not a judgement of the people who live there.

What would make us wrong¶

An arc is a hypothesis, and a hypothesis should name what would break it. If a single funder's money were to carry more than a part of any year, we would have repeated the mistake that closed the civic organisations of chapter 3. If a government asked for a score of its residents as the price of a pilot, the pilot would not happen. If a lender wanted a number instead of a file, we would offer the file and the statistics and nothing else. If a vote on a hub could be bought, we would have failed to make evidence weigh as much as the ballot. If the operators' association did not form by the fourth operator, growth would wait for it. Each of these is a pause, not a pivot, and each would be recorded with its date and its author in the open, as every decision behind this book is.

The open square¶

Five years from now the three squares of the mark (chapter 4) will be fuller, in more languages and more places. The fourth will still be open. It is not ours to fill.

Five years, five courses. The fourth square stays open.

How we chose¶

Trustwork's launch research ranks ten countries by where a construction hub can start: Peru, the Philippines, Kenya, Ghana, Colombia, the Dominican Republic, Uzbekistan, Nigeria, Mexico and Rwanda. The fourteen countries this book studies are those ten launch countries, the United States, Armenia, Georgia and Azerbaijan. The civic ring asks a different question. A participatory budget, an open tender and a public record of who built what are only useful where residents may use them without being punished for it. So the civic scorecard weighs the openness of civic space as heavily as the need itself: seven criteria, each rated from one to five on the evidence, weighted and summed to a score out of a hundred. Weighting need three times as heavily as openness lifts Peru, Kenya, the Philippines and Nigeria and puts local organisations at risk; we do not take that view. United States, with San Diego and California

The need Cost, exclusion and the age of the stock: California's housing shortage is estimated at about three million units (secondary). The reference collapse is Champlain Towers South in Surfside, Florida, 24 June 2021, 98 dead; Florida answered with milestone inspections, reserves that cannot be waived and inspection records online.
Civic landscape Participatory budgeting is real but small: Vallejo since 2012; New York's council districts since 2011 and a city-wide vote since 2023; Long Beach's youth vote; Los Angeles' zones. San Diego has no city-run participatory budget; a coalition has asked for one since 2012. The city publishes 122 datasets daily, including every 311 request, every development approval and the budget.
Best local idea Open 311 and open permits as a public evidence stream, where field evidence can be tied to a public asset record before any vote; and Surfside's answer, "evidence, not trust" written into law.
Who does this work today The City of San Diego publishes its open data, 122 datasets updated daily; the Center on Policy Initiatives and the Community Budget Alliance have campaigned since 2012 for a participatory budget and publish budget analyses; the cities of Vallejo, New York, Long Beach and Los Angeles run participatory budgets; People Powered rates the digital participation tools.
Risks The politics of "transparency" after the federal retreat; defamation claims against any public record about a contractor or inspector; state privacy law; the equity problem of city participatory budgets (turnout of one to five per cent, affluent participants).
A hub would do first Not a vote. A works of the city layer: capital projects and permits as sourced pages with their contractors' pages and field evidence, city inspectors invited to confirm, a building's inspection record public on its page. A one-district pilot only if the city asks.
Financing angle The regime already exists: construction-to-permanent loans whose lenders inspect before each draw and hold back undrawn funds, with a completion report and lien waivers, and a mortgage data standard its publisher says most regulators require (chapter 10).

Open government can retreat at one level while it stands at another; a hub builds on the level that stands.

Peru

The need About 70 per cent of homes are self-built, by the Housing Ministry's own account (2021). The Comptroller's register counted 2,428 stalled works worth 44.3 billion soles in June 2025, seven in ten at local governments. The Real Plaza Trujillo roof collapsed on 21 February 2025, killing eight (as reported), after the mall had been ordered closed for structural deficiencies in 2023 and reopened in January 2024.
Civic landscape The only country where participatory budgeting is mandatory by national law at every sub-national level: Law 28056 of 2003 binds about 1,900 governments to an annual process with elected vigilance committees. Evaluations call it formal and elite-dominated, and approved projects are not always built. Open contracting data is published daily; the Infobras register records every public work with photographs and takes citizen reports. Civic space Repressed after the protest deaths of 2022 and 2023.
Best local idea Both ends of the loop in law, a duty to vote on local works and a register of every public work. The middle is missing: cost before the vote, evidence after the contract.
Who does this work today The Comptroller General runs Infobras, the register of every public work, and a citizen-monitor programme; the Ministry of Economy and Finance runs the participatory-budget process and publishes its guidance; OECE publishes open contracting data daily; the IEP and PUCP have published the evaluations of the process cited here.
Risks Frequent changes of president since 2018 and a new government since July 2026; rules on non-governmental organisations; extortion against construction crews; a hub used in local electoral fights, because mayors own the budget process.
A hub would do first One province with a long paralysed-works list: every work a sourced page, the gap between the priority list and what was executed shown plainly, the vigilance committee's dated photographs attached; the citizen-monitor programme's published method as the reference frame. No new vote for one budget cycle.
Financing angle Techo Propio's housing bond (a base of 32,100 soles in 2025) works through registered technical entities (chapter 10); a microfinance bank offers a construction loan. The state standardised the role; nobody standardised the record.

Peru has a law for the vote and a register for the work. The hub is the middle.

The Philippines

The need The 2025 flood-control scandal: 9,855 projects worth 545.64 billion pesos between July 2022 and May 2025; about 100 billion to 15 of 2,409 accredited contractors; 421 works found not to exist among about 8,000 inspected. On 1 September 2025 the contractors' licensing board revoked the licences of nine companies under common ownership, citing their owner's sworn testimony to the Senate that they had bid for the same projects (Board Resolution 075, as reported). An independent commission held 32 hearings in its first four months and ended its work at the end of March 2026 (secondary, provisional figures). The housing backlog is contested.
Civic landscape Local governments hold more money since the Mandanas ruling and spend it slowly. Bottom-up Budgeting funded 41,156 projects in 2013 to 2015 and completed 27.8 per cent of those planned before it ended with its government. The audit commission's citizen participatory audit earns a full score for participation at the audit stage. The presidential portal of August 2025 shows each flood-control project's contractor, cost and completion date and drew over 12,000 reports in a month. No open contracting feed yet. Civic space Repressed; libel is criminal; there is no whistle-blower law.
Best local idea Audit as participation, and a national project list turned into a map with a complaint button: evidence first, judgement later.
Who does this work today The Commission on Audit runs the citizen participatory audit; the presidential portal publishes each flood-control project with its contractor, cost and completion date; the Philippine Contractors Accreditation Board licenses and classifies contractors; the Independent Commission for Infrastructure held the hearings of 2025 and 2026 and handed its findings to prosecutors.
Risks Red-tagging and the anti-terror law; online libel used against critics; political dynasties; typhoons; a national identity system to handle with care. Off the FATF list since February 2025.
A hub would do first The flood-control list for one province as sourced pages, the resident auditor and an accredited training centre as reference institutions, residents' evidence, the gap between paper and ground published; then the local investment programme and a small participatory pot.
Financing angle The Pag-IBIG Fund releases construction proceeds in tranches after inspection (secondary). Families abroad pay for homes at home, so remote owner oversight with a local representative matters. The fund's staged release and a resilience rating are what a build file could carry.

Evidence first, judgement later: the Philippines already wrote the rule. The hub keeps the record.

Kenya

The need About 60 per cent of Nairobi's residents live in informal settlements by one scholar's estimate (41 per cent on another basis); about 50,000 homes are built a year against demand of 250,000. The construction authority's 2022 audit marked 10,791 of 14,895 sampled buildings unsafe; the engineers' institution counted 87 collapses in five years. Budget participation scores 93 at formulation and zero at implementation and audit.
Civic landscape Since 2010, 47 counties with at least 15 per cent of national revenue and a legal duty of participation. Makueni County runs the best-known participatory budget in Africa, village to county, over at least a quarter of its development budget (3,867 meetings and over 350,000 participants by one count); its elected project committees must approve a work before the contractor is paid. A World Bank initiative tripled women's participation to 35 per cent by meeting near homesteads. Evaluations note uneven progress and elite privilege. Counties spent 57 per cent of their development budgets in 2024/25; one spent nothing.
Best local idea A resident's signature between the contractor and the money. And the engineers' institution's proposal of March 2026 for a national building register, so anyone can check who designed, built and approved a building: a sourced page by another name.
Who does this work today Makueni County publishes its participation reports and a project tracking system; the Controller of Budget publishes the counties' implementation reviews; the National Construction Authority registers contractors; the Institution of Engineers of Kenya proposed the building register; the World Bank's Kenya participatory-budgeting initiative published the monitoring pilots cited here.
Risks On the FATF list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring since February 2024 (as reported), which licensed payment providers weigh; strict consent rules; police violence at protests; pending bills that stall county projects; the elite capture the evaluations note.
A hub would do first Import Makueni's published projects and tenders as sourced pages, an evidence channel for ward committees, implementation shown. In Nairobi, the building register as a sourced-page type. Payments go through licensed providers, which weigh the country's FATF status.
Financing angle The Kenya Mortgage Refinance Company, licensed by the central bank, refinances lenders against eligibility criteria, backed by a World Bank project of 250 million dollars (2019 to 2025). If a build file enters its criteria, every lender it refinances reads it.

Ghana

The need A housing deficit of about 1.8 million units. Budget transparency fell from 46 to 22 in the latest survey, because the executive's proposal and the citizens budget were not published in time. Civic space is the most open in Africa on our list.
Civic landscape 261 district assemblies share a common fund of at least five per cent of revenue; their chief executives are appointed. Sekondi-Takoradi joined CoST in 2019 as the first local member of the Open Government Partnership to do so, built a disclosure portal, persuaded its neighbours to publish on it, and by 2026 all 14 districts of the Western Region take part in independent review, with 28 projects reviewed. The national infrastructure transparency index stood at 35.76 in December 2025, against a global average of 50, "not encouraging" in the words of Transparency International's Ghana chapter (secondary).
Best local idea A city that disclosed first and persuaded its neighbours, without waiting for a national law. That is how a hub should grow: one territory, a standard, neighbours peering.
Who does this work today The Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolitan Assembly, a CoST member since 2019, runs the disclosure portal the Western Region's districts publish on; CoST's independent review covered the region's projects in 2026; the Ghana Integrity Initiative, Transparency International's chapter, publishes the national infrastructure transparency index with CoST.
Risks National openness can slip fast; appointed district executives; a data-protection registration duty; contractor registers not online.
A hub would do first Import the Western Region's CoST disclosures as sourced pages, the evidence and confirmation layer added, one place for resident reports in the 14 districts; then one assembly's common-fund works as a participatory pilot.
Financing angle Roughly 90 per cent of the housing stock is produced informally (a 2011 figure) and formal finance for incremental building is largely absent. Evidence and statistics first.

In Ghana the mesh's own idea, one territory peering with the next, has already happened on paper. The hub would give it evidence.

Colombia

The need Thirty-one per cent of households were in housing deficit in 2021, most of it qualitative. Unfinished public works, the "white elephants", have a national registry (figures unverified).
Civic landscape Medellín has voted on part of its investment budget since 2004; its documented failures include capture by armed actors in some districts and clientelism. The participation statute of 2015 defines participatory budgets, and the law of 2003 gives veedurías ciudadanas, citizen oversight committees, the right to watch any public contract. The national procurement system publishes daily open data; among its users direct contracts fell and bidders rose (the standard's steward's own reading). Civic space Repressed: the monitor Indepaz counted at least 187 social leaders killed in 2025 (as reported).
Best local idea Legal standing to inspect, plus the open data to find the work, and twenty years of voting. The missing link is evidence from the site, structured, dated, confirmable.
Who does this work today Medellín's participation secretariat runs the city's local planning and participatory budget, in place since 2004; Colombia Compra Eficiente publishes the national procurement data daily; the national planning department's investment map, MapaInversiones, has been open source since May 2025; veedurías ciudadanas watch contracts under the law of 2003; the monitor Indepaz publishes the count of social leaders killed.
Risks Violence against social leaders makes evidence-gathering dangerous in some regions; armed-group capture of local budgets, documented in Medellín's own history; a new national government since August 2026.
A hub would do first Medellín: the participatory works of two districts as sourced pages, a veeduría's evidence attached where one chooses to attach it, and what was voted, contracted and stands shown to the district.
Financing angle A 2025 decree exempts subsidised improvement, progressive housing and building on one's own plot from the construction licence, replacing it with a letter of responsibility signed by professionals (secondary). A green building certificate went from zero to more than a fifth of new build in a few years (IFC). A build file is the natural record behind such a letter.

Colombia is the country to learn from, not only to serve: its oversight committees need a place where a photograph, a date and a contract line sit together.

The Dominican Republic

The need The Jet Set nightclub roof collapsed in Santo Domingo on 8 April 2025, killing 236; the country had no building inspection code written into law. The President announced one and an expert commission (secondary).
Civic landscape A law of 2007 created a mandatory municipal participatory budget over the share of national transfers the law reserves for capital works, with community assemblies, a two per cent reserve for design and monitoring, and committees that watch works and then maintain them; no sanctions are written. Compliance is contested: one critic claims only eight per cent of town halls comply (undated, secondary), while municipal regulations of 2024 to 2026 show the practice alive. Open contracting data daily since April 2020; a new procurement law since January 2026; a CoST member since 2021, first as an affiliate, with an application for full membership made in October 2024.
Best local idea The whole frame already in law, and now the will for an inspection code. Nothing has to be invented; the loop has to be connected and shown.
Who does this work today The procurement directorate (DGCP) publishes open contracting data and leads the country's CoST membership; the budget directorate publishes the municipalities' budget execution; the municipalities publish their participatory-budget regulations; the expert commission announced after the Jet Set collapse is drafting the inspection code.
Risks A weak data-protection law; a small market; municipal capacity; reform that depends on one administration; hurricanes.
A hub would do first The first civic pilot, below; and the engine's building page as one form the public record of inspections could take, once a code exists.
Financing angle Mortgage-market trusts since a 2011 law; no staged owner-build instrument was found; remittances build houses. The inspection code and the build file want the same object: a dated record per building.

Uzbekistan

The need Construction is booming (15.9 million square metres of housing commissioned in 2025) and mass housing runs from the centre. No open contracting feed was found.
Civic landscape Probably the largest initiative-budgeting programme in the world by votes: in spring 2023, 16.13 million votes for 1,666 winners; 2,369 winners in the first season of 2025 (as reported by the press from ministry figures). The press reported vote buying at about a dollar a vote and teachers sent door to door to collect votes; the labour ministry acknowledged the coercion in 2023, and in 2025 the finance ministry disqualified coerced projects and called compelled voting forced labour. A civic monitor found a third of 135 projects finished with defects, unfinished or never contracted. Local governors are appointed; personal data must stay in the country.
Best local idea Scale and simplicity: a share of local money, a phone and a short season brought millions to decide on their street. And the lesson of its failure: when the vote is the only thing that counts, votes are bought, as the press there reported, so evidence of what was built must weigh as much.
Who does this work today The Ministry of Economy and Finance runs the Open Budget portal and the vote; the civic monitor Yuksalish published the audit of 135 projects cited here. The programme is evidence and a lesson, not a place for a civic hub; in a Closed civic space no independent civic organisation is named.
Risks Contractor reputation or budget gaps read as political; data localisation; secondary-sanctions exposure in the financial system; requests for things we will never build.
A hub would do first A construction hub only, the fastest technical launch on the list: the pack exists and both its languages are in the engine. No civic ring.
Financing angle A mortgage market built in five years by building its records first (chapter 10), with a housing-improvement loan line (as reported by the Asian Development Bank).

We learn from Uzbekistan more than we can offer it today. The civic ring stays paused there, for the reason given at the end of this chapter.

Nigeria

The need A professional guild counts 653 collapses between 1974 and May 2025 with 1,616 deaths, more than half in Lagos (via the press). The 21-storey Ikoyi tower fell on 1 November 2021, killing at least 42, after the building-control agency had approved 15 floors. The official housing deficit is about 15 million units.
Civic landscape Strong governors, weak local governments. Kaduna State, a local member of the partnership and of CoST, has an "Eyes and Ears" app that lets residents locate projects within two kilometres and report on them. BudgIT's Tracka follows over 19,000 projects in over 17,000 communities in all 36 states with community champions (self-reported); Budeshi publishes procurement as open data; the federal portal promised in 2016 had eight compliant ministries in 2018 and is to be rebuilt from 2026. Civic space Repressed. Off the FATF list since October 2025.
Best local idea Community champions: a decade of residents photographing budgeted projects, run by a civic organisation, the field-evidence half of the loop already working. The hub learns its discipline, phone first and champion-led, and offers the other half.
Who does this work today BudgIT runs Tracka; the Public and Private Development Centre runs Budeshi; Kaduna State runs the Eyes and Ears app and discloses under CoST; the Bureau of Public Procurement owns the federal portal to be rebuilt from 2026; the Building Collapse Prevention Guild keeps the collapse count; the Lagos State Building Control Agency approves and inspects buildings in Lagos.
Risks Kidnapping and insurgency in the north, fraud, currency volatility; local processes that depend on the state government of the day; defamation suits against monitors. Expectations stay modest and local.
A hub would do first Kaduna first: disclosed projects and contractors as sourced pages, a structured place for the champions' evidence. Lagos second, for the building register the collapses demand.
Financing angle 71.4 per cent of landlords hold no title (statistics bureau, via the Centre for Affordable Housing Finance in Africa). Family Homes Funds and the refinance company NMRC are the national instruments; neither has an owner-build product. Evidence first, finance later.

Half of the loop already works in Nigeria: residents photograph budgeted projects. The hub offers the other half.

Mexico

The need Self-build is 55.6 per cent of the housing market by value. The 2017 earthquake killed 370, 228 of them in Mexico City, where over 6,000 construction-violation complaints since 2012 had no public record of follow-up. The Metro Line 12 overpass fell on 3 May 2021, 26 dead; the independent investigator found missing and badly welded studs, unauthorised design changes and no inspections for seven years, a report the city rejected (secondary).
Civic landscape Mexico City has voted on a share of each borough's budget since 2011; a 2019 law puts the consultation in the hands of the city's independent electoral institute, in about 1,800 territorial units, with elected execution and oversight committees per project. The share is reported as three or four per cent (unverified). Boroughs reject four proposals in ten or leave winners unbuilt; since 2026 they must report which winners cannot be executed. The national transparency institute was abolished in 2025. Civic space Repressed: Reporters Without Borders counted Mexico the second most dangerous country for journalists in 2025, after Gaza (secondary).
Best local idea An electoral institute runs the vote. The vote belongs to a neutral keeper, execution to the borough, evidence to the public; the engine's vote module is designed for such a keeper, never for a hub operator.
Who does this work today Mexico City's electoral institute (IECM) runs the participatory-budget consultation and publishes its results; the boroughs execute the winners; Jalisco discloses infrastructure projects as a CoST member; the federal ComprasMX publishes procurement; Infonavit runs ConstruYO and the green mortgage; IMCO publishes the analyses of direct awards cited here.
Risks Violence against journalists and monitors; transparency functions concentrated in the executive; organised crime in construction supply; a city that can reject an independent report.
A hub would do first One borough: three years of winning projects and their contracts as sourced pages, execution shown, the committees invited to attach evidence; Jalisco's CoST disclosures as sourced pages.
Financing angle Infonavit's ConstruYO lets a worker build on land they own with money released in stages (secondary), and its green mortgage shows a standard turned into a product. The clearest country for chapter 10: the engine can be the record, not the regime.

Mexico gives the civic ring its institutional shape and the financing ring its first market. Neither waits for the other.

Rwanda

The need The fastest urbanisation on the list (5.6 per cent a year) and a large share of Kigali's housing in unplanned settlements (often cited at 60 to 70 per cent, unverified). Perceived integrity is the best in Africa on our list and second of the fourteen countries of this book, after the United States (58 against 64, as reported).
Civic landscape Thirty districts with annual Imihigo performance contracts, publicly evaluated. Umuganda, the monthly community work, builds schools, roads and bridges; its roots lie in colonial-era compulsory labour, and some residents describe it as compulsory (secondary). The first civic pilot, and why¶

We recommend that the first full loop of public trust run in one municipality of the Dominican Republic, chosen from the places with a documented participatory record, in the second year of the arc. Participatory budgeting there has been mandatory since 2007, so nobody has to be persuaded that residents may decide. The budget is sufficient, the participation score is the best of the fourteen countries of this book, civic space is among the most open on the list, open contracting data is daily, CoST lists the country as a member, and Spanish is already an interface language of the engine, in a machine draft awaiting review. Since April 2025 how a building is inspected is a national question, asked in grief; a hub can offer the public record the answer needs without claiming to be the answer. The country is small enough to show the whole loop in one budget cycle, and to fail safely if we are wrong.

The pilot imports before it asks: the project list and the contracts become sourced pages, the evidence and confirmation layer is added, and one cycle from budget to building is published, whether it flatters or not. The municipality keeps its vote, its law and its data. If it wants the engine's vote module, a neutral keeper runs it, the municipality itself, an electoral institute or an independent civic body, with identity proven outside the hub. After it, in order and only where law and a consenting public body allow: a Peruvian province and a Colombian city with a participatory record; then a Ghanaian assembly that discloses under CoST and a Kenyan county with a participatory record, with a disclosing Nigerian state third; then a Philippine province, an Armenian community and a Mexico City borough. Ecuador, Moldova and Ukraine are the watch list. Nothing civic is promised to any government; the sequence names the kinds of place where the published record is strongest.

Where the civic ring stays paused¶

In four places the engine runs as a construction hub and the civic ring stays off: Uzbekistan, Azerbaijan and Georgia, and Rwanda with institutional evidence only and never resident reputation. We say why with care, because the people of these places need sound building as much as anyone, and some of them have taught the world more about participation at scale and about budget transparency than countries we rank above them.

The civic ring asks residents to attach their names, or at least their photographs, to evidence about public money. Where civic space is Closed, or Repressed without a working civic-budget practice and membership in good standing, that act can cost a resident, a journalist or a local organisation more than any record is worth, and a state may ask the hub for what we will never build: a score of residents, a window into private data. The kindest way to keep our lines is not to open a door we would have to close. The construction hub still helps: a self-builder in Tashkent, a family in Baku, an engineer in Tbilisi get the same plan, the same labelled pages and the same reputation from evidence as anyone else. The pause is reviewed every year against the CIVICUS rating and the partnership's standing, with the date and source recorded in the country pack. It is a consequence of our lines, not a judgement of people.

A civic-space rating is a gate, not a score. We walk through it where it is open, and we wait, kindly, where it is not.

22How a hub opens and how a partner joins

The network has one door and several rooms. A hub operator, a municipality, a lender, a manufacturer, a funder, a contributor and a country-pack maintainer each join differently, but the shape is the same: a real place or a real case, a named accountable person, a clear way the engine reaches you, evidence of what you did, and a record built only from that. This chapter says what each room looks like, what every partner can count on, what we ask, and what Trustwork and Contribuda keep to themselves.

What Trustwork does itself¶

The engine does a small number of things and is careful not to drift beyond them. It keeps the record, gives every person one representative agent, runs the gathering point, carries each country's facts in a pack and federates public projections (chapters 1 and 8). Contribuda maintains it and gives it away under its licence, with no licence fee and no implementation fee; nobody needs Contribuda to run a hub.

What neither does: hold money, lend, insure, inspect, vote, license, build, practise law, or decide a claim. Every one of those is a partner's room, and a partner who does one of them well is not a competitor; they are a participant the record is waiting for.

The ways to join¶

  • As a hub operator

    You run the engine for a country, a region or a city: an association, a chamber, a publisher, a university, a training body, a cooperative, an entrepreneur or a community of engineers. The launch playbook takes you from decision and preparation to installation, lawful sourcing, the compliance gates and a soft launch with real people, before you switch to public (chapter 9). The minimum real hub is two people and the agent team. A hub can start free, charge nothing, and still be a hub. If you wish, and only then, you will be able to open the hub as a project in your personal Contribuda account and manage its team, plans and development from there; the next chapter says what that would mean and what never crosses.

  • As a civic partner

    A municipality, a county, a province, an audit office, a procurement authority or a civic body brings what it already has: a budget, a project list, a procurement feed, an oversight committee, a law that lets residents watch a work. The hub imports what is public as sourced pages before it asks anyone for anything, adds the evidence and confirmation layer, and shows the trail from budget line to building for one cycle. The partner keeps its vote, its law and its data; a vote, if the engine's module is used, is run by a neutral keeper, a municipality, an electoral institute or an independent civic body, never by an industry hub operator, and identity is proven outside the hub. What a civic partner never gets: private data of residents or companies, a say in anyone's reputation, a ranking of anyone, or a record that flatters by design. The civic ring is a direction of this book and opens only where the civic-space gate is open.

  • As a financing partner

    A licensed lender, a housing-finance institution, a microfinance body, a mortgage refinance company, an escrow provider, an insurer, an independent inspector or a valuer. You are a reader of the build file, with the borrower's consent: plan, permits, named professionals, frozen quotes, inspection gates, field evidence, acceptance, changes and defects, the contractor's record. You disburse, insure, inspect or value in your own systems and under your own licence, and the engine records the act as evidence with your name on it. The lines of chapter 10 apply in full, among them that the engine never lends or holds money. You earn a reputation from your acts like every other participant, and a stage released against bad evidence is a record too. None of this is built yet (chapter 18); it begins with one real build, in a place where a staged, inspected disbursement already exists.

  • As a knowledge partner

    A manufacturer, a supplier, a professional body, a training centre, a university or a publisher brings knowledge: technical guides, course material under a written licence, experts who answer consultations, reviewers for seed guides, events. Your material appears on the plan steps where it helps, labelled as yours, because it fits and never because you paid. Your experts earn reputation and income from the consultations they give. Sponsored content never ranks. Certificates your body issues can be recorded on the hub with the accreditation shown, and the engine never calls anything "certified by Trustwork".

  • As a funder

    A development bank, a donor, a foundation, a government programme or a company funds a hub, a country pack, a language, a module or the engine itself. What you buy is work in the open: a work package with acceptance criteria, a pull request anyone can read, a status that says Live only when it is. What you do not buy: a ranking, a data set about people, a say in reputation, a hub's silence, or exclusivity. Chapter 3 says why a single funder is a risk, and we will tell you what else funds the work.

  • As a contributor to the engine

    Developers, designers, translators, lawyers and practitioners, alone or with an AI agent you are accountable for. Work packages are public and decisions are recorded in the open; chapter 27 says how a change is made.

  • As a country-pack maintainer

    You know a country: its identifiers, registers, licensing, codes, procurement, tax, signatures, payments, holidays, and now its civic facts too, the participation statute, the oversight committee's standing, the whistle-blower protection or its absence, the civic-space rating with its date and source. Every fact needs a source and a verification level. You are the source of truth for your country's facts, and every hub in that country configures itself from them. Even one well-sourced facet helps.

What every partner can count on¶

  • Nothing to pay us for the engine. No licence fee, no implementation fee. A hub's income stays with its owner in its country; a hub operator's own costs, hosting, people and local adaptation, are its own. Contribuda's only part is the international layer, in a form to be decided and disclosed to both parties (chapter 7).
  • Honest status. A partnership is named only when it exists and only with your agreement. A documented API is not a partnership.
  • Evidence, not advertising. Your record on the hub is built from accepted work and confirmed acts. Every reputation snapshot shows its method and its sample size. No score is for sale, and no payment, plan, sponsorship or staff action changes a ranking.
  • Your data stays yours. Private stays private, including from hub staff, who read a private record only under a scoped case grant. Deal data travels only between the parties' nodes. Public projections are the only thing that federates by default.
  • One lawful frame. Sanctions compliance applies to every deployment and every counterparty; restricted places are never territories, hosts or markets; counsel is named on every legal step; nothing in the book or the handbook is legal advice.
  • A right of reply. A contractor, an institution or a partner can answer any observation on its page with evidence; a disputed observation stops counting until a different person decides; a fair negative record can be answered, never deleted.
  • An exit. The engine is free software. A hub operator can leave with its data; a participant can export theirs and move to another hub; a node can run without the Trustwork name. Nobody is locked in, and that is why the record can be trusted.

What we ask¶

A real first case, not a catalogue of possibilities: one municipality, one build, one province's project list, one course. A named person on your side who is accountable and can decide. Clear terms we can read and act within. Patience: the first integration is a manual step until the adapter has earned its place. Acceptance of the rule that evidence is published whether it flatters or not, with a right of reply, never a right of deletion. And for a civic partner, one more: that the vote, where there is one, belongs to a neutral keeper, and that no resident is ever the object of a record.

A few things are not for partnership, because the record depends on their not being shared. The reputation method and its computation stay with the participant's home node; others display it with attribution. The lines of chapter 15 are not configurable by any hub, partner or funder, and neither is the outreach floor: every hub starts strict, and outreach beyond that happens only under a counsel-approved hub policy with a second person, to organisation and role addresses, never as bulk messages to private persons. Private data never leaves its node and is never a term of any agreement. The civic-space gate is to be set by a recorded decision, with its sources; a partner may ask for a review of it, not for a lower bar. The Trustwork name on a hub follows the brand rules and the charter, and the engine can always be run without it. One door, several rooms, the same floor under all of them: a real case, a named person, and a record nobody can buy.

But a hub is also a small company, and Contribuda is a tool for running small companies with people and agents. This chapter says what that would mean, where the boundary is, how San Diego fits, what is free, and how little of it exists today.

Contribuda develops the engine and gives it away, and any hub may decline it entirely. Where a hub owner wishes, Contribuda can serve as the tool for running and developing the hub, on Contribuda's own terms: the hub's organisation mirrored by a project team, so that someone who wants a hub for their region can open it as a project in their own Contribuda account and receive the local services and configuration it needs. The San Diego hub is described at the end of the chapter.

Anyone may refuse¶

Start with the refusal, because it is what makes the rest credible. The engine is free software under a licence that lets anyone run it, change it and run it unbranded. Its handbook, its console, its setup wizard, its launch playbook and its operating company of agents are part of the engine, and so are free of charge. Nothing in the engine requires Contribuda. No gate in the launch checklist asks for a Contribuda account. The public directory of hubs, designed to be run by Contribuda (Proposed; to be confirmed), is a phone book any operator may also run, and no hub needs it in order to peer. A municipality that must win a tender on its own costs, a chamber whose procurement rules forbid a foreign platform, an entrepreneur who prefers their own tools: each runs a complete hub and owes Contribuda nothing, not even a conversation.

Two roles, kept apart¶

Contribuda plays two roles, and the book keeps them apart. As the developer of the engine it creates Trustwork, develops it and maintains it, and gives it to everyone free. As a company it runs its own platform and services under its own rules, the laws that apply to it and its own commercial terms, for any client.

The second role is easiest to see by imagining that Trustwork had been written by someone else. A person could still open a project in Contribuda, point it at that open-source engine, launch a business on it and grow it. They would be a client of Contribuda, working with it on commercial terms under Contribuda's rules, like the client of any other project. A hub owner who opens a Trustwork hub through Contribuda is in exactly that position. That Contribuda also wrote the engine gives the hub owner no extra obligation and Contribuda no extra claim: the engine stays free, and only the service the hub owner chooses is paid, by the same rules as for every client.

Three paths are therefore always open. Run the hub entirely alone, without Contribuda. Launch it through Contribuda and use its services for as long as they help. Or leave Contribuda at any moment and keep running the hub alone, with its infrastructure, accounts and data, which were created in the owner's name from the start.

What Contribuda is, briefly¶

Contribuda is a project operating system and a managed delivery service. A person brings an idea; Contribuda's assistant understands it in one conversation and frames a project. The plan is a work breakdown, each work package naming a professional role at a level. The team is a seat chart derived from the plan; a seat is held by the owner's own person or agent, by a contributor, or by one of Contribuda's system agents, and an agent is never accountable. The runtime creates what the plan declared: a repository, hosting, a database, a domain in the owner's name, later a company through a formation operator. Every decision that is a person's comes to them as a gate. The project's conversation lives beside the plan. Money follows accepted work: the client pays Contribuda for a result, and Contribuda pays the people who made it, as the one counterparty on both sides.

Trustwork is an engine others run. What follows is how the tool that runs projects could run the company around a hub, if its owner wants it to.

Opening a hub as a project¶

Told step by step, as a design and not as a thing that exists. The roles are roles; the region is any region.

A hub for a province, opened as a project
  1. Day one
    The director of a builders' association · a provincial capital

    Signs in to a personal Contribuda account and tells the assistant: a Trustwork hub for the province. The assistant recognises the kind and asks what the launch playbook needs first: the territory, the operator entity, the languages, where personal data will live, the budget, the accounts the association already has. What the director does not know stays an open decision with the director's name on it, never a guess.

  2. Day one, later
    The assistant · the project page

    Frames the project and composes the plan from a versioned playbook whose chapters are Trustwork's own launch phases: decide, prepare, install, source data, let the agent teams work, claims and outreach, compliance gates, soft launch, operations. Each step names a role, a holder kind and the evidence that closes it. The team appears as a seat chart, the association's own people where the director named them, the rest open. The director approves the plan once.

  3. Week one
    The runtime · in the director's name

    Forks the engine into the association's own repository, provisions hosting in the region the residency decision named, a database, a domain bought in the association's name, a mail sender domain, and pulls the country pack. Every account is the association's. The hub comes up as a draft with every provider a fake and nothing sent anywhere, as the setup wizard would have left it.

  4. Weeks two to six
    Counsel, a formation operator, a bookkeeper · gates

    The plan's legal steps ask the country router for a formation operator and find none for this country; the step becomes a named manual gate, which the association's lawyer takes with the registration as evidence. Counsel reviews the terms, the privacy notice, the sanctions section and the outreach policy at gates of their own. A translator from the network is seated by offer.

  5. Weeks three to ten
    The editor-in-chief, the data steward · in the hub

    Inside the hub, not in Contribuda, the operator's agent team is launched from a preset: scouts, writers, fact-checkers, the claim assistant, the sanctions review assistant, all at Draft, each with an accountable person. The positions appear in the project as seats held by the same people; the queues and drafts stay in the hub.

  6. Week twelve
    The director and a second person · the hub console

    The twelve launch gates and the country's own gates are green, with versions and dates. The switch to soft launch, and later to public, belongs to the director, in the hub, and no gate in Contribuda can take it away. In Contribuda, the project's channel records the day; in the hub, the daily brief does the same, and each says which system it describes.

After the launch the project goes on living. Hire signals from the hub become open seats or positions. Development requests from the hub, a missing register, a new adapter, a fix, become work packages taken by the association's own people, by contributors or by system agents, and delivered as pull requests to the public engine, because the licence requires it and because every hub gains from it. The hub's brand, content and configuration are its own and never go upstream.

The organisation, synchronised¶

The hub operating company has twenty-three functions, each enabled function with exactly one accountable person at every moment, positions that say what a person is responsible for, staff roles that say what they may do, and operator agents under hub grants, budgets and kill switches. Contribuda has units, seats, a role catalogue and one accountable person per deliverable, always a person. The two fit, if one rule is kept: Contribuda mirrors the hub's organisation, never its operations.

Local services, by country¶

A launch needs a legal entity, counsel, a bookkeeper, hosting in the right country, a registrar, a mail route and translators. Contribuda's design for this is a country router over an administered catalogue of providers: for a step and a country it names a verified, enabled provider and the step runs; where there is none, the step becomes a manual gate with a named person and evidence, never a silent skip and never an agent pretending to a tool it does not have. Which steps run through a provider and which through a manual gate depends on the country and on what Contribuda has verified at the time, and a hub owner should expect to take the local steps, the legal entity, counsel and bookkeeping among them, at a manual gate with their own lawyer and accountant. The payment providers that move participants' money are never Contribuda's to choose: the hub configures them in its console, with a licence on record, and Trustwork never holds the money.

The boundary that never moves¶

What syncs: the organisation, plans, tasks, decisions, obligations, development requests and development work, and counts in cohorts of ten or more. What never crosses: hub participants' private data; deal data, from a need to a frozen quote to a counterparty confirmation; staff case data, from a support case to a sanctions match to an appeal; datasets with personal data; the operator agents' queues, drafts and grants; provider secrets; audit beyond counts. Contribuda gets no access to a hub's private records, not for its staff, not for its system agents when they hold seats in the hub's project, not for support. If a hub ever wanted a Contribuda employee to read a private record, the only door would be the hub's own scoped, time-limited, second-person-approved case grant, the same door as for any staff member, and the decision would be the hub's.

Identities are separate. A Contribuda account and a hub account are two accounts of one person, joined for the sync by one explicit act and parted by another. There is no single sign-on, and none is planned before federated sign-in across hubs, which is itself only proposed. Neither system has "log in as user". The owner's representative agent acts for them in the hub; their own agent in Contribuda acts for them there; no agent of one system holds a grant in the other. And the hub owner can disconnect at any moment. The hub keeps running; the repository, the hosting, the database, the domain, the mail and every provider account were theirs from the day the runtime made them, and stay theirs. Contribuda's rule is that what it builds is delivered in the owner's name; here that rule is the condition of being allowed in at all.

Contribuda may hold the plan. It never holds the hub.

San Diego, the exemplary hub¶

The hub in San Diego is a demonstration of the engine, and, by the project owner's recorded decision, it is the place where every new method, technology and function lands first. The release channel, as we propose it: in demo mode with labelled synthetic data while a feature is unproven and on the live hub once it is proven, with the network announcements carrying each release to the other hubs, which adopt on their own schedule. Contribuda is its decided operator; today it is a demo on synthetic data, and real sign-ups open only after a release review, counsel review and the project owner's recorded decision. Whatever the hub charges before a transfer, if anything, would be set as hub settings, accounted to the hub and pass with it; that rule is to be decided. Its ownership is to be transferred by an open competition to a local operator.

The competition, as we propose it: announced on the hub and in the directory; open to any California entity eligible to operate a hub, screened, with two staff and counsel; judged on a credible plan for English and Spanish, for the works of the city and for the region's contractors, training bodies and colleges, and on acceptance of the charter, the engine's invariants and the list of things it will never build; decided by a panel with at least one person from outside Contribuda, with the reasons published. The transfer moves the domain and every account to the new operator, as the runtime is designed to do at any handover; changes the data controller, with notice to every participant in advance and the right to export or delete first; rotates the node's keys with a second person and a published revocation; keeps every configuration, fee and legal document version and the whole audit history; and hands over function by function through the shadowing and supervised states the operating company already defines. Sale or gift is to be decided.

One tension must be named. A hub owned by a local operator may not want to be the place where every unproven feature meets real people first. Three resolutions exist: a showcase deployment kept by the project, on synthetic data that never federates, where the unproven lands; a transfer condition that binds the new operator to the release channel for a fixed period, with a right to delay for a stated reason; or a public reference channel any hub can opt into, so that San Diego is simply the first to say yes. We recommend the first with the third, and the second only for the transfer's first year. The decision is to be taken.

What is free, and what follows Contribuda's terms¶

Free, settled, and not for revisiting: the engine; its installation, which is a documented stack anyone can run; the handbook, the console and the launch playbook; the operating company of agents inside the hub; a listing in the directory. No licence fee, no implementation fee, no share of anything a hub earns at home.

A hub owner who wants Contribuda's help, or who runs the hub's company through Contribuda, opens a Contribuda project like any other and becomes a client of Contribuda, on its commercial terms and under its rules, as anyone would be who built a business on any open-source engine. It is a service the hub owner chooses, not a fee for the engine or for implementing it, and it can be stopped at any moment without the hub losing anything.

What we are still missing¶

Several gaps remain; these are the ones a hub owner should know before choosing.

A United States counterparty. Contribuda works under United States law and screens money against United States sanctions lists. A hub in Peru or Kenya is lawful on its own; using Contribuda adds a United States relationship, and whether anything in the hub owner's country restricts buying project tools from a foreign platform is a question for counsel.

Staff data abroad. No participant data crosses, but the synchronised organisation carries the names and positions of the hub's staff, which are personal data, stored where Contribuda runs, in the United States. Until counsel and a privacy notice on the hub's side settle it, positions should cross as roles and seats without names unless the person consents.

Public bodies. A municipality may be unable to open a personal account and pay by card; it needs an invoice, a contract and sometimes a tender, and some countries require data to stay at home. So a public body should be able to run a hub without Contribuda at all, and the engine is built so that no tender depends on it.

Conflicts of interest. Contribuda maintains the engine, is designed to run the directory, operates San Diego and would offer its tool to every hub, San Diego's future competitors included. The mitigations: a directory that never ranks; the San Diego transfer; a published rule that Contribuda learns nothing from a hub project that it could not learn from public sources; its roles disclosed wherever they meet; and, in time, an operators' association that holds the charter and reviews the tool. That association does not yet exist; until it does, each such decision is the project owner's, recorded and dated.

Service levels. The free parts of the engine carry no service level. A hub that needs one arranges it under Contribuda's terms for the work it orders, or with a hosting partner of its own.

Words. The two systems use some of the same words for different things: "hub", "operator" and "function" each mean something else in Contribuda. The synchronisation needs a published table that maps them, and this book keeps each system's words for its own objects.

Where it stands¶

Almost everything above is a direction of this book: neither the synchronisation nor the hub playbook is designed in the repository. On Trustwork's side, the hub operating company is an accepted decision whose function catalogue and team presets are still an open work package, while function accountability, staff invitations and offboarding with hand-over are Live in the API; of the hub console, the setup wizard, approvals, hub settings, legal drafts and launch-gate checks run on the live API and the remaining sections on fixtures; and no hub, San Diego included, is deployed for real use. What Contribuda itself can do today is described on Contribuda's own pages, under its own terms.

So the reading for a hub owner is this: the engine, and everything in it, stands on its own today; the synchronisation described here is the system as it will be, told as such.

Take the engine for nothing. Take the tool if it helps. Keep the keys either way.

Refinance company
A licensed institution that refinances many lenders' mortgages against its own eligibility criteria (Kenya's KMRC, Nigeria's NMRC, Uzbekistan's refinancing company). The most influential reader of a build file: a file in its criteria is read by every lender it refinances.
Representative agent
The one agent each person has, acting under mandates the person can read, change and revoke. It works for you, not on you. It will connect your own AI chat over MCP; the authorisation server is Live and the transport is an open work package.
Reputation
What the engine computes from evidence: accepted deliveries, confirmed acts, defects remedied, payments on time, reported in separate dimensions with sample sizes and a published method, never as one public score, never bought. Absence of evidence is unknown, not failure.
Reputation passport
A person's portable, signed, revocable record of certificates and confirmed work, issued by the home hub as verifiable credentials, with the holder choosing what to include. Proposed.
Resident
A person who lives in a place, whether or not a citizen. The book's default word; "citizen" appears only where a law or a programme uses it. A resident is never the object of a record.
Restricted place
A territory the engine refuses at every level under sanctions law: Russia, Crimea, Sevastopol, the Donetsk and Luhansk regions, Cuba, Iran and North Korea. Never a territory, never a host, never a market.
Sealed request for quotes
A request whose quotes stay sealed until the deadline: no late undercutting; the buyer sees who answered but no price until then. The private-sector analogue of a sealed public bid.
Second-person activation
The rule that sensitive changes, a live provider, prices, a dataset with personal data, the release of a kill switch, need a second staff member with a distinct role to take effect.
Social audit
A public verification of a programme's records by trained residents, ending in a public hearing with the responsible officials present. Credible when an independent, funded body convenes it and consequences follow.
Sourced page
A page about a company, a tender, a contract, a public work or a building built from a public source, labelled "from an open source" with field-level provenance, licence and date. Nothing on it is a Trustwork judgement.
Staged disbursement
Money released by a licensed lender, escrow provider or funder stage by stage against accepted evidence and an inspector's act. The engine exposes a release signal and never holds the money.
Statuses
The engine's five words for what exists: Live (implemented end to end with tests, not yet deployed to production anywhere), Demo (an interface on synthetic data), Contract (data shapes frozen as tested schemas), Designed (an accepted design, no code), Proposed (designed in the handbook for the first time, with work packages, open for review). See also direction of this book.
Task
A piece of work from a plan item given to a trade or a worker, accepted against criteria, and the smallest unit from which an informal worker's portfolio is built.
Territory
A hub's declared country, region or city, identified by standard codes and a place key. Descriptive, never exclusive.
Tombstone
A signed record that something was deleted or unpublished, which every peer applies so that a withdrawn projection disappears across the mesh, and which is replayed after a restore so that nothing deleted comes back.
Valuer
A licensed professional who states what an asset, finished or unfinished, is worth. For a lender, the person the build file was written to help; in the engine, a participant with a reputation from their own acts.
Verified page
A claimed page whose register record is active and matching, with a signatory tie and sanctions screening. An uploaded extract or a registration number alone proves nothing.
Vote module
The engine's participatory ballot: approval, knapsack or equal-shares counting, district envelopes, a verified-once pseudonymous ballot that cannot be linked to a person by any role, identity proven outside the hub, and a neutral keeper who runs it, a municipality, an electoral institute or an independent civic body, never an industry hub operator. A direction not yet designed.
Warranty insurance
Cover for defects in a finished build for a stated period, priced by an insurer from the build file's evidence of who built what and how it was inspected. A possible outcome of chapter 10, never a Trustwork product.
Work package
A public unit of engineering work with a goal, acceptance criteria and owned paths, claimed on its issue and accepted by review. The civic engine has none until its decision record lands.

27This book is part of the engine

The Trustwork Book is not written about the engine from outside it. It is one of the engine's parts. It lives in the same public repository as the code, under the same licence and in the same history. It changes the same way: in the open, through changes anyone can read, question and trace. It is a living book, and anyone who builds with Trustwork can write in it.

Why a book belongs to an engine¶

An engine that many people run in many countries needs more than code that works. It needs a shared understanding of what the code is for. A hub operator in Accra, a city budget office in Medellín, a lender reading a build file and a contributor's agent opening the repository for the first time should reach the same answers. They need to know why the engine is free, whom a hub belongs to, what is never done with a resident's data and why reputation cannot be bought. The code enforces those answers, and the decision records fix them. This book explains them. Without the explanation, every new hub would rediscover the reasons, or lose them.

So the book is treated like the rest of the engine. It is versioned with the code, so any reader can see what it said on the day a decision was taken. It is checked by the same pipeline: a change that breaks its rules fails the build. And it is open to the same people. A book about building trust from evidence should itself be built that way.

A living book¶

Each edition is a dated snapshot. This is the first, of November 2026. Between editions the book grows. Corrections land when a reader finds a wrong number. Research lands when someone brings evidence from their country. Chapter 18 changes when something is built. And once hubs run, the book gains chapters about what they taught us. When the engine changes what is true, the book changes with it. When the book's reasoning turns out wrong, the book says so and says what replaced it.

The status words in this book (Live, Demo, Contract, Designed and Proposed) describe the state on the date of the edition. The engine's status page in the repository is always authoritative.

Everyone can write in it¶

Hub owners and operators, partners, researchers, engineers, people who have built their own house, students and AI agents working for a person: anyone may propose a change. The book needs most what only its readers have:

  • Corrections. A fact, a number, a source or a status that is wrong or out of date, or a sentence that reads wrong to someone in another country.
  • Research. Evidence about participatory and open budgets, building standards, housing finance, learning, building culture or civic space, with a source for every claim. Research notes sit beside the book, and editors carry what holds up into the chapters.
  • Places. A profile of a country or a city where the engine is needed, or a correction of one in chapter 20.
  • Cases and thoughts. What happened on a real site or in a real budget, what a family learned from a build, and what a hub should do differently. Ideas that change the direction come as a proposal and are discussed in the open.
  • Translations. Editions in other languages, each reviewed by a native speaker.

What contributors cannot change by editing are the book's positions. These are the mission, the engine being open and free, income earned inside a country belonging to that country, the invariants and the lines in chapter 15. They change only by a recorded decision, and anyone may propose one.

How a change is made¶

The sources are in docs/book/ of the repository github.com/Contribuda/trustwork. docs/book/README.md is the book's own page: how to read it, how to contribute and the editorial rules. book.json holds the parts and the order of chapters. Each chapter is one small HTML file in chapters/. The figures are in figures/, drawn with colours taken from the book's theme. The research notes are in research/.

  1. Open an issue for anything larger than a correction, so that the change fits the book and does not repeat another chapter.
  2. Edit the chapter, or add one and name it in book.json. Add every new outside source to chapter 28.
  3. Run npm run book. It writes the designed offline edition and a text edition with one Markdown file per chapter. Commit the sources and both editions. npm run validate:docs fails when an edition drifts from its sources.
  4. Open a pull request as for any other change: say what and why, name the source, and sign off. People review it, and it lands when a maintainer accepts it.

The build refuses whatever would make the book depend on a network or break its promises. It rejects anything fetched from outside the file. It rejects an outside link anywhere but the author's page and the sources, and a repository path anywhere but this chapter. It also rejects text that is not English and an unfinished passage.

The rules the editors keep¶

  • Two kinds of chapter. Vision and the system as it will be fill most of the book. What is built today lives in chapter 18. When something lands, its sentence there changes, and the rest stands.
  • Evidence. No invented numbers, people, quotations or prices. Every outside fact has a source in chapter 28. What could not be verified is said plainly or left out.
  • Organisations. Naming an organisation records its public work. It implies no contact, interest or agreement. "Connected" is used only for an integration that works today. Nobody is called a partner unless an agreement exists and both sides have made it public.
  • Places. Restricted places never appear as hosts or markets. The civic-space review in chapter 20 is repeated every year and dated.
  • People. The book holds no personal data beyond the author's public biography and the published contacts.
  • Words. The terms of the glossary, "resident" rather than "citizen" unless a law says so, plain professional prose, no hype.
