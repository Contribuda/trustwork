# H. The family home: the largest asset, a house that remembers, learning across generations, free learning, protection

Research note for the Trustwork Book, first edition, November 2026. Working evidence, not the book's position.

Feeds chapter 2. Every web source was accessed on 2026-11-04 unless marked.
Evidence levels: **primary/official**, _(secondary)_, _(vendor/self claim)_,
_(search excerpt only: the document is primary but was read only through a
search engine's excerpt, not fetched)_. Nothing here is legal advice. Naming an organisation records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

**Question.** A family's house is its largest investment and passes from
generation to generation; it accumulates mistakes and learns. How can everyone
learn to build and keep a home well, free of charge, and be protected, and what
record, statistics, insurance and financial instruments does that take?

This note does not repeat research notes D (education, Baukultur, self-builder
programmes, Nigeria collapse figures, verifiable credentials) and E (housing
finance, the bankable build file, décennale and dommages-ouvrage, NHBC
Buildmark, MaBV, Techo Propio, insurers' needs). It cites them where the facts
belong to them.

## Summary

1. In the OECD, owner-occupied housing is on average half of all household
   wealth and more than 60 per cent of middle-class wealth; the OECD calls it
   "households' largest lifetime investment and the majority of their wealth".
   In the euro area 61.7 per cent of households own their main residence (median
   value €191,600, above the median household's total assets of €158,500); in
   the United States the median homeowner's net worth is $396,200 against
   $10,400 for renters.
2. Houses are what most families pass on. Below £1 million, British estates "are
   likely to consist mainly of residential property and cash" (HMRC); OECD
   countries exempt the main residence from inheritance tax so often that most
   estates pay nothing. For the poor of developing countries "land and housing
   are the most important assets" (World Bank), built in stages from savings and
   remittances, and 70 per cent of Peru's homes are self-built.
3. Four countries have built a record that travels with the house: Japan's
   "ie-karute" house history (3.57 million dwellings by 2018, with a national
   house ID and a long-life housing law whose certified houses must keep
   maintenance records; 39.3 per cent of new detached houses were certified in
   FY2024), Flanders' free Woningpas (2018, a file for over 3 million
   dwellings), France's carnet d'information du logement (law of 2021, in force
   2023, owner-kept, handed to the buyer at the deed) and the EU's digital
   building logbook, defined in law for the first time in the 2024 EPBD recast.
4. The United Kingdom's Home Information Packs (2007–2010) are the warning: more
   than 90 per cent of buyers wanted condition information and fewer than one in
   six trusted it when the seller supplied it. A record is worth what its
   verification is worth. No sourced study was found that quantifies a resale
   premium for maintenance records; the "5–10 per cent" figure circulating
   online is unsourced marketing and must not be used.
5. Defect observatories exist and work: France's Sycodés (since 1986, fed by
   décennale claims; water is 61 per cent of all defects), Japan's warranty
   bodies (water ingress 90 per cent of defects), Denmark's Byggeskadefonden (1
   per cent levy, 1- and 5-year inspections, "serious failures in over 25 per
   cent of new buildings" in the early 1990s, "under 8 per cent" today) and NHBC
   (0.7 per cent of covered homes a year make a valid latent-defect claim). Each
   turns individual misfortune into shared knowledge and insurance pricing.
6. Disasters teach when the lesson becomes practice: 80 per cent of Kobe's 1995
   deaths were from collapsing houses, mostly pre-1981; Alabama's FORTIFIED
   homes (51,000+) cut hurricane claim frequency by 55–74 per cent in a
   40,000-property study, with mandatory insurance discounts and $10,000 grants;
   after Haiyan "many can quote the key messages, price is the main barrier".
7. Free, good learning exists (FEMA's homeowner guides, Australia's "Your Home"
   since 2001, UNAM's Spanish self-build course with 159,540 learners, Habitat's
   homeowner classes) and the evidence on what makes it bite is consistent: a
   trusted local builder, a short message, the learner's own language, and money
   or materials released at the step where the lesson applies.
8. Protection in rich markets rests on five pillars (a licensed or registered
   builder, a statutory warranty or defect insurance, inspected stages,
   protected deposits and a dispute body); Japan added compulsory insurance or
   deposit in 2009 after a fraud left buyers paying two mortgages. In self-build
   markets every pillar presupposes a professional builder and a record, so
   families who build with an informal mason have none of them.
9. Trustwork already holds the parts: plan to people, execution records with
   hashed evidence, the build file of chapter 10, consent-based shared knowledge,
   learning tied to plan steps, the reputation passport and hub-cohort
   analytics. What is missing is the house record that continues after
   completion and is owned by the family and passed with the home, the
   independent adviser as a role, and a Sycodés-like consented observatory per
   hub. All of this is a direction of the book, not yet designed in the
   repository.
10. The human meaning is simple and sourced: the house is most families' biggest
    thing; it is what they leave; it forgets nothing it is allowed to remember;
    and a family that can read its own house, learn for free, and be protected
    when it builds is the point.

## 1. The home as a family's largest asset

### 1.1 OECD countries: half of all wealth, most of the middle class's wealth

The OECD's 2022 study _Housing Taxation in OECD Countries_ says it in one
sentence: "Housing also constitutes households' largest lifetime investment and
the majority of their wealth, though its significance varies across countries.
Housing is a key vehicle for wealth accumulation and is a particularly important
asset for middle-class households. In OECD countries, on average, owner-occupied
housing accounts for 50% of total household wealth across all households
(Figure 1) and for more than 60% of middle-class wealth." The figure is an
average over 29 OECD countries with 2019 data from the OECD Wealth Distribution
Database; "total housing wealth (including both owner-occupied and secondary
housing) accounts for at least 80% of total household wealth in Chile, Latvia,
Lithuania, and Greece, but less than 40% in the United States and New Zealand."
The same brochure adds that housing "is on average the single-largest
expenditure item across all income groups". — OECD, _Housing Taxation in OECD
Countries: Highlights_ (brochure), 2022,
https://www.oecd.org/tax/tax-policy/brochure-housing-taxation-in-oecd-countries.pdf,
**primary/official**. The full report (OECD Tax Policy Studies No. 29) puts the
share at "roughly 65% of household wealth on average for the second, third, and
fourth quintiles" and much lower at the top, where wealth is financial _(search
excerpt only; the report page could not be reached)_.

The OECD's 2019 _Under Pressure: The Squeezed Middle Class_ frames the cost
side: housing is the largest expense of middle-income households, about a third
of disposable income against about a quarter in the 1990s, and house prices grew
about three times faster than median household income over two decades. — OECD,
2019,
https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/social-issues-migration-health/under-pressure-the-squeezed-middle-class_689afed1-en
_(secondary: the figures were read from press summaries of the report, not from
the report itself)_.

The OECD Wealth Distribution Database working paper behind these numbers
(Balestra and Tonkin, 2018, 28 countries) also gives the distribution: the 60
per cent least wealthy households own a little over 12 per cent of household
wealth, the top 10 per cent about 52 per cent. —
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/inequalities-in-household-wealth-across-oecd-countries_7e1bf673-en.html,
**primary/official** (the 52 per cent figure is also on slide 4 of the OECD
inheritance presentation below).

### 1.2 Euro area: the main residence is the one big thing

The ECB's Household Finance and Consumption Survey, 2021 wave (fieldwork
2020–2021, published 2023): "The most significant real asset remained the
household main residence, with an ownership rate of 61.7% in 2021, essentially
unchanged from 2017. The conditional median value of the household main
residence was €191,600, while the conditional mean was €247,300". Ownership of
any real asset was 91.7 per cent; of other real estate about 25 per cent. The
median household's _total_ assets were €158,500, which means that for a typical
owner the home is not one asset among several but the asset. — ECB, _Household
Finance and Consumption Survey: Results from the 2021 wave_, Statistics Paper
Series No 46, Table 2 and section 2.1,
https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpsps/ecb.sps46~3563bc9f03.en.pdf,
**primary/official**.

### 1.3 United States: owners and renters live in different wealth worlds

The Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances: "The homeownership rate
increased slightly between 2019 and 2022, to 66.1 percent"; "Between 2019 and
2022, the median and mean net worth of renters or other non-homeowners grew 43
percent to $10,400 and 40 percent to $154,900, respectively. Among homeowners,
these measures grew 34 percent to $396,200 and 20 percent to $1,530,900"; the
median primary residence was worth $323,200; "The median net housing value...
increased 44 percent between 2019 and 2022, from about $139,100 to $200,000";
"almost two-thirds of homeowners have home-secured debt, while just over
one-third of homeowners own their home free of debt." — Board of Governors,
_Changes in U.S. Family Finances from 2019 to 2022_, Federal Reserve Bulletin,
October 2023, https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf23.pdf,
**primary/official**. NAHB's reading of the same survey: the primary residence
is more than a quarter of all household assets; for families whose head has no
high-school diploma it is about half of their assets, for those with a
bachelor's degree 23 per cent. — NAHB Eye on Housing, March 2024,
https://eyeonhousing.org/2024/03/examining-differences-between-homeowner-and-renter-wealth/
_(secondary analysis of primary data)_.

### 1.4 Homes are what families pass on

- **OECD, _Inheritance Taxation in OECD Countries_ (2021).** Inheritances are "a
  growing share of private wealth" (the presentation reproduces Alvaredo,
  Garbinti and Piketty's series for France, Germany, Sweden, the UK and the US);
  they are unequally distributed and "benefitting wealthier households more"; 24
  of 37 OECD countries levy an inheritance or estate tax, raising on average 0.5
  per cent of tax revenue. — OECD presentation, May 2021,
  https://www.oecd.org/tax/tax-policy/inheritance-taxation-in-oecd-countries-presentation-may-2021.pdf,
  **primary/official**. The report says relief for the main residence is common
  and that "a majority of estates go untaxed in a number of countries in large
  part due to the highly preferential tax treatment applying to transfers to
  close relatives and because of the relief provided for transfers of specific
  assets (e.g. main residence)"; relief is often conditional on heirs continuing
  to live in the house. —
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/inheritance-taxation-in-oecd-countries_e2879a7d-en.html
  _(search excerpt only; the report pages could not be reached)_. The report's
  share of bequests that are housing could not be read.
- **United Kingdom.** HMRC's commentary for 2023–24: 30,400 estates paid
  inheritance tax; "Where net estate value is less than £1 million, estates are
  likely to consist mainly of residential property and cash." —
  https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/inheritance-tax-liabilities-statistics/inheritance-tax-liabilities-statistics-commentary,
  **primary/official**. Press readings of the HMRC tables put residential
  property at 46.8 per cent of net estate value in 2022–23, and at about half of
  taxpaying estates in London _(secondary; the HMRC table itself was not read
  today)_.
- **United States.** About one in five households had received an inheritance by
  2022 (SCF, via press) _(secondary)_; a claimed breakdown by asset type
  attributed to the Richmond Fed could not be verified and is not used.

### 1.5 Developing countries: the house as savings, pension and security

- "Land and housing are the most important assets of the poor." — World Bank,
  Land topic page, https://ppp.worldbank.org/node/9456 _(search excerpt of a
  World Bank page)_. A 2007 World Bank brief on low-income housing in Latin
  America and the Caribbean describes housing as the poor's main way of
  accumulating assets, "representing more than 50 percent of the assets of
  households" _(search excerpt only; the document page showed only navigation
  today)_.
- Housing in developing countries is built in stages and paid for informally:
  "informal finance continues to dominate the funding of incremental housing",
  with newer sources being housing microfinance, community savings-and-loan
  groups and consumer credit for building materials. — Ferguson and Smets,
  "Finance for incremental housing; current status and prospects for expansion",
  _Habitat International_ 34(3), 2010,
  https://hofinet.org/documents/doc.aspx?id=1720, **primary (peer-reviewed
  abstract)**. The World Bank's 2015 stocktaking ("most housing is built
  incrementally... savings and small loans"), UN-Habitat's World Cities Report
  2026 and CAHF's country profiles are quoted in note E, section 1.1, and are
  not repeated here.
- The balance-sheet mirror: in India financial assets are only 25.8 per cent of
  gross household wealth, among the lowest of 56 markets, against nearly 79 per
  cent in the United States; the rest is land, housing and other real assets. —
  UBS Global Wealth Report 2025/2026, via press _(secondary)_.
- Peru, which is in Trustwork's top-10 launch research: "cerca del 70 % de las
  viviendas en el Perú son producto de la autoconstrucción. En regiones como
  Huancavelica, Cajamarca y Huánuco, más del 85 % de las viviendas son
  autoconstruidas." The Housing Ministry's proposed national policy would offer
  "herramientas y asistencia técnica para la construcción asistida" by certified
  professionals, especially for progressive (incremental) housing. — Andina
  (state news agency) reporting the Ministerio de Vivienda, 25 June 2021,
  https://andina.pe/agencia/noticia-ministerio-vivienda-alista-propuesta-para-reducir-riesgos-viviendas-autoconstruidas-850718.aspx,
  **primary/official statement via state agency**. A 2023 GRADE study reported
  by Caretas found 69 per cent of Lima's dwellings built without technical
  advice and 24 per cent of those structurally very precarious _(secondary)_.

What these numbers say together: for the middle class of rich countries and for
the poor of poor countries alike, the house is the one large thing a family
owns, the thing it borrows against, retires on and leaves behind. It is also, in
most of the world, the one large thing that nobody records.

## 2. A house that remembers

### 2.1 Japan: the house history ("ie-karute") and the long-life housing law

Japan built the most complete system found. The idea came from the "200-year
housing" policy of 2006–2008; the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport
and Tourism (MLIT) ran a house-history study committee in 2007–2009; and in May
2010 the Housing History Information Accumulation and Utilisation Promotion
Council (住宅履歴情報蓄積・活用推進協議会) was founded as the management body
for the private service organisations that keep the records. By September 2018:
48 member service organisations, about 15,000 participating builders, and
"住宅履歴情報登録蓄積数 累計 357万棟" (3.57 million dwellings with a house
history on file), of which 94,000 had received the national "common housing ID"
(住宅共通ID), a 32-digit hexadecimal ucode issued once per dwelling and
inherited across owners. The Council sets the common rules (personal-data
protection, proper information management) and defines what the record holds: at
the new-build stage the building-confirmation documents (ground survey, permit,
supervision, completion inspection), performance evaluations, long-life
certification papers and as-built drawings; at the maintenance stage the
maintenance plan, inspections and diagnoses, planned and other repairs,
renovations, and "the maintenance records whose preservation is mandatory for
certified long-life quality housing". Its stated purpose is to move "from the
age of building houses to the age of protecting, nurturing and handing them on"
and to raise "the value of housing as a social asset". The Council's own list of
unresolved issues (2019) includes extending the maintenance and record duty
beyond certified houses, keeping inspection records of long-life components, and
"incorporating [the record] into the mortgage". — Council presentation to MLIT's
committee on the long-life housing system, 29 January 2019,
https://www.mlit.go.jp/common/001271071.pdf, **primary/official**.

The legal anchor is the Act on the Promotion of Long-Life Quality Housing
(長期優良住宅の普及の促進に関する法律), in force June 2009, which certifies a
house's design _and_ its maintenance plan and obliges owners of certified houses
to keep records. MLIT's annual count: in FY2024, 136,842 new detached houses
were certified, "39.3%" of new detached starts
("約４割に（５年連続で過去最高）", about four in ten, a record for the fifth
year running); 145,073 new dwellings in all; cumulative new detached
certifications since June 2009: 1,694,243. — MLIT press release, 27 June 2025,
https://www.mlit.go.jp/report/press/house04_hh_001283.html,
**primary/official**. A 2026 release reportedly puts the share near five in ten
_(search excerpt only)_.

Alongside it sit the existing-home inspection scheme (licensed architects who
completed an MLIT training course inspect foundations, walls and water ingress),
the 2018 amendment to the real-estate brokerage law that made inspection
disclosure part of a sale, and the "Anshin R" (安心R住宅) label for inspected
existing homes. — Organization for Housing Warranty, _Housing Warranty Scheme in
Japan_, December 2022,
https://www.how.or.jp/information/docs/Housing%20Warranty%20Scheme%20in%20Japan%20Dec2022.pdf,
**primary (industry body established under MLIT guidance)**; Council slide 9 of
the 2019 presentation above.

**What could not be verified:** any study quantifying the effect of a house
history on resale price. MLIT documents from 2008 list "complete repair and
maintenance history" among buyers' stated needs, and the Council admits that
awareness among owners, buyers and agents "is not yet sufficient" _(search
excerpts)_. Japan built the record and the law; the price effect is believed,
not measured.

### 2.2 The European Union: from a 2020 study to a definition in law

The European Commission's _Study on the development of a European Union
framework for digital building logbooks_ (final report December 2020, led by
BPIE with Ecorys, VITO and R2M) gave the first common definition after a review
of the literature and consultation of 32 experts: a digital building logbook is
"a dynamic tool that allows a variety of data, information and documents to be
recorded, accessed, enriched and organised under specific categories". It
recommended work on data management, interoperability and the legal framework,
guidelines for linking existing databases, and publicly funded research
projects. — https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/growth/items/690184 and
https://www.bpie.eu/news/new-report-from-european-commission-provides-first-ever-common-definition-of-a-european-digital-building-logbook/,
**primary/official (Commission) and secondary (BPIE summary)**.

The recast Energy Performance of Buildings Directive, Directive (EU) 2024/1275
(signed 24 April 2024, in force 28 May 2024), is "the first time in an EU
legislative instrument" that the logbook is defined, in Article 2(41), "as a
common repository for all relevant building data, including building information
tools, such as building information modelling (BIM), policy instruments, such as
EPCs and indicators, such as global warming potential (GWP)". A draft
standardisation request "aims to harmonise DBL formats by 2028", and the
Construction Products Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, Article 75, adds a construction
digital product passport. The same review classifies the national models:
public, centrally managed platforms (Flanders' Woningpas, Estonia's e-ehitus,
Greece's Electronic Building Identity), legally mandated market-driven
frameworks (France's CIL) and permit-tied logbooks (Vienna); in Flanders the
service is free, in Greece and Estonia owners pay fees. Estonia's platform is
the one where "insurers, banks, real estate agents and valuers can access
building information for due diligence processes such as mortgage approvals and
property transactions". The authors are blunt about the state of play: logbooks
"remain highly fragmented and are frequently perceived as abstract and overly
complex". — Gokarakonda and Toth, "A framework for digital building logbooks",
_eceee Summer Study proceedings_, 2026,
https://www.bpie.eu/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/A-framework-for-digital-building-logbooks-Drivers-of-affordable-circular-and-sustainable-built-environment.pdf
_(secondary, peer-reviewed; EUR-Lex returned empty pages, so the Article
2(41) wording rests on this source)_.

### 2.3 Flanders: the Woningpas

The Flemish Government's decree of 30 November 2018 created the Woningpas; the
"light" version launched in December 2018. It is "a unique integral digital file
of every house, accessible to the homeowner", "available for free for all
Flemish house owners" through eID or itsme, with "a record for over 3 million
dwellings"; it bundles the energy performance certificate with renovation advice
toward the 2050 target, permits, inspections, certificates, soil and environment
data; owners may authorise buyers, tenants or architects to view it; and
"personal data is only kept volatile in the platform". The team's first lesson:
"Our first challenge was to get all the noses in the same direction." —
Interoperable Europe (European Commission), _Building passport Flanders:
Woningpas_, last updated 2019,
https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/egovernment/solution/building-passport-flanders-woningpas,
**primary/official (self-description by the public operator)**. In 2025 the
Flemish Energy and Climate Agency added a collective Woningpas for the shared
parts of apartment buildings, heating-inspection notices, fully integrated
environmental permits, and the "Vastgoedinlichtingen voor Overdracht" (property
information for transfer) that a notary or broker prepares for a sale. — VEKA,
https://www.vlaanderen.be/veka/woningpas-en-gebouwenpas-zorgen-voor-digitale-sprong-vooruit-in-2025,
**primary/official**. User counts were not published on either page.

### 2.4 France: the carnet d'information du logement (CIL)

Article 167 of Loi n° 2021-1104 of 22 August 2021 (Climat et Résilience)
inserted articles L. 126-35-2 to L. 126-35-11 into the Code de la construction
et de l'habitation. The carnet applies to building permits and prior
declarations filed from 1 January 2023 and to energy-relevant renovations quoted
or started from that date. "Le carnet d'information du logement est établi et
mis à jour par le propriétaire du logement" (it is drawn up and kept by the
owner); constructors must hand the owner what they hold by completion; for new
builds it contains floor plans and sections, network plans (water, electricity,
gas, ventilation) and maintenance notices; for renovations the dates and
descriptions of works; for both, the materials and equipment that affect energy
performance and the documents proving it; elements are provided "in an open
standard format"; and "Le carnet d'information est transmis à l'acquéreur lors
de toute mutation du logement", no later than the signing of the notarial deed,
with the buyer acknowledging receipt in the deed. — Légifrance,
JORFARTI000043957107,
https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/article_jo/JORFARTI000043957107,
**primary/official**; implementing Décret n° 2022-1674 and arrêté of 27
December 2022. The builders' federation notes that "aucune sanction n'est
prévue" if an owner does not create one and that a missing carnet is not
expected to block a sale. — FFB,
https://www.ffbatiment.fr/actualites-batiment/actualite/le-carnet-d-information-du-logement-est-entre-en-vigueur-le-1er-janvier-2023
_(secondary)_. A report that the Conseil d'État gave a negative opinion on an
earlier version could not be verified.

### 2.5 Germany, the Netherlands, the United States

No statutory house logbook was found in Germany or the Netherlands today.
Germany has the individueller Sanierungsfahrplan (iSFP), a subsidised renovation
roadmap introduced in 2017, which is a plan rather than a record; counts of
issued roadmaps were not found _(secondary)_. The United States relies on seller
disclosure at sale: California's Civil Code §1102 requires a Transfer Disclosure
Statement of known material defects for one-to-four-unit dwellings, delivered
"as soon as practicable before transfer of title", and most states require
written disclosure of known material defects in some form. — Nolo and others
_(secondary legal summaries; the statute itself was not fetched)_. Disclosure is
a snapshot by the seller at the moment of sale; it is not a record that
accumulates.

### 2.6 The United Kingdom's Home Information Packs: the lesson

England and Wales required sellers to supply a Home Information Pack from 2007.
The coalition government suspended the requirement from 21 May 2010 and the
Localism Act 2011 repealed it on 15 January 2012; the Energy Performance
Certificate was kept. The housing minister's explanation, citing Ipsos MORI
research: "more than 90% of home buyers wanted information about a property's
condition", yet "fewer than 1 in 6 buyers trusted that information when the
seller provided it", and experienced buyers preferred "an informal check by a
tradesperson they already knew over a formal condition survey". "People who buy
and sell homes want to know more about their condition... forcing them to swap
bits of information they don't want or trust is a foolish way to try and improve
the property market." — gov.uk, "HIPs failed because no one trusted them", 10
September 2010,
https://www.gov.uk/government/news/hips-failed-because-no-one-trusted-them,
**primary/official**; impact assessment
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/abolishing-home-information-packs-impact-assessment,
**primary/official**. The abolition was also a manifesto commitment made during
a housing slump, so the evidence of "failure" is partly political; but the trust
finding stands and is the most important fact in this section for Trustwork's
design.

### 2.7 Does a record raise value, insurability or credit? What is and is not sourced

- **Sourced:** Estonia's e-ehitus gives insurers, banks and valuers access to
  building data for mortgage approvals (section 2.2). France's CIL is handed
  over at the deed (2.4). Japan's Council names "incorporation into the
  mortgage" as a goal, not an achievement (2.1). NHBC's warranty exists because
  the insurer saw the key stages, and lenders expect it (note E, section 2.2).
  Alabama's insurers give mandatory discounts for a _verified_ FORTIFIED
  designation (section 3.4 below).
- **Not sourced:** any hedonic study isolating a resale premium for documented
  maintenance. The "5–10% premium" repeated on contractor and software blogs
  names no study; it must not appear in the book.
- The honest statement: a verified record changes what an insurer, a lender and
  a buyer can know; where it is verified by a third party (an inspector, an
  insurer's surveyor, a designation body), markets have priced it; where it is
  the seller's own word, buyers have not trusted it.

## 3. Learning from mistakes across generations and across a community

### 3.1 France: Sycodés, forty years of claims turned into prevention

The Agence Qualité Construction (AQC) was created in 1984; "durant les deux
années qui ont suivi sa création", it set up Sycodés (Système de collecte des
désordres), "un appareil photographique de la pathologie dans la construction".
Its founding aim was "d'offrir aux professionnels du secteur un retour
d'informations statistiques sur les origines techniques des désordres... avec
une vision consolidée à l'échelle nationale", so that prevention actions can be
programmed; since 2007 it is also used "pour évaluer l'impact des actions de
prévention sur l'évolution des désordres déclarés". It collects defects that are
the subject of a décennale claim with a repair cost between €762 and €250,000
excluding VAT; a public site shows the data by region and period since 2000. The
2025 observatory (2024 data): "L'eau, sous toutes ses formes, infiltrations,
condensation ou ruissellement, demeure en 2024 la principale source des
désordres identifiés"; "défaut d'étanchéité à l'eau 61% en 2022-2024 (64% en
2012-2021)"; "défaut de stabilité 11% (10%)"; in 2024 the number of Sycodés
reports received "a plus que doublé". The "Flop 10" ranks the ten building
elements with the largest counts and repair costs for detached houses, apartment
buildings and commercial premises over "près de trois périodes décennales (1995
à 2024)". — AQC, _Observatoire de la Qualité de la Construction 2025_,
https://qualiteconstruction.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Rapport-Observatoire-Qualite-Construction-2025-AQC.pdf,
**primary (industry observatory funded by the compulsory insurance system)**;
https://sycodes.qualiteconstruction.com.

The design lesson is the chain: a compulsory insurance (note E, 2.4) produces
claims; claims produce a national defect database with a four-level
nomenclature; the database produces prevention guidance and measures its own
effect. Nobody has to volunteer the data; the insurance system generates it.

### 3.2 Japan: the warranty bodies' defect statistics

Under the voluntary Housing Quality Assurance Scheme (1980–2008), "Defects
related to water ingress accounted for 90% of total defects, of which water
ingress incurred in walls accounted for 75% of total defects. Regarding defects
of structural parts, defects in foundations were the highest at 6%."
Registrations of builders rose sharply "especially after the Hanshin-Awaji
Earthquake Disaster", peaking at 44,000; 140,000 units were covered in 2000, 12
per cent of starts. — Organization for Housing Warranty, December 2022 (URL in
2.1), **primary (industry body)**. Under the compulsory insurance of 2009, MLIT
reported by March 2017 6,567 incidents notified to the insurance corporations,
3,486 paid, ¥3.92 billion in total (average about ¥1.12 million), with
waterproofing about 90 per cent of paid cases. — MLIT document
https://www1.mlit.go.jp:8088/common/001190774.pdf _(search excerpt only; the
server could not be reached)_. The same lesson as France, found
independently: water, then structure.

### 3.3 Denmark: the Building Defects Fund, a levy that buys inspections and shared experience

Byggeskadefonden is "en selvejende institution, der er oprettet ved lov i 1986
pga. dårlig byggeteknisk kvalitet i det støttede boligbyggeri" (an independent
institution created by law in 1986 because of poor technical quality in
subsidised housing), with three purposes: 1- and 5-year inspections, cover of
building damage, and "formidling af erfaringer" (dissemination of experience;
the law's §8 requires building "by use of experience"). It is funded by "1 % af
anlægsomkostninger" paid at handover; about 15 per cent of that pays for the
inspections, the rest for damage cover, experience dissemination and
administration. Its own account of the result: "I starten af 1990'erne var der
alvorlige svigt i over 25 % af nybyggerierne. I dag er der alvorlige svigt i
under 8 % af de nye bygninger." — Byggeskadefonden presentation to BL (the
Danish social housing federation), October 2024,
https://bl.dk/media/l1upwiok/bsf-bl-erfa-moeder-oktober-2024-rev-7102024-241024.pdf
_(self claim by the fund)_. The Danish Building Research Institute called the
fund "the primary source of information on building defects in Denmark"
(SBi, 2010) _(secondary)_.

For private new homes Denmark added a compulsory building-damage insurance in
2008, with 1- and 5-year inspections by the insurers and a secretariat at the
Technological Institute that collects and checks the reports. Its 2024 annual
report: 6,311 five-year inspections completed (76 per cent "copies", that is
repeat-type units) registering 5,595 damages, 29 per cent of them in exterior
walls; 6,898 one-year inspections (78 per cent copies) with 4,169 damages, 24
per cent in exterior walls; 4.7 per cent of all registered damages were eligible
for cover and 6.5 per cent judged significant; insured units fell from about
14,000 a year (2016–2020) to 5,894 in 2024. Importantly: "bebyggelse, hvor
forbrugerne selv står for byggeriet, [er] undtaget fra forsikringskravet"
(self-built housing is exempt from the insurance requirement). — Sekretariatet
for Byggeskadeforsikring, _Årsberetning 2024_,
https://www.sbst.dk/Media/638790079894689045/rsberetning_2024.pdf,
**primary/official**.

### 3.4 United Kingdom: NHBC's inspections, claims and the new ombudsman

NHBC told Parliament's APPG inquiry in November 2015: "just 0.7% of Buildmark
warranty holders per annum experience problems with their homes that stem from
latent defects in the design or construction of the home that constitute a valid
claim"; "less than 5% contact NHBC with issues that result in a valid claim"; it
carried out 750,000 inspections in 2014/15 at "specified key stages for each
plot", with "sites with a higher level of risk... targeted for extra
inspections" using "claims data, Reportable Items and Homeowner Customer
satisfaction"; in 2002 it required a final inspection before cover is confirmed;
over 80 per cent of new homes carry Buildmark and over 1.6 million homes were
then under its ten-year cover. — NHBC submission,
https://www.cic.org.uk/uploads/files/old/nhbc.pdf _(self claim by the warranty
provider, in a parliamentary submission)_. NHBC's own trade material reports
superstructure as about 30 per cent of claims each year, most of it water entry
through defective damp-proof courses and cavity trays _(secondary)_. The New
Homes Ombudsman Service, operating since 2023 for developers registered to the
New Homes Quality Code, reported for 2025/26: complaints up from 593 to 1,802,
82 per cent decided in the homeowner's favour, almost £215,000 compensation, 581
registered developers; defects and snagging were the most frequent issues;
membership is voluntary and about a third of new homes sold fall outside it. —
trade press reports of the NHOS annual report, September 2026,
https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/mortgage-news/2026/09/04/defects-and-snagging-are-biggest-issues-as-complaints-to-new-homes-ombudsman-triple/
_(secondary; the report itself was not read)_.

### 3.5 Germany: counting defects on the owner's behalf

The Bauherren-Schutzbund (BSB), a consumer association for private building
clients, with the Institut für Bauforschung analysed about 700
construction-stage quality inspections on 100 sample projects from 2020 to 2024:
"Im Durchschnitt traten während der Bauphase rund 25 Mängel pro Projekt auf",
plus about six more at acceptance, 31 in all, against 29 in 2019; the examples
are mostly in thermal insulation and external-wall systems. — BSB/IFB study
reported by Baulinks, 20 October 2025,
https://www.baulinks.de/webplugin/2025/1497.php4 _(secondary report of an
association's study)_. This is the "independent adviser" model (section 5.6)
producing statistics as a by-product.

### 3.6 Disasters that became practice

- **Kobe, 1995.** "About 80 percent of the deaths from the Kobe Earthquake were
  caused by the collapse of houses", most of them timber houses built before the
  1981 seismic code. — Fire and Disaster Management Agency,
  https://www.fdma.go.jp/relocation/bousai_manual/e/pre/preparation101.html,
  **primary/official**; Shizuoka's "TOUKAI-0" programme (2001) subsidised
  seismic evaluation, retrofit planning and construction for pre-1981 timber
  houses, about 18,600 retrofits in its first 13 years _(secondary: conference
  paper, 16WCEE 2017)_. Japan's warranty registrations rose after the earthquake
  (3.2), and the 2000 Housing Quality Assurance Act followed (5.2).
- **Typhoon Haiyan, Philippines, 2013.** The Shelter Cluster and government
  agreed eight "Build Back Safer" key messages (strong foundations, tie-downs,
  bracing, strong joints, roofing, site, shape, preparedness) and carpenters
  were trained "village by village" (IFRC). The independent outcome assessment
  (REACH for the Shelter Cluster, May 2016): assisted households "achieved a
  higher level of Build Back Safer (BBS) standards... compared to those that
  relied only on their own resources"; techniques "had become known in more
  detail following the typhoon... due to the extensive dissemination of the 8
  key BBS messages"; "Training for the wider community was reported only to be
  effective when materials were distributed to participants in conjunction with
  the training"; "'Many can quote the key messages, price is the main barrier'
  [Shelter agency]"; and a carpenter "well versed in BBS methods" said
  "ultimately I build what people ask me to build". — REACH, _Typhoon Haiyan
  (Yolanda) Shelter Response Outcome Assessment, Final Report_, May 2016,
  https://repository.impact-initiatives.org/document/reach/9824b43b/phl_report_shelter_recovery_outcome_assessment_may_2016.pdf,
  **primary (independent assessment)**.
- **Hurricane Sally, Alabama, 2020, and the FORTIFIED standard.** The Alabama
  Department of Insurance commissioned the University of Alabama's Center for
  Risk and Insurance Research to study more than 40,000 insured coastal
  properties (25,093 conventional, 7,685 built to supplemental codes, 7,417
  FORTIFIED): FORTIFIED construction reduced claim frequency "by at least 55%
  and as much as 74%" at all levels studied, severity by 15–40 per cent and loss
  ratios by 51–72 per cent; Alabama had "more than 51,000" FORTIFIED-designated
  homes, 83 per cent insured in the private market. IBHS's chief executive:
  homes "can be built to survive storms, making them eminently more insurable".
  — Alabama Department of Insurance, 19 May 2025,
  https://www.aldoi.gov/currentnewsitem.aspx?ID=1321, **primary/official**.
  Alabama is the only state with a mandatory minimum insurance discount for
  FORTIFIED homes (reported at up to about half of the wind premium), and its
  Strengthen Alabama Homes programme gives grants of up to $10,000 for
  retrofits, "more than $86 million" and "more than 8,700 homes" by 2023. —
  WBHM/Louisiana reporting, 2023,
  https://wbhm.org/2023/an-alabama-program-helps-residents-stormproof-their-homes-louisiana-wants-to-copy-it
  _(secondary)_. The mechanism to copy: a standard, a third-party designation of
  each house, a public register of designations, an insurance discount tied to
  the designation, and a grant at the moment of the work.
- Nepal's homeowner-driven reconstruction and Nigeria's collapse statistics are
  in note D (1.2–1.3).

### 3.7 What the observatories have in common

Every working system pairs a _money event_ with a _record event_: the insurance
claim (France, Japan), the inspection the levy pays for (Denmark), the warranty
inspection at key stages (NHBC), the designation that unlocks a discount
(Alabama), the grant tranche released after inspection (Nepal). The record is
never asked for as a favour. And every one publishes aggregates, not addresses.

## 4. Free, quality learning for homeowners and self-builders

### 4.1 Free public programmes that exist

- **FEMA, _Homeowner's Guide to Retrofitting_ (P-312, third edition 2014).**
  Written "for homeowners who have little or no knowledge of flood protection
  methods or building construction techniques"; downloadable free, and "free
  hard copies... may also be ordered by calling the FEMA Publication Warehouse".
  — https://www.fema.gov/homeowners-guide-retrofitting, **primary/official**.
  FEMA's broader library of free homeowner guidance (wind, flood, earthquake) is
  the model of a public body writing for the layperson.
- **Australia, _Your Home_.** First edition 2001, developed for the Australian
  Government by UTS's Institute for Sustainable Futures; "every Council in
  Australia was to receive a copy"; now in its sixth edition (2021), "the
  product of a 20-year partnership between the Australian Government and the
  building, design and research communities", free online, aimed "at ordinary
  householders as well as building professionals". — https://www.yourhome.gov.au
  and CSIRO/UTS notices, **primary/official**; readership figures were not
  published.
- **UNAM, _Cómo autoconstruir tu vivienda_ (Coursera).** A Spanish-language
  course by the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México on preparing the site
  and building foundations safely: "159,540 already enrolled", about 14 hours of
  material, rated 4.8 from 3,366 reviews; the certificate is paid, the content
  is open. — https://www.coursera.org/learn/como-autoconstruir-tu-vivienda,
  **primary (platform page)**. This is direct evidence that self-builders in
  Spanish-speaking countries will take a free, practical course in their
  language in large numbers.
- **Habitat for Humanity homeowner education.** Future owners earn sweat-equity
  credit "as they learn about their mortgage, insurance, maintenance, home
  safety and more"; hours and class counts are set by each local affiliate (one
  Florida affiliate: 20 home-buyer education classes; Twin Cities moved from 100
  hours to a "Volunteer and Learn" model). — https://www.habitat.org/node/12769
  and affiliate pages, **primary (self-description)** _(figures vary by
  affiliate)_.
- **Build Change's Technical Support Centres, Patrimonio Hoy's promoters,
  Nepal's municipal engineers, ILO recognition of prior learning:** see note D,
  1.1–1.2.
- **Japan's inspector training:** MLIT prescribes the course that licensed
  architects complete to inspect existing homes (2.1), a professional, not
  homeowner, programme.

### 4.2 What makes learning effective: the evidence

- **Money or materials at the step where the lesson applies.** REACH on Haiyan:
  training "only... effective when materials were distributed... in conjunction
  with the training"; "price is the main barrier" (3.6). Alabama: grant plus
  discount plus designation (3.6). Nepal: grant tranches tied to inspected
  stages (note D).
- **A trusted local builder as the teacher.** Haiyan households "learned about
  BBS techniques by watching people they saw as the most skilled local
  carpenters, while they worked on other structures" (REACH, p. 7). The HIP
  research: buyers trusted "an informal check by a tradesperson they already
  knew" over formal paperwork (2.6). Patrimonio Hoy's 200 promoters from the
  communities (note D).
- **The learner's own language, short and practical.** UNAM's 159,540 learners
  in Spanish (4.1). The eight Haiyan messages were designed as "simple
  accessible advice to families". Trustwork's rule that every string exists in
  every engine language (ADR-009) is the precondition.
- **The lesson must survive the site.** A Nepal study of 368 building sites
  found trained masons knew the skills but "the work on the site has not been
  found satisfactory because of the pressure from the supervisor and contractor
  to speed up the works and to minimize the cost" _(secondary: Nepalese journal
  article)_. The Haiyan carpenter: "ultimately I build what people ask me to
  build." The owner, not only the mason, has to be the learner, because the
  owner gives the order.
- **A record of the learning.** Certificates checkable by number (Trustwork's
  learning design) and recognition of prior learning (note D, 1.1) turn a course
  into a credential the next client can see.

### 5.2 Japan: what a fraud taught

The Housing Quality Assurance Act (approved 1999, in force April 2000)
introduced a voluntary performance-indication system, designated
dispute-resolution bodies, and "the mandatory 10-year defect guarantee liability
for the fundamental structure and prevention of water ingress". It was not
enough. After the 2005 scandal of falsified structural calculations, "the sales
company filed for bankruptcy. As a result, the owners of these apartments had to
take out additional loans to buy a new unit on top of having already bought the
one which was built based on the fake structure statement. This issue revealed
that even though sale companies have an obligation to cover defect warranties,
without the appropriate financial resources, consumers are not protected." The
Act for Execution of Defect Warranty Liability (approved December 2007, in force
1 October 2009) "obligates housing business providers to deposit money or
provide complete defect warranty insurance"; in practice about 99 per cent
choose insurance and 1 per cent the deposit. — Organization for Housing
Warranty, December 2022 (URL in 2.1), **primary (industry body)**. A warranty is
a promise; the 2009 law made it a funded promise.

### 5.3 Australia, the Netherlands, the United States

- **New South Wales.** Statutory warranties under the Home Building Act 1989: "6
  years for major defects and 2 years for minor defects" from completion (for
  contracts from 1 February 2012). — NSW Fair Trading,
  https://www.fairtrading.nsw.gov.au/about-fair-trading/our-services/resolving-issues/building-complaints,
  **primary/official**. The Home Building Compensation Fund pays when the
  builder is insolvent, dead or missing, up to $340,000 (IPART 2020 fact sheet,
  **primary/official**, figure may have changed).
- **Netherlands.** Woningborg is described as market leader "with a share of
  approximately 70 per cent of all project-based new-builds", with a completion
  guarantee and a repair guarantee that survives the contractor's liquidation;
  SWK, Woningborg and BouwGarant hold the official keurmerk. — flib.nl, _The
  Woningborg scheme: an introduction_ _(secondary; the site returned 522
  today)_; parliamentary answer ah-tk-20222023-890, **primary/official**.
  Woningborg's 2024 annual report notes a 29 per cent rise in construction
  bankruptcies in 2024 _(search excerpt of a primary document)_.
- **Arizona.** The Residential Contractors' Recovery Fund pays owner-occupants
  harmed by a _licensed_ residential contractor up to $30,000 per claimant and
  $200,000 per licence; "If you were dealing with an unlicensed entity in
  construction in Arizona, you will not qualify" (the Registrar's director); in
  a 2025 pool-builder collapse eight homeowners shared the $200,000 cap at
  $25,000 each and others recovered nothing. — A.R.S. §32-1132 and news reports
  (FOX 10, azfamily, 2025) _(secondary)_. Licensing protects; it also draws the
  line of who is protected.

### 5.4 Kenya and Peru: what families in self-build markets lack

- **Kenya.** The National Construction Authority Act 2011 created a register of
  contractors to weed out "rogue contractors" after collapses; owners must
  register a project within thirty days of awarding a contract to a registered
  contractor; after four collapses the NCA ordered signboards naming the
  contractor and professionals. But "a skilled construction worker or
  construction site supervisor carrying out construction works is exempt from
  contractor registration" (NCA Regulations 2014), which is exactly the person
  who builds most Kenyan homes. No statutory warranty, defect insurance, deposit
  protection or ombudsman for homeowners was found. —
  https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/ln/2014/74/eng@2022-12-31,
  **primary/official**; press reports _(secondary)_.
- **Peru.** Seventy per cent self-built nationally, over 85 per cent in three
  regions (1.5). The state's answer in subsidised building is the _Entidad
  Técnica_ that supervises Techo Propio works (note E, 2.5); outside the
  subsidy, the Ministry's 2021 proposal of "construcción asistida" by certified
  professionals was still awaiting approval (Andina, **primary/official
  statement**). Denmark's own law makes the same point from the other side: the
  compulsory insurance exempts "bebyggelse, hvor forbrugerne selv står for
  byggeriet", self-built housing (3.3).

The pattern is general: every protection in 5.1 assumes a professional builder,
a contract, a record of stages and an institution that can be found afterwards.
A family that builds with a neighbourhood mason, pays cash by the week and keeps
the plan in its head has none of the five pillars, in rich countries as in poor
ones.

### 5.5 Independent advice as protection

Germany's Verband Privater Bauherren and Bauherren-Schutzbund sell owners an
independent expert who checks contracts and visits the site; the BSB's 31
defects per new house (3.5) is the statistic such advisers produce. The VPB
claimed in 2004 that owners who used an expert before signing avoided defects
worth about €16,000 per house _(self claim, old)_. Peru's Entidad Técnica, Build
Change's Technical Support Centres (52,350 homeowner visits in Nepal, note D),
Nepal's municipal engineers and Patrimonio Hoy's promoters are the same role in
different clothes: someone on the owner's side who knows building. The HIP
research showed what owners already do unprompted: they ask "a tradesperson they
already knew".

## What it means for Trustwork

What already exists in the engine's design, as accepted or designed (from
`docs/engine/` and the ADRs; see notes D and E for citations):

- **Plan to people** (`vision.md`, `projects.md`): a family launches a house;
  its agent turns it into phases, trades, permits, budget ranges and risks; plan
  items become consultations and tasks; "finished projects become shared
  knowledge with the owner's consent" ("Permits, diaries and shared knowledge").
- **Execution records** (`execution-and-reputation.md`): delivery, work record,
  acceptance, defect, remediation, obligation, with hashed evidence and
  counterparty confirmation; staged acceptance of hidden works.
- **The build file** (note E, section 8a, a direction of the book): frozen approved plan
  versions, inspections, change records with reasons, licence checks,
  projections to a lender or insurer with the owner's consent, an open Build
  File standard.
- **Reputation passport** (REP-002, verifiable credentials; note D 1.7): a
  lifelong profile that belongs to the person; evidence-based, never bought.
- **Learning** (`learning.md`): short practical courses, certificates checkable
  by number (Demo); authoring, assessments, accreditation (Proposed).
- **Analytics by hub cohort** (`getting-paid.md`, note E section 5): aggregates
  per (country, region, building type, stage) with minimum samples; no
  individual histories outside the parties.
- **Manufacturers as knowledge partners on plan steps**, "because they fit,
  never because they paid" (`vision.md`).

What the research adds, all directions of the book, not yet designed in the
repository:

1. **The house record that outlives the build.** Today the project ends at
   completion. Japan, Flanders and France show the record must continue:
   maintenance plan, inspections, repairs, renovations, energy works, sale,
   inheritance. Design: a project becomes a _house_ at completion; the house
   carries the build file as its first chapter; plan items continue as a
   maintenance plan (the long-life law's 維持保全計画) with dated inspections;
   every later work order, quote and acceptance attaches to the house, not only
   to the trade. The house record is **owned by the family** (not the hub, not
   the builder); ownership transfers with the home by an explicit act at sale or
   inheritance (France hands the carnet over at the deed; Japan's house ID is
   "inherited"); heirs and buyers receive the whole record and the mandates over
   it; the seller cannot edit the past. Export in an open format (France
   requires "un format ouvert"; the EU is standardising logbook formats by 2028)
   so that a family can take its record to a national logbook (CIL, Woningpas,
   ie-karute, a future national DBL) through a country-pack adapter, and so that
   nobody is locked in. One house ID per dwelling, stable across owners,
   generated by the hub and mappable to national IDs where they exist.
2. **Verification, not say-so (the HIP lesson).** Fewer than one in six buyers
   trusted seller-supplied information. Every entry in the house record must
   show _who_ recorded it and _who_ confirmed it (the mason, the inspector, the
   insurer's surveyor, a counterparty), with hashes and dates; owner-only
   entries are shown as owner-only. A buyer or insurer reading the record sees
   evidence levels, not a brochure. No "house score".
3. **Learning tied to plan steps and to money moments.** The evidence says
   learning sticks when it arrives with the step and the payment: show the short
   lesson (foundations, damp-proof course, roof tie-downs, the hidden-work
   acceptance) on the plan item itself, in the user's language, before the stage
   is paid; let the owner's agent ask "what should I check before I accept this
   stage?"; offer the certificate checkable by number to the mason who completes
   it (recognition of prior learning, note D). Courses stay free; the
   certificate is the record. Public guides (FEMA, Your Home, UNAM) are
   possible sources for curated, sourced course content with attribution, never
   implied endorsement.
4. **Protection designed into the plan.** At the step where a family chooses a
   builder, show the licence or register check from the country pack (Kenya's
   NCA register, Arizona's ROC, France's décennale certificate), what statutory
   warranty applies and for how long (6+2, 10, 2+8), whether deposit protection
   exists, and where disputes go. Where none exists (self-build markets), say so
   plainly and offer what Trustwork can: staged acceptance with evidence,
   payment through licensed escrow or staged providers (Trustwork never holds
   money), an independent adviser, and a record that will exist afterwards.
5. **The independent adviser as a first-class role.** An _owner's adviser_
   (Entidad Técnica, Bauherrenberater, Technical Support Centre engineer, a
   retired master builder) with a mandate from the family to inspect stages,
   record defects and confirm acceptances. Their confirmations are evidence;
   their reputation accrues from evidence like anyone else's. Hubs may recruit
   such advisers from associations, universities and training bodies; the role
   is paid by the family or by a programme, never by the builder.
6. **A consented defect observatory per hub (Sycodés for the self-build
   world).** Defects and remediations already exist as execution records. With
   the owner's consent at completion (already in the design), a hub publishes
   aggregates by region, building type, stage and element, with minimum cell
   sizes and no addresses: the first defect statistics most countries have ever
   had for private houses. This is the public good insurers and lenders need
   (note E, section 5) and the feedback loop that lets a hub measure whether its
   courses reduce defects (AQC has used Sycodés this way since 2007). Minimum
   rules: consent is per project and revocable for future use; cells below the
   minimum are suppressed; re-identification in small towns is tested; the
   family's record is never the statistic.
7. **Insurance and finance read the record; they do not own it.** Estonia lets
   banks and insurers read the building file for due diligence; Alabama
   discounts a verified designation; Japan wants the record "in the mortgage".
   Trustwork's build file projection (note E) extends to the house record: a
   family may grant a lender or insurer a time-limited read of defined fields.
   Trustwork never lends, insures, advises on credit or sells placement (note E,
   6.1).
8. **Country packs carry the protection facts.** For each country: the statutory
   warranty, the insurance obligation and its exemptions (Denmark exempts
   self-build; France exempts the private builder from penal sanction), the
   register to check, the recovery fund and its caps, the dispute body, the
   national logbook if any, each with a source. These are legal facts for
   counsel to confirm, shown as facts, not advice.
9. **What Trustwork must not do here.** No score of a house or a family; no
   resale-premium promise; no selling of the house record or of aggregates; no
   obligation to share; no hub access to a family's private record (private
   stays private, including from staff); no fake "verified" badge without a
   named confirmer.

## Key findings, sourced

Fifteen statements, each with its source; "we" is Trustwork.

1. For most families the house is not one asset among several. It is the asset.
   In OECD countries owner-occupied housing is half of all household wealth and
   more than 60 per cent of middle-class wealth (OECD, _Housing Taxation in OECD
   Countries_, 2022).
2. In the euro area, 61.7 per cent of households own their home; its median
   value, €191,600, is larger than the median household's entire assets (ECB
   HFCS 2021 wave, 2023).
3. In the United States the median homeowner is worth $396,200 and the median
   renter $10,400 (Federal Reserve, Survey of Consumer Finances 2022). Ownership
   is the line between two wealth worlds.
4. The house is what families leave. British estates under £1 million "are
   likely to consist mainly of residential property and cash" (HMRC, 2023–24),
   and most OECD countries exempt the family home from inheritance tax so widely
   that most estates pay nothing (OECD, 2021).
5. For the poor of the developing world "land and housing are the most important
   assets" (World Bank), built in stages from savings and remittances, mostly
   without a plan on paper, a professional or a record. In Peru about 70 per
   cent of homes are self-built (Ministerio de Vivienda, 2021).
6. A house can remember. Japan gives each dwelling a lifelong ID and a house
   history; 3.57 million houses had one by 2018, and four in ten new detached
   houses are now certified under a law that obliges owners to keep maintenance
   records (MLIT, 2019 and 2025).
7. Europe is writing the same idea into law: Flanders' free Woningpas holds a
   file for over 3 million dwellings; France's carnet d'information du logement
   is kept by the owner and handed to the buyer at the deed; the EU defined the
   digital building logbook in law in 2024 (Interoperable Europe, Légifrance,
   Directive 2024/1275).
8. A record is worth what its verification is worth. England's Home Information
   Packs failed because more than 90 per cent of buyers wanted condition
   information and fewer than one in six trusted it when the seller provided it
   (UK Government, 2010). We record who confirmed every entry.
9. Buildings fail in the same ways everywhere, and mostly from water. In France
   61 per cent of insured defects are water-tightness failures (AQC Sycodés,
   2025); in Japan water ingress was 90 per cent of warranty defects
   (Organization for Housing Warranty). A country that counts its defects can
   teach its builders.
10. Denmark created a defects fund by law in 1986, financed by 1 per cent of
    construction cost, inspecting every subsidised building at one and five
    years and publishing what it learns; serious failures fell from over 25 per
    cent of new buildings to under 8 per cent (Byggeskadefonden, 2024, its own
    figures).
11. Lessons from disasters become practice when a standard, a verified
    designation, an insurance discount and a grant arrive together: Alabama's
    51,000 FORTIFIED homes cut hurricane claim frequency by 55 to 74 per cent
    (Alabama Department of Insurance, 2025).
12. People learn building from a builder they trust, in their own language, at
    the step where the money is spent. After Typhoon Haiyan "many can quote the
    key messages, price is the main barrier", and training worked "only... when
    materials were distributed... in conjunction with the training" (REACH,
    2016). A free Spanish self-build course has 159,540 learners (UNAM on
    Coursera).
13. A warranty is a promise; a funded warranty is protection. Japan learned this
    when a fraud left buyers paying two mortgages and, in 2009, made insurance
    or a deposit compulsory for every new home (Organization for Housing
    Warranty).
14. Every protection that rich markets offer, a registered builder, a statutory
    warranty, inspected stages, protected deposits and a dispute body,
    presupposes a professional and a record. A family building with a
    neighbourhood mason has none of them, in Nairobi or in Arizona, where only
    victims of licensed contractors can claim (NCA Regulations 2014; A.R.S.
    §32-1132).
15. So the house record is the family's, not ours: it begins with the plan,
    continues through every repair, passes with the deed and the inheritance, is
    read by an insurer or a lender only when the family says so, and joins the
    hub's statistics only with consent and only as a number among many. That is
    how a house accumulates its mistakes and learns, and how the next family
    learns from it.

Possible closing lines: "A house that remembers is a house that teaches." "The
record belongs to the family. The lessons belong to everyone."
