# F. Open and free: why public bodies and public tenders favour open, free-of-charge engines

Research note for the Trustwork Book, first edition, November 2026. Working evidence, not the book's position.

Feeds chapter 7, "Open and free, on purpose". Access date for everything below:
2026-11-04 unless stated. Evidence levels: **primary/official**, _(secondary)_,
_(vendor/self claim)_. Naming an organisation records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

**Question.** Why do public bodies and public tenders favour an engine that is
open and carries no licence or implementation fee, and what does a free licence
still cost to run?

## 1. Public procurement and open source

**European Union — Interoperable Europe Act.** Regulation (EU) 2024/903, adopted
13 March 2024, in force 11 April 2024, applying from 12 July 2024
(interoperability assessments from 12 January 2025). The rule is specific to
interoperability solutions, not to public software in general: public bodies
choosing an interoperability solution must prioritise those without restrictive
licensing terms, such as open-source ones, where they are equivalent in
functionalities, total cost, user-centricity, cybersecurity or other relevant
objective criteria (Article 4(6)); and they must share, on request, the
interoperability solutions behind trans-European digital public services, with
documentation and, where available, source code (Article 4(1), with exceptions),
through the Interoperable Europe Portal (the former Joinup, transformed in
November 2024). FSFE called the final text "watered-down" and the equivalence
criteria a loophole. Sources: European Commission guidelines on sharing
interoperability solutions, pathway 1 (Article 4(6)),
https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/guidelines-sharing-interoperability-solutions/pathway-1,
and pathway 3 (Article 4(1)),
https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/guidelines-sharing-interoperability-solutions/pathway-3
(**primary**);
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/interoperable-europe-act.html
(EUR-Lex summary, **primary**; dates checked against
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interoperable_Europe_Act _(secondary)_ and
https://datos.gob.es/en/blog/interoperable-europe-act _(secondary, Spanish
government blog)_); FSFE critique via Wikipedia _(secondary)_.

**European Commission open source strategy 2020–2023.** Adopted 21 October 2020
(C(2020) 7149); six principles "think open, transform, share, contribute,
secure, stay in control"; open-source solutions preferred when equivalent in
functionalities, total cost and cybersecurity; software the Commission produces
for outside use is published under the EUPL. It governs the Commission's own IT,
not Member States.
https://commission.europa.eu/departments/informatics/open-source-software-strategy_en
(**primary**);
https://april.org/en/free-software-strategy-european-commission-think-open-not-too-big
_(secondary, advocacy)_.

**Commission study on the impact of open source on the European economy
(September 2021, DG CONNECT).** Estimates EU companies invested about €1 billion
in open-source software in 2018 with an economic impact of €65–95 billion; its
public-sector case studies name three benefits of procuring open source: lower
total cost of ownership, less vendor lock-in, more digital autonomy. No
quantified procurement saving was found in the press release; the book must not
invent one.
https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-study-impact-open-source-european-economy
(**primary** press release; full report not read).

**"Public Money? Public Code!" (FSFE, launched September 2017).** Demand: "If it
is public money, it should be public code as well." Reasons given: collaboration
with other public bodies, independence from single vendors, potential tax
savings, increased innovation, better basis for IT security. Supporters: "over
200 civil society organisations and more than 31.000 individuals" (campaign's
own figure). https://fsfe.org/activities/publiccode/publiccode.en.html (_(self
claim)_ for figures; **primary** for the demand); launch date from
https://lwn.net/Articles/734169/ and FSFE newsletters _(secondary/primary)_.

**India — Policy on Adoption of Open Source Software for Government of India
(2015).** Issued by the Department of Electronics and Information Technology
(now MeitY); makes OSS the preferred option over closed source for government
organisations, with comparison on capability, strategic control, scalability,
security, lifetime costs and support. The policy PDF at meity.gov.in could not
be reached; wording rests on SFLC.in's summary and the department's "Framework
for Adoption of Open Source Software in e-Governance Systems" (April 2015,
v1.0), which states the policy was approved and notified.
https://sflc.in/policies-and-cases/policy-on-adoption-of-open-source-software-for-government-of-india/
_(secondary)_;
https://egovstandards.gov.in/sites/default/files/2021-07/Framework%20for%20Adoption%20of%20Open%20Source%20Software%20in%20e-Governance%20Systems.pdf
(**primary**, not re-read).

**Brazil — Portal do Software Público Brasileiro.** The federal recommendation
to adopt free software became policy in 2003; the portal launched in 2007 to
share software built by or for government; Instrução Normativa No. 1 of 17
January 2011 set the procedures for public software; a government item around
2013 reported over 170,000 registered users and more than 60 solutions; industry
groups objected in 2012. Current status not verified.
https://www.serpro.gov.br/menu/noticias/noticias-antigas/governo-aprimora-modelo-do-software-publico
(**primary**, dated); https://repositorio.enap.gov.br/handle/1/349
_(secondary)_.

**France — Loi n° 2016-1321 du 7 octobre 2016 pour une République numérique.**
Made the source code of software used by public bodies a communicable
administrative document (with security exceptions), following CADA's earlier
position. Consolidated article wording in the CRPA not checked.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loi_pour_une_République_numérique _(secondary)_;
https://cms.law/en/fra/legal-updates/logiciel-libre-objectif-d-ouverture-des-codes-sources
_(secondary, law firm)_; text at https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/489066
(**primary**, not read).

**Germany — ZenDiS and openDesk.** The Centre for Digital Sovereignty (ZenDiS)
was founded in December 2022; the Interior Ministry's "Sovereign Workplace"
became openDesk under ZenDiS in 2024, version 1.0 at the end of 2024; the code
is on Open CoDE; BWI (armed forces' IT house) signed a seven-year framework
agreement. Adoption and savings figures are the vendor's.
https://opendesk.eu/en/about _(vendor/self claim)_;
https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/open-source-observatory-osor/news/bwibundeswehr-chooses-open-source-adopting-opendesk
_(secondary, Commission OSOR)_.

**Germany — Schleswig-Holstein.** April 2024 cabinet decision to make
LibreOffice the standard office suite and move about 30,000 workstations away
from Microsoft Office, Linux to follow; the stated driver is digital sovereignty
and independence from non-EU vendors. The state press release could not be
reached; facts rest on
https://www.theregister.com/2024/04/04/germanys_northernmost_state_ditches_windows/
_(secondary)_. Completion status and savings not verified.

**Denmark — Ministry of Digitalisation.** June 2025: the ministry announced a
move from Microsoft Office 365 to LibreOffice for about half its staff within
months, aiming at the whole ministry by year end, citing dependence on US
vendors; a claim that Windows would be replaced by Linux was retracted by the
original newspaper; a fallback to Microsoft was kept.
https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2025/denmark-digital-ministry-drops-microsoft
_(secondary)_; https://heise.de/-10438942 _(secondary)_.

**France — Lyon.** June 2025: the city decided to progressively replace
Microsoft 365 and Skype with OnlyOffice, Linux and PostgreSQL, framed as digital
sovereignty and local economic benefit.
https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/open-source-observatory-osor/news/municipality-lyon-moves-towards-open-source
_(secondary, Commission OSOR)_;
https://www.theregister.com/2025/06/26/lyon_leaving_microsoft/ _(secondary)_.

## 2. Digital public goods and digital public infrastructure

**UN Secretary-General's Roadmap for Digital Cooperation (A/74/821, 11 June
2020).** Calls for "a concerted global effort to encourage and invest in the
creation of digital public goods": open-source software, open data, open AI
models, open standards and open content. The Digital Public Goods Alliance
(co-hosted by UNICEF and UNDP) followed.
https://www.un.org/en/content/digital-cooperation-roadmap (**primary**);
https://www.un.org/techenvoy/sites/www.un.org.techenvoy/files/Update_on_Roadmap_implementation_July_2022.pdf
(**primary**).

**DPG Standard (Digital Public Goods Alliance).** Nine indicators: SDG
relevance; open licensing; clear ownership; platform independence;
documentation; mechanism for extracting data; privacy and applicable laws;
standards and best practices; do no harm by design (9A data privacy and
security, 9B inappropriate and illegal content, 9C protection from harassment).
Standard text CC-BY-SA 4.0. The page states no version number.
https://www.digitalpublicgoods.net/standard (**primary**). Note D (section 3.1)
judged Trustwork's gaps to be documentation and policy (SDG mapping, data
export, do-no-harm policy), not architecture; applying is free; the book must
not call Trustwork a DPG until listed.

**Global Digital Compact (22 September 2024, annex to A/RES/79/1)** names
open-source software, data, AI models, standards and content as digital public
goods and commits to increased investment in DPGs and DPI by 2030. From research
D (**primary**, https://www.un.org/global-digital-compact).

**50-in-5 (launched November 2023; UNDP, Gates Foundation, Co-Develop, Centre
for DPI, DPGA).** Aims for 50 countries to design, launch and scale at least one
DPI component by 2028; membership reported above 30 by 2026, Rwanda and Kenya
among members (Kenya not re-verified). From note D:
https://www.undp.org/news/11-first-mover-countries-launch-50-5-campaign-accelerate-digital-public-infrastructure-adoption-around-world
(**primary**); 2026 count _(secondary)_.

**GovStack.** Led by Germany's BMZ and GIZ, Estonia, the ITU and DIAL; "based on
the principles of openness and reusability of digital components"; builds
specifications for reusable building blocks and a reference platform as a
digital public good; the site says nothing about cost.
https://www.govstack.global/about/ (**primary/self**). The founders intend an
independent foundation in Estonia (note D, 2025 **primary**).

## 3. Open civic platforms adopted because they were free and open — and the lesson that a free licence is not free to run

**Decidim (Barcelona City Council, 2016; AGPL; Free Software Association since
2019; recognised DPG).** Its own "used by" page: "More than 450 organisations
and institutions have proudly used Decidim", with facts and figures of 574
instances, 36 countries, 366 institutions, 195 organisations.
http://decidim.org/usedby _(self claim)_; DPG registry
https://www.digitalpublicgoods.net/r/decidim (**primary**). Sustainability
(note D): by 2022 two contracted workers were paid by two €50,000 grants and
the association faced a crisis; a diversification plan followed; in
2025, of 15 funding applications 9 were refused and 6 accepted; a framework
agreement with the Generalitat de Catalunya and Barcelona City Council remains
the main source (FOSDEM 2025, **primary/self**).

**Consul Democracy (Madrid, 2015; free and open source).** Its site: "used by
more than 250 cities and organizations all over the world" _(self claim;
https://consuldemocracy.org/)_; the Commission's OSOR case study says more than
150 local, regional and national governments _(secondary)_; a 2018 directory
listing says 33 countries and 100 institutions _(secondary)_. The Consul
Democracy Foundation was established in 2019 "to ensure Consul's continued
maintenance as political support from the Madrid City Council declined"; it has
no developers of its own; Munich "contributes €20.000 annually"; Scotland's
COSLA centralised hosting, deployment and onboarding for 32 councils,
"centralising technical management and costs, lowering the barrier to entry for
local governments", while councils lack capacity to customise; local governments
fund their own features and most did not return to the core; the Foundation
"struggles financially"; "long-term financial stability remains a key concern".
https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/open-source-observatory-osor/document/case-study-consul-democracy
_(secondary, Commission OSOR)_.

**X-Road (Estonia; core released under the MIT licence on 3 October 2016; NIIS
founded 2017 by Estonia and Finland, took over development June 2018; Iceland's
Straumurinn began November 2018; a verified DPG).** NIIS, a non-profit
association founded by the Estonian and Finnish governments, develops and
manages the core; each country runs its own deployment (Estonia's X-tee is run
by the Information System Authority, RIA).
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-Road _(secondary)_;
https://island.is/en/o/digital-iceland/straumurinn-x-road (**primary**,
Iceland); https://niis.org/blog/2018/5/27/changes-in-the-x-road-development
(**primary/self**).

**MOSIP (IIIT Bangalore).** Up to 29 signed country MoUs reported at MOSIP
Connect 2026; the Philippines its largest live deployment; Peru piloting
components (note D; _(secondary)_ and _(self claim)_; "largest" is MOSIP's own
description).

**The lesson.** In every case the licence cost nothing and the running cost
something: hosting, people, adaptation, support and the maintainer's own
survival. Consul's foundation has no developers; councils depend on suppliers;
Scotland pooled costs to lower the barrier; Munich pays a yearly contribution;
Decidim lived on two grants in 2022. Note D's summary: "one funder is a
death sentence on a delay"; "earned income from the people who benefit
(councils, operators, members) is the only income that grows with use". For
Trustwork this is the argument for the book's rule: the hub's owner, on the
country's side, keeps the hub's income, because that income is what pays for the
hub to exist.

## What it means for Trustwork

1. A zero-licence, zero-implementation-fee engine with an open licence meets the
   Interoperable Europe Act's preference for solutions without restrictive
   licensing terms, India's 2015 preference for OSS, and the DPG Standard's
   open-licensing indicator; "equivalent in functionalities, total cost and
   cybersecurity" is the bar an open engine still has to clear on its merits.
2. Public tenders often score the implementation budget: an engine with no
   licence fee and no implementation fee lets the budget line be the operator's
   own costs (hosting, people, local adaptation), which stay in the country.
3. "Free to run" must never be promised. The hub owner's income (commissions as
   hub settings, events, courses, services) is the sustainability model; the
   engine and its implementation carry no fee, so that the hub owner's income is
   not taxed by the maintainer. Contribuda's optional tool for managing a hub,
   and any help a hub owner asks of it, is a separate service on Contribuda's
   own terms, never an engine or implementation fee.
4. The maintainer's own sustainability must come from somewhere not domestic:
   the book's direction is the international layer (deals, transactions and
   reputation across borders), its form to be decided and to be reconciled in a
   decision record with ADR 016 decision 10 ("each hub charges only its own
   participant, with both fee versions disclosed and recorded") and section 6.11
   of the hub exchange architecture (revenue sharing between hubs is outside the
   protocol, never changes ranking, matching or delivery, disclosed on both
   price pages).
5. AGPL §13 keeps improvements made by hubs offered as network services open
   (`docs/oss/LICENSE-DECISION.md`; ADR 009 decision 8), which is how country
   packs, compliance fixes and accessibility work return.
6. The reference hub in San Diego, whose decided operator is Contribuda (ADR 012
   decision 26; today a demo on synthetic data, not deployed), sits inside a
   country; whether it charges commissions and how that fits "everything inside
   a country benefits that country" is to be decided.

## Key findings, sourced

1. The EU's Interoperable Europe Act (Regulation 2024/903, in force 11
   April 2024) requires public bodies choosing interoperability solutions to
   prioritise those without restrictive licensing terms, such as open source,
   where they are equivalent in functionality, total cost, user-centricity,
   cybersecurity or other objective criteria, and to share, on request, the
   solutions behind cross-border public services with their documentation and,
   where available, their source code.
2. India's 2015 policy makes open source the preferred option for government
   software, compared on strategic control, lifetime costs and support among
   other criteria (as summarised; the policy PDF could not be read).
3. France's 2016 Digital Republic law made the source code of software used by
   public bodies a communicable administrative document.
4. Brazil has treated free software as public policy since 2003 and ran a public
   software portal from 2007.
5. Germany founded ZenDiS in 2022 and ships openDesk; Schleswig-Holstein decided
   in 2024 to move about 30,000 workstations to LibreOffice; Denmark's digital
   ministry and the city of Lyon decided in 2025 to leave Microsoft's office
   suite, all citing digital sovereignty (as reported).
6. The Commission's 2021 study names three public-sector benefits of open
   source: lower total cost of ownership, less vendor lock-in, more digital
   autonomy. No saving figure is quoted.
7. "If it is public money, it should be public code as well" (FSFE, campaign
   since 2017; over 200 organisations and more than 31,000 people signed, by its
   own count).
8. The UN Secretary-General's 2020 Roadmap called for a concerted global effort
   to invest in digital public goods; the 2024 Global Digital Compact commits to
   investing in them by 2030; the DPG Standard has nine indicators.
9. Decidim reports more than 450 organisations in 36 countries; Consul Democracy
   more than 250 cities and organisations (self-reported); X-Road's core has
   been MIT-licensed since 2016 and is developed by an institute founded by the
   Estonian and Finnish governments.
10. A free licence is not free to run: Consul's foundation has no developers and
    struggles financially, Munich pays a yearly contribution, Scotland pooled
    hosting for 32 councils, and Decidim lived on two grants in 2022 (Commission
    OSOR case study; FOSDEM 2025).
