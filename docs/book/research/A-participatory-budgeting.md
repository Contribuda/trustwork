# A. Participatory and initiative budgeting, and digital civic participation, worldwide

Research note for the Trustwork Book, first edition, November 2026. Working evidence, not the book's position.

Feeds chapters 12, 15 and 20. Access date for every source: 2026-11-04 unless
stated. Evidence levels: **primary/official** (law, government, programme
operator, peer-reviewed study), _(secondary)_ (press, NGO, encyclopaedic,
conference), _(vendor/self claim)_. All figures are quoted from the source
named; where a figure could not be verified it is said. Russia appears only as
the historical origin of the term "initiative budgeting" (World Bank Local
Initiatives Support Program); it is a restricted place and never a market or a
model. Naming an organisation records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

## Summary

1. Participatory budgeting (PB) began in Porto Alegre in 1989, spread to more
   than 11,000 recorded processes worldwide by 2019, and was suspended in its
   birthplace in 2017 (WRI 2018; PB World Atlas 2019).
2. The best causal evidence is Brazilian: municipalities that adopted PB shifted
   spending to health and sanitation and saw infant mortality fall by 1–3 per
   1,000 live births, with effects appearing only after four or more years
   (Gonçalves, _World Development_ 2014; Touchton and Wampler, _CPS_ 2014).
3. Where PB is mandated by national law without strong civil society (Peru, Law
   28056), it has still increased pro-poor spending and quality of life, but
   field evaluations call it formal and elite-dominated and note that approved
   pro-poor projects "are not always carried out" (McNulty 2013, 2019; Abbott,
   McKiernan and McNulty 2024).
4. Digital PB scales participation (Paris 162,395 voters in 2025; Uzbekistan
   16.13 million votes in spring 2023) and imports a new failure: vote buying,
   fake accounts and coerced voting by public employees, which Uzbekistan has
   answered with disqualifications, dismissals and a 50 per cent co-financing
   fast lane rather than, so far, a published identity-verification design.
5. The common weakest link is delivery. Philippines Bottom-up Budgeting
   completed 27.8 per cent of 59,684 planned projects in 2013–2015 and dropped
   7,928; New York council PB projects take up to five years; Mexico City
   alcaldías reject four of ten proposals and the electoral institute reports
   opacity and negligence in execution; a civic monitor in Uzbekistan found a
   third of 135 Initiative Budget projects finished with defects or not at all.
6. The strongest counter-examples put a citizen signature between the contractor
   and the money: Makueni County, Kenya, pays contractors only after a citizen
   Project Management Committee approves; Dominican law 170-07 creates community
   works committees; Mexico City elects execution and oversight committees per
   project.
7. Co-financing (residents and business paying a share) is the signature of
   "initiative budgeting" and of Kenya's and Kyrgyzstan's community-contribution
   schemes; it raises ownership and raises the risk of excluding the poor
   (Kyrgyz evidence, _Development in Practice_ 2011).
8. Fairer counting exists: the Method of Equal Shares, first used in Wieliczka
   and Aarau in 2023, spreads winners across neighbourhoods and interests
   instead of letting the largest bloc take everything.
9. The leading platforms are AGPL-3.0 free software governed by non-profits
   (Decidim association, Consul Democracy Foundation, Polis), listed as Digital
   Public Goods; Your Priorities is MIT; Go Vocal is open core. None of them
   tracks a build after the vote beyond a status label.
10. Trustwork's existing parts (plans, costed RFQs, sealed quotes, frozen
    agreements, field evidence, staged acceptance, counterparty confirmation,
    evidence-based reputation, OCDS import) map exactly onto the gap every PB
    programme leaves after the vote.

## 1. Origins and evidence: what PB reliably achieves and what it does not

### 1.1 Porto Alegre and the arc of the idea

Porto Alegre's _Orçamento Participativo_ started under the Workers' Party
administration that took office in 1989 and became the model exported worldwide.
The World Resources Institute case study (Abers, King, Votto and Brandão, _Porto
Alegre: Participatory Budgeting and the Challenge of Sustaining Transformative
Change_, WRI 2018,
https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change,
primary/official research) records six outcomes in the 1990s — including the
poor in decisions, breaking clientelism, redistributing infrastructure and
services, building civil society, developing administrative capacity — and four
conditions for them: structured participation, adequate money, political
commitment and flexibility, and government commitment to implement what was
decided. It finds PB worked for small neighbourhood infrastructure and less well
for large projects, that political commitment declined over time, and that the
city suspended the process in 2017.

Infrastructure results are quoted with different baselines by different sources,
so the book should use one metric and name it. A frequently cited pair is sewer
coverage rising from 46 per cent of the city in 1989 to 86 per cent (2007 report
cited by ZNetwork, _(secondary)_), while the World Bank and the Lincoln
Institute give water connections rising from 75 per cent of residences in 1988
to 98 per cent in 1997 (Goldsmith, Lincoln Institute,
https://www.lincolninst.edu/node/43426, _(secondary)_). Participation grew from
628 people in 1990 to about 17,000 by 2002 (SDG16 Plus,
https://www.sdg16.plus/policies/participatory-budgeting-brazil/, _(secondary)_).
A World Bank country report (_Brazil: Toward a More Inclusive and Effective
Participatory Budget in Porto Alegre_, Report 40144-BR, 29 January 2008,
https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/ca9f0984-5ad0-5435-ad75-828abaf06d4d/full,
primary/official) concluded that PB "is a participatory mechanism with
significant potential for pro-poor distributive impacts" while "its ability to
have a positive impact on fiscal performance is less evident."

Brazil's own decline is documented: adoption peaked at 137 cases in 2004
(Spada's Brazilian Participatory Budgeting Census, cited in _Brazilian Political
Science Review_, https://www.scielo.br/j/bpsr/a/Tx6VMmgTLphDZXvCRsccLMq,
primary/peer-reviewed), fell to about 70 programmes by 2016, and dropped by more
than 80 per cent between 2016 and 2019 against about 22 per cent in Portugal
(Lüchmann and Bogo, _Opinião Pública_ 2023,
https://www.scielo.br/j/op/a/C8gMN9mfrBJY86WQ94WdX4N/abstract/?lang=en,
primary/peer-reviewed). Bezerra and Junqueira attribute it to fiscal laws that
shrank municipal investment room; municipalities with less budget manoeuvre
abandon PB or never adopt it. Lesson for the book: **PB lives on discretionary
capital budgets; when those shrink, PB dies first.**

### 1.2 The outcome evidence

- **Gonçalves, S. (2014), "The Effects of Participatory Budgeting on Municipal
  Expenditures and Infant Mortality in Brazil", _World Development_ 53: 94–110**
  (https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:wdevel:v:53:y:2014:i:c:p:94-110,
  primary/peer-reviewed). Brazilian municipalities 1990–2004. Adopters raised
  health and sanitation from about 13 to 25 per cent of spending; infant
  mortality fell by 1–3 per 1,000 live births (10–20 per cent of the 2004 rate).
  Effects are larger and significant only for municipalities with PB for more
  than four years.
- **Touchton, M. and Wampler, B. (2014), "Improving Social Well-Being Through
  New Democratic Institutions", _Comparative Political Studies_ 47(10):
  1442–1469** (https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/polsci_facpubs/146/,
  primary/peer-reviewed). Twenty years of Brazil's largest cities: PB is
  associated with higher health spending, more civil society organisations and
  lower infant mortality, strengthening "dramatically" the longer the programme
  runs and under Workers' Party mayors. Their 2019 follow-up (114
  municipalities, 2009–2016) ties the mortality effect to broader participation,
  more deliberation and embedding in ongoing policy venues.
- **Wampler, B., McNulty, S. and Touchton, M. (2021), _Participatory Budgeting
  in Global Perspective_, Oxford University Press**
  (https://www.peoplepowered.org/resources-content/participatory-budgeting-global-perspective,
  _(secondary summary of a primary book)_). Six types: empowered democracy and
  redistribution; deepening democracy through community mobilisation; mandated
  by national government; digital PB; social development and accountability;
  efficient governance. The social development and accountability type is now
  the most common in the Global South and rural areas. Their 2023 review
  (_Journal of Public Budgeting, Accounting & Financial Management_) finds
  well-being effects in Brazil, Peru and South Korea.
- **Abbott, J., McKiernan, K. and McNulty, S. (2024), "Technocracy for the
  People?", _Comparative Political Studies_ 57(2): 187–220**
  (primary/peer-reviewed, via
  https://scite.ai/authors/stephanie-mcnulty-eP8ZOm). Peru's nationally mandated
  PB increased pro-poor spending and improved quality of life, which the authors
  attribute to reduced information asymmetries made possible by an autonomous
  central agency (the Ministry of Economy and Finance), not to strong civil
  society.
- **Baiocchi, G. and Ganuza, E., "Participatory Budgeting as if Emancipation
  Mattered", _Politics & Society_**
  (https://oidp.net/en/publication.php?id=1621, primary/peer-reviewed). PB has a
  communicative dimension (meetings, deliberation) and an empowerment dimension
  (a real link from meeting to decision). Only the communicative part travelled
  in PB's global expansion.
- **Sintomer, Y., Herzberg, C. and Röcke, A. (2008), "Participatory Budgeting in
  Europe: Potentials and Challenges", _IJURR_ 32(1): 164–178**
  (https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ijurrs:v:32:y:2008:i:1:p:164-178,
  primary/peer-reviewed). Six European ideal types; only "Porto Alegre adapted
  for Europe" and "community funds" allow empowered participatory governance.
- **Wampler, B. (2007), _Participatory Budgeting in Brazil: Contestation,
  Cooperation, and Accountability_, Penn State UP**
  (https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/fac_books/75, primary). Eight
  municipalities; success depends on mayoral incentives to delegate, how civil
  society responds, and the rules of delegation.

**What PB reliably achieves** (where the evidence is strongest): a shift of
capital spending toward basic services for the poor; measurable health effects
after four or more years; growth of local associations; a visible pipeline from
an idea to a budget line that residents can read. **What it does not reliably
achieve:** fiscal discipline; large infrastructure; implementation (see section
5); persistence across political cycles; equal participation by the poorest; and
anything at all when the money is token. The OECD's 2024 trust survey adds the
general correlation that people who feel they have a say report trust in
national government at 69 per cent against 22 per cent for those who do not
(OECD, cited at
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/tackling-civic-participation-challenges-with-emerging-technologies_ec2ca9a2-en/full-report/component-4.html,
primary/official) — a correlation, not an evaluation of PB.

### 1.3 Scale of the field

The _Participatory Budgeting World Atlas_ (Dias, Enríquez and Júlio,
Oficina/Epopeia, Portugal, 2019; 2020/21 edition 2022;
https://oficina-atlas.onesource.pt/en/pb-world-atlas-2019, primary/research
compilation, "always an approximation") lists more than 11,000 PB experiences;
South America holds about 3,061–3,081 (about 26 per cent). WRI quotes "more than
2,700 governments". The Participatory Budgeting Project (US and Canada)
publishes cumulative counters on its home page, but they did not render in the
fetched page and could not be verified (https://www.participatorybudgeting.org/,
_(vendor/self claim)_). People Powered (https://www.peoplepowered.org,
_(secondary, network)_) runs the global hub of participation practitioners and
publishes the Digital Participation Tool Ratings described in section 4.

## 2. Scale and national models

### 2.1 Europe

**Portugal.** Lisbon adopted PB on 9 July 2008, the first European capital to do
so; it allocated over EUR 31 million across 105 projects (Participedia,
https://participedia.net/case/4967, _(secondary)_). The programme was suspended
from 2021 "to re-evaluate the model after the pandemic" (civic manual, LinkedIn,
_(secondary, weak)_); a 13th edition with EUR 2 million, below the EUR 2.5
million of the three preceding editions, was proposed in 2025 (Câmara proposal,
https://lisboaparapessoas.pt/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/oplisboanovo_propalt.pdf,
primary/official). The 2019 Lisbon edition drew 303,208 votes (ECO,
https://eco.sapo.pt/2019/05/09/..., _(secondary)_). Cascais, running since 2011,
reports more than EUR 15.8 million in 88 projects and 219,000 aggregate votes
over six editions (Cascais 2017 book,
https://cascais.pt/sites/default/files/anexos/gerais/new/2017_op_livro_portugues_final.pdf,
primary/official) and calls itself "the largest and most voted PB in the
country" _(self claim)_. **Portugal's national PB (OPP)**, the first national PB
in the world, ran in 2017 with EUR 3 million and in 2018 with EUR 5 million; the
2018 edition recorded 119,703 votes (Government of Portugal,
https://portugal.gov.pt/gc21/comunicacao/comunicados/numero-de-propostas-subiu-quase-30-na-2-edicao-da-opp,
primary/official); proposals were gathered in more than 50 regional meetings and
voting was online and by SMS (AMA materials via OECD OPSI,
https://oecd-opsi.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Communication-and-info-materials_03OUT18_OPP.pdf,
primary/official). A presentation by Nelson Dias lists 75,357 voters (2017),
78,449 (2018) and 69,766 (2019) _(secondary)_. The State Budget Law for 2019
centralised EUR 5 million in the Ministry of Finance to continue executing 2018
projects (SAPO, _(secondary)_). **Whether a national edition ran after 2019
could not be verified**; the opp.gov.pt domain did not resolve on 2026-11-04.
Treat the national programme as dormant unless confirmed. Portugal also ran a
schools PB (OP Escolas, 5th edition 2021) and a youth PB (OPJP 2019, EUR
500,000).

**Poland.** Two instruments. The **village fund (fundusz sołecki)**, Act of 2009
amended 2014, lets rural residents decide small sums at the sołectwo level with
a partial state refund; in 2023, 1,522 of 2,477 gminas created it, about 70 per
cent of those with sołectwa (Sejm interpellation 484 and MSWiA report,
https://api.sejm.gov.pl/sejm/term10/interpellations/484/body, primary/official).
The **civic budget (budżet obywatelski)** became **mandatory for the 66 cities
with powiat status** by the Act of 11 January 2018, which added article 5a to
the Act on Municipal Self-Government: at least 0.5 per cent of the
municipality's expenditure in the last budget execution report (art. 5a(5),
consolidated text Dz.U. 2024 poz. 1465;
https://lexlege.pl/ustawa-o-samorzadzie-gminnym/art-5a/, primary/official).
Turnout: Poland's Supreme Audit Office (NIK) found an average of 18 per cent of
eligible residents voted for the 2016–2018 budgets and called it low, while
judging PB "an effective tool" with violations (NIK via money.pl,
_(secondary)_). Identity: cities verify by PESEL number or a city-issued
identifier (Kraków 2026 rules, primary/official); Wrocław filed a criminal
complaint after implausible PESEL ages (many 90–99-year-olds on the last day)
(City of Wrocław,
https://www.wroclaw.pl/rozmawia/miasto-sklada-zawiadomienie-do-prokuratury-w-sprawie-anomalii-przy-glosowaniu-wbo,
primary/official); in Gdańsk 2019 journalists voted with the mayor's PESEL
because nothing checked the number belonged to its holder (Bezprawnik,
_(secondary)_). The Ombudsman asks cities to prevent double voting while
collecting the least personal data (RPO,
https://bip.brpo.gov.pl/pl/content/rpo-budzety-obywatelskie-glosowania-dane-osobowe-puodo-odpowiedz,
primary/official). Wieliczka's "Green Million" (2023) was the first use of the
Method of Equal Shares in Poland (University of Warsaw,
https://en.uw.edu.pl/?p=32975, primary/official research).

**France: Paris.** Since 2014 the city set aside about 5 per cent of its
investment budget, about EUR 100 million a year, for resident-chosen projects, a
half-billion-euro envelope for 2014–2020 (Urbact,
https://archive.urbact.eu/sites/default/files/490_Paris_GPsummary.pdf,
_(secondary)_). The scheme was reworked in 2020–2021 and adopted **majority
judgment** voting from 2021 (Linfodurable,
https://www.linfodurable.fr/pour-son-budget-participatif-la-mairie-de-paris-teste-le-jugement-majoritaire-28518,
_(secondary)_). Turnout: 142,122 voters in 2022 (+33 per cent on 2021), about
143,000 in 2024 choosing 121 of 235 projects, and **162,395 voters in 2025**
choosing 104 of 261 projects at 505 ballot boxes and online, open from age 7
without a nationality condition (Ville de Paris,
https://www.paris.fr/pages/budget-participatif-2025-decouvrez-les-104-projets-laureats-de-la-11e-edition-32684,
primary/official). Paris publishes an open dataset of winning projects "from
planning to completion" with status stages (data.gouv.fr,
https://data.gouv.fr/en/datasets/budget-participatif-les-operations-des-projets-laureats-jusqua-leurs-realisations,
primary/official, updated 3 November 2026). A study of the 4,043 projects
financed 2014–2021 (Côme, Magne and Steyer, HAL hal-04459732, 2023,
primary/peer-reviewed) finds that the words in a project's title are associated
with the budget it receives; the HAL page was behind bot protection and only the
abstract could be read. The 2021 reform's exact envelope (figures of EUR 75
million a year and 25 per cent of investment circulate) could not be verified.

**Spain: Madrid and Barcelona.** **Decide Madrid** launched in 2015 on Consul
with EUR 100 million a year (EUR 30 million city-wide, EUR 70 million by
district) (Ayuntamiento de Madrid,
https://diario.madrid.es/blog/notas-de-prensa/madrid-destina-100-millones-de-euros-para-la-tercera-edicion-de-presupuestos-participativos/,
primary/official). Reports note difficulty handling the volume of proposals,
delays and frustration; after the 2019 change of government the budget was
halved, 182 approved proposals were discarded, cycles became biennial "to give
the administration more time to evaluate and execute", and a preliminary
feasibility filter was added (Consul Democracy, "Made with Consul: Madrid", June
2026, https://consuldemocracy.org/2026/06/made-with-consul-madrid/,
_(vendor/self claim)_; Democracy Technologies, _(secondary)_). Worked examples
of delivery time: a pedestrian bridge proposed February 2016, selected 2017,
built December 2018; an elevator proposed 2018, built 2024–2026. **Barcelona**
ran its first city PB on Decidim in 2020–2021: announced at EUR 75 million, cut
to EUR 30 million so that projects could be finished before the end of the term
in 2023; 1,982 proposals, 823 technically validated, 184 put to vote, 76
winners, 39,433 voters casting 55,076 votes, 64,571 participants in all (Decidim
case study, https://decidim.org/case-studies/barcelona-budgeting/, _(vendor/self
claim)_; betevé, _(secondary)_). The second edition (2025) had 48,796
participants and 211,812 votes (Ajuntament de Barcelona,
https://ajuntament.barcelona.cat/participaciociutadana/en/noticia/1050842,
primary/official). A completion count for the 76 projects could not be found.

**Scotland.** A 2017 Scottish Government–COSLA framework set the goal that at
least **1 per cent of council budgets** be decided by PB ("Community Choices")
by 2020/21, covering revenue and capital. COSLA reported the milestone reached
in 2021/22 (GBP 154 million, 1.4 per cent, 13 councils meeting it) and again in
2022/23 (GBP 152 million, 1.3 per cent, 14 councils) (COSLA figures in a
Scottish Government FOI release,
https://www.gov.scot/publications/foi-202500489708/, primary/official; Scottish
Government progress page,
https://www.gov.scot/publications/open-government-participation-commitment-milestone-progress/pages/participatory-budgeting/,
primary/official). The 2016–2018 evaluation warned that resourcing and strategic
framing were needed for the approach to last
(https://www.gov.scot/publications/evaluation-participatory-budgeting-activity-scotland-2016-2018-2/pages/1/,
primary/official). Caveat: much of the "1 per cent" is mainstream budget
consulted on rather than allocated by vote; how the measurement is done matters.

**Ukraine.** Kyiv's public budget (Hromadskyi biudzhet) ran from 2016; the 2022
competition (sixth) had UAH 200 million, 393 winning projects worth UAH 199.9
million, and 276,793 voters casting 726,519 votes in 2021, with a 2021 rule of
one vote per theme across ten themes to spread winners; UAH 880 million was
allocated over five years (Kyiv City State Administration,
https://kyivcity.gov.ua/news/finansuvannya_gromadskogo_byudzhetu_kiyeva_na_2022_rik_zbilsheno_do_200milyoniv_griven/,
primary/official; Glavcom, _(secondary)_). Nationally, PB was suspended after
the full-scale invasion: 13 competitions in 2022 against 134 in 2021; by
November 2023 only 8 of 100 studied cities held a PB for 2023–2024 and Kyiv was
not among them; in Lviv, Dnipro and Uzhhorod funding of already chosen projects
was halted (Transparency International Ukraine,
https://ti-ukraine.org/en/news/participatory-budgeting-in-wartime-how-are-cities-involving-citizens-in-spending/,
_(secondary, NGO)_). Ivano-Frankivsk fell from 54,961 voters (2021) to 42,047
(2023) and recovered to 53,459 (2024) (peer-reviewed study, University of
Münster, primary). **Kyiv's status in 2024–2026 could not be verified** (the
gb.kyivcity.gov.ua portal could not be reached). Lviv reported a 40 per cent rise in
votes after moving to BankID authentication (Zaxid, _(secondary)_); Vinnytsia
invalidated only 170 online votes after checks (_(secondary)_).

### 2.2 The Americas

**United States.** Chicago's 49th Ward (Alderman Joe Moore) held the first US PB
in November 2009, with USD 1 million of discretionary "menu money" a year
(Wikipedia and NPR, _(secondary)_). **Vallejo, California** approved the first
city-wide US PB in 2012 after its 2008 bankruptcy, funded from a sales-tax
measure; it is still running (Cycle 8 vote May–June 2023, 2,553 residents; over
USD 9.3 million allocated across six cycles) with low turnout (City of Vallejo,
https://myvallejo.com/pb8 and
https://stories.opengov.com/cityofvallejo/published/LrBAfhEHW, primary/official;
Vallejo Sun, _(secondary)_). **New York City** has two layers: council-district
PB since 2011 (each participating member sets aside about USD 1 million of
capital funds) and the city-wide "The People's Money" run by the Civic
Engagement Commission since 2023 — in 2024 nearly 140,000 residents aged 11 and
over voted, 31,000 online and the rest on paper at sites across the city, in 13
languages, allocating USD 3.5 million of expense funds to 20 projects; over USD
16.6 million allocated since 2023; a fifth cycle with USD 4 million was
announced 15 October 2026 (NYC CEC,
https://www.nyc.gov/site/civicengagement/news/civic-engagement-commission-citywide-participatory-budgeting-vote-results.page
and
https://www.nyc.gov/site/civicengagement/news/cec-launches-fifth-cycle-of-citywide-participatory-budgeting.page,
primary/official). **Cambridge, Massachusetts** has run PB since 2014–2015 (USD
528,000 then; USD 2 million in the 10th cycle 2024; USD 1 million in cycle 11;
USD 1,032,000 in cycle 12 announced 18 March 2026) (City of Cambridge,
https://www.cambridgema.gov/participatorybudgeting, primary/official). Boston
runs the first youth PB in the US with USD 1 million. **San Diego:** only an
older coalition proposal for about USD 3 million of PB in low-income areas was
found (Participedia, _(secondary)_); **no city-run PB in San Diego could be
verified** — a real gap and a real opening for the reference hub. Long Beach ran
a council-district pilot and a "People's Budget" campaign; Oakland allotted USD
100,000 for a seven-month process (MTC fact sheet, _(secondary)_).

**Peru — Law 28056 (2003).** The first country to mandate PB for every regional,
provincial and district government; the law was amended by Law 29298 (2008), its
regulation is DS 142-2009-EF, the 2009 reform cut eight steps to four, and since
2010 the process is "presupuesto participativo basado en resultados" with a
technical team costing and vetting projects, formal agreements (actas de
acuerdos y compromisos) and **Comités de Vigilancia** elected from participating
agents to watch execution (MEF guidance,
https://cdn.www.gob.pe/uploads/document/file/1324482/2017%20Presupuesto%20Participativo%20Basado%20en%20Resultados%20Comite%20de%20Vigilancia.pdf,
primary/official). Evaluations: McNulty (2012, _Journal of Public Deliberation_)
credits broad engagement of civil society organisations; McNulty (2013, SSRN)
finds the process "still dominated by elite actors", approved pro-poor projects
"not always carried out", and no improvement in sub-national conflict or trust;
McNulty (2019, PUCP, "Participación decepcionante") calls fifteen years of the
law largely a formality without real deliberation
(https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2300681;
https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/index/handle/123456789/175005,
primary/peer-reviewed; the PUCP page could not be read and only the catalogue
abstract was read). Abbott, McKiernan and McNulty (2024) nevertheless find
increased pro-poor spending and better quality of life. **Lesson:** a national
mandate plus a competent finance ministry delivers some redistribution even
where participation is thin; it does not deliver trust or completion by itself.

**Dominican Republic — Law 170-07 (13 July 2007).** Creates the Municipal
Participatory Budget System over the 40 per cent of national transfers (Law
163-03) that municipalities must spend on capital works; a 2 per cent reserve
pays for design, execution, monitoring and evaluation; three stages (diagnosis
and pre-allocation by population; community assemblies for communities over 30
families, section assemblies, final Cabildo Abierto; transparency and follow-up
with monthly reviews and semi-annual accountability); **Comités de Seguimiento y
Control** (arts 7–9) and **Comités de Auditoría Social or Comités Comunitarios
de Obras** (art 10) that watch that works follow plan and budget and become
maintenance committees when construction ends; inclusion of the approved plan in
the budget is mandatory (art 12); no sanctions are written (Justia,
https://republica-dominicana.justia.com/nacionales/leyes/ley-170-07/gdoc,
primary/official). Compliance is contested: DIGEPRES reports RD$575.32 million
of PB works executed by local governments in 2017
(https://www.digepres.gob.do/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Ejecucion-Presupuestaria-de-los-Gobiernos-Locales-2017.pdf,
primary/official); a municipal-association director claims only 8 per cent of
alcaldías comply and calls SISMAP's compliance scoring a "scam" (CDN,
_(secondary, undated critic)_). Municipal regulations on SISMAP show the
practice alive in 2024–2026 (Nigua 2024, Miches 2026, Bonao 2026,
primary/official).

**Mexico City.** The 2019 Ley de Participación Ciudadana made PB a yearly
consultation run by the Instituto Electoral de la Ciudad de México (IECM) in
every colonia and pueblo, with an **elected Comité de Ejecución and Comité de
Vigilancia per project** (arts 130–132: the oversight committee verifies correct
use of money, progress and quality of the work) (Guía Operativa PP2025, Gaceta
Oficial,
https://tlalpan.cdmx.gob.mx/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/ANEXO-GUIA-OPERATIVA-PP2025.pdf,
primary/official). The share of each alcaldía's budget is reported as 3 per cent
(adn40, Excélsior) and as 4 per cent, MXN 2,143.3 million in 2025 (Expansión,
https://politica.expansion.mx/cdmx/2025/08/17/..., _(secondary)_), with a 2026
article citing art 117 for 4 per cent; **the exact schedule from 3 to 4 per cent

### 2.3 Asia and the Caucasus

**South Korea.** The Local Finance Act was amended in 2005 to allow PB and
**wholly amended on 4 August 2011 (Act 10991) to make resident participation in
budget compilation mandatory for every local government** (Participedia,
https://participedia.net/case/7431, _(secondary)_; OGP commitment KR0053,
https://www.opengovpartnership.org/members/republic-of-korea/commitments/KR0053/,
primary/official). **Seoul** started its citizen participatory budget in 2012
with KRW 50 billion, decided by a committee of residents and later weighted
votes including m-Voting; e-voting reached about 1 per cent of the population
(_Policy & Governance Review_ 2017, primary/peer-reviewed). The envelope was KRW
70 billion for 2022 (announced 2021) and KRW 50 billion for 2025 and 2026, split
into themed calls ("accompanying the vulnerable" KRW 20 billion, youth KRW 10
billion, free proposals KRW 20 billion for 2026) (Seoul Metropolitan Government,
https://mediahub.seoul.go.kr/archives/2013746 and
https://news.seoul.go.kr/gov/archives/565252, primary/official). A civic group
protested a planned 70–80 per cent cut in 2021 (Asiae, _(secondary)_); the
stated reasons for the reduction could not be verified.

**Uzbekistan — the "Initiative Budget" on openbudget.uz.** Legal basis: a 2018
presidential decree required at least 10 per cent of district budget surplus
revenues for citizen-proposed projects; a 2021 decree set aside 5 per cent of
approved district and city expenditure and at least 30 per cent of additional
revenues; from 2022 every district runs it (Global Voices,
https://globalvoices.org/2023/04/06/the-new-uzbekistan-budget-battle-democracy-in-action-or-a-flawed-system/,
_(secondary)_). The programme is run by the Ministry of Economy and Finance;
citizens propose on the Open Budget portal and vote on the portal, the mobile
app or a Telegram bot, formerly also by SMS. Scale: 2022, 1,418 projects funded
(32 per cent internal roads, 13 per cent school repair and equipment, 12 per
cent street lighting, 8 per cent water and drainage, 6 per cent hospital
renovation); spring 2023, 5,970 proposals, 3,678 received votes, **16.13 million
votes, about a third of the adult population**, 1,666 winners for UZS 1.57
trillion plus a presidential top-up of UZS 1.3 trillion for 1,309 runner-up
projects with over 2,000 votes (Gazeta.uz,
https://gazeta.uz/en/2023/04/10/open-budget, _(secondary)_); 2024 second season,
1,772 winners for UZS 2.139 trillion from 9.081 million votes, the top project
with 14,663 votes and the smallest winner with 52 (Kun.uz quoting the Ministry,
https://kun.uz/en/news/2024/09/02/..., _(secondary)_); 2025 first season, 2,369
winners for UZS 3.2 trillion from 10.45 million votes (Kun.uz,
https://kun.uz/en/news/2025/03/21/..., _(secondary)_); 2026, UZS 6 trillion in
total, cost ceilings of UZS 1.648 billion for construction and UZS 824 million
for equipment projects, a 50-vote minimum to advance (UzDaily,
https://uzdaily.uz/en/uzbekistan-launches-voting-for-2026-initiative-budget/,
_(secondary)_). The President said that despite UZS 7.5 trillion allocated in
one year, not a single project was selected in 2,136 mahallas (president.uz,
primary/official). Rules introduced in January 2025 require each district to
fund at least 10 and at most 30 projects a year and exclude financially
independent universities (Kun.uz, _(secondary)_).

_Controversies and responses._ Vote buying at UZS 10,000–15,000 (about USD
1–1.5) per vote, payment in cooking oil, drinks and phone credit, multiple fake
accounts, and teachers and doctors sent house to house to collect votes were
reported in 2023 (Global Voices; Kun.uz,
https://kun.uz/en/news/2023/03/27/ministry-of-labor-confess-state-employees-have-been-forced-to-collect-votes-for-open-budget,
_(secondary)_). The Ministry of Labour cited the Labour Code's ban on forced
labour and opened hotlines. In March 2025 the Ministry of Economy and Finance
proposed removing a Kuva district school's projects from the vote after
confirming staff were pressured, classified compelled voting as forced labour,
and the Education Minister ended the contract of a principal who demanded UZS
700,000 from each teacher's salary; a Bukhara principal was fined UZS 34 million

https://kun.uz/en/news/2025/03/18/authorities-to-disqualify-projects-with-forced-votes-in-open-budget-initiative,
_(secondary)_; Qalampir, _(secondary)_). The government announced "one citizen,
one vote" and identity confirmation of vote owners to stop bulk SIM-card
purchases for the 2024 cycle (Kun.uz 2024, _(secondary)_); **the actual
verification design (MyID, Face ID or other) could not be verified from a
primary source**. A presidential decree of August 2025 added two fast lanes from

are automatically winners with the state paying the rest (UZS 506 billion,
target 1,000 projects), and each deputy of the Legislative Chamber may name one
or two non-winning projects per year as winners (UZS 496 billion, UZS 3.3
billion per deputy) (UzDaily,
https://uzdaily.uz/en/uzbekistan-updates-initiative-budget-introduces-partnership-based-financing-for-social-projects,
_(secondary)_). The deputy lane is, in design, a step back toward discretionary
allocation.

**Kyrgyzstan.** The World Bank Village Investment Project required community
contributions; a study of 16 rural communities found poorer and marginalised
residents could not always take part in consensus building or freely choose on
the contribution requirement, and concluded that co-financing procedures must be
designed with care to support inclusion (_Development in Practice_ 21(3), 2011,
https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/cdipxx/v21y2011i3p317-329.html,

priorities, agree a joint action plan with the municipality and monitor
implementation, with budgeting training; a committee including the Ministry of

primary/official donor; Times of Central Asia, _(secondary)_). A World Bank
local self-government capacity project (P148471) is closed. **A current national
Kyrgyz initiative-budgeting programme could not be verified.**

**Georgia.** Municipal "civic budgets" run in Zugdidi, Gori, Ozurgeti, Batumi

https://www.opengovpartnership.org/members/ozurgeti-georgia/commitments/geogt0004/,

**Armenia.** Government Resolution 1890 of 2 November 2023 created a subsidy

### 2.4 Africa and South-East Asia

**Kenya — Makueni County.** Devolution under the 2010 Constitution, the County
Governments Act 2012 and the Public Finance Management Act 2012 (s. 125(2))
require public participation in county budgets (Makueni FY 2024/25 participation
report,
https://makueni.go.ke/sandbox/site/files/2024/05/Makueni-County-FY-202425-Budget-Public-Participation-Report-30-4-2024.pdf,
primary/official). Makueni's model under Governor Kivutha Kibwana ran sequential
forums from village to county: 3,867 meetings, over 350,000 participants, 1,000
people setting final priorities, at least 25 per cent of the development budget
decided this way (World Bank blog,
https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/governance/citizen-engagement-kenya-law-practice,
primary/official; Participedia, _(secondary)_). Its distinctive instrument is
the **Project Management Committee (PMC)**: citizens of the neighbourhood,
elected in a public forum for each approved project, who oversee the investment;
**the PMC's formal approval is necessary for contractors to be paid** (World
Bank, _Making Devolution Work for Service Delivery in Kenya_, Box 6.3,
https://issuu.com/world.bank.publications/docs/9781464817267/148,
primary/official). A Projects Monitoring and Tracking System launched in March
2024 publishes projects, takes citizen queries and publishes official answers
(County of Makueni, https://pmts.makueni.go.ke/, primary/official); 26 counties
came to learn the PMC model in 2025 and the State Department of Devolution named
Makueni and Homa Bay model counties (makueni.go.ke, primary/official). The
process continues under the next administration (FY 2023/24 to FY 2026/27 cycles
documented). Evaluations: Touchton and Wampler's World Bank evaluation of
Makueni, Baringo and Elgeyo-Marakwet (2020) finds "encouraging developments in
citizens' attitudes and behaviors" but "some of the results of our pilot
evaluation are less encouraging" and "progress toward accountability through PB
is not uniform" (People Powered summary,
https://peoplepowered.org/resources-content/participatory-budgeting-in-kenya-piloting-new-techniques-for-project-monitoring,
_(secondary summary of primary)_); the companion World Bank note _Participatory
Budgeting in Kenya: Piloting New Techniques for Project Monitoring_ (2020,
https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/471371592554290653/pdf/...,
primary/official) could not be read in this environment (no PDF tools). An APSR
article by the same authors finds Kenya's PB acts "more as technical
policymaking tools rather than deliberative venues" (_(secondary summary)_).

**Philippines — Bottom-up Budgeting (BuB), 2012–2016.** Introduced by Joint
Memorandum Circular 1 of 8 March 2012; national agencies funded projects chosen
by Local Poverty Reduction Action Teams with civil society holding half the
seats; PHP 8.9 billion in 2013, PHP 24.7 billion for 14,324 projects in the 2016
budget, PHP 51.7 billion for 41,156 funded projects over 2013–2015 (DILG, April
2016). Delivery: of 59,684 planned projects in 2013–2015, **16,576 (27.8 per
cent) were completed and 7,928 dropped** (cancelled, unfunded or replaced;
non-feasibility a major factor), with PHP 8.5 billion tied up in dropped
projects; the DILG quoted a 40 per cent completion and 61 per cent delivery
rate; typical timelines were 1–3 months to a funding agreement, 2–3 months of
procurement, about 18 months for water projects and 6 months to 2 years for
infrastructure. The PIDS assessment (Manasan, FY 2015) found civil society
"often only rubber-stamp LGU plans" and that in the 12 study municipalities only
one FY2013 subproject was complete by March 2014 (BusinessMirror, 5 October
2016,
http://www.businessmirror.com.ph/duterte-admin-drops-aquinos-grassroots-budgeting-strategy/,
_(secondary quoting primary PIDS and DILG)_; OGP IRM via Manila Times: only 23
per cent of 2013 projects complete by early 2016, _(secondary)_). The Duterte
administration excluded BuB from the 2017 budget, telling local governments to
fund projects from their Internal Revenue Allotment. **Lesson:** a national
programme that routed money through line agencies to thousands of small projects
could not build what it had promised at the pace it promised, and died with the
government that created it.

### 2.5 Historical origin of "initiative budgeting"

The term comes from the World Bank's **Local Initiatives Support Program
(LISP)**, piloted in Stavropol region from 2007 (regional practice claimed from
2006), with the Bank assisting five regional governments from 2009; it is "a
participatory project identification and financing mechanism… to provide
financial support to microprojects initiated directly by local communities",
with co-financing by residents and local business as a defining feature (World
Bank Social Protection and Jobs Discussion Paper 1931, Shulga, Shilov, Sukhova
and Pojarski, May 2019, https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/hdnspu/137448.html,
primary/official; the World Bank results page of 2014 could not be reached).
Co-financing shares differ by region and year; one World Bank presentation gives
a municipal minimum of 1 per cent and population and business contributions in
cash and in kind, an OIDP document gives more than 3 per cent from residents and
more than 5 per cent from the local budget (_(secondary)_). The model travelled
through PEMPAL and World Bank advice to Central Asia and the Caucasus;
Uzbekistan's programme carries the name. That is the whole of the origin.

## 3. Co-financing, "initiative budgeting" versus classic PB, and professional costing

**Two families.** Classic PB (Porto Alegre, Paris, Madrid, NYC) allocates a
share of the public capital budget by deliberation and vote; the state pays and
builds. Initiative budgeting (LISP lineage, Uzbekistan, Kenya's and Kyrgyzstan's
community-contribution schemes) adds **co-financing by residents and local
business**, often in kind (labour, materials, land), and frames the project as a
joint venture of community and state. Baiocchi and Ganuza's distinction applies
to both: what matters is whether the vote binds the budget.

**What co-financing does.** Evidence for: ownership and maintenance improve when
residents paid part (the LISP literature, Makueni's PMCs that become maintenance
committees under Dominican law 170-07 art 4 §III). Evidence against: the Kyrgyz
study (2011) found the contribution requirement excluded the poor from both
consensus and choice; Uzbekistan's 2026 rule that a 50 per cent citizen
contribution wins automatically favours mahallas with money and makes the vote
irrelevant for them. **Design rule:** co-financing must be optional, in kind
counted at a published rate, capped as a share of winners, and never a condition
for the poorest neighbourhoods to be served. The share paid by local business
should be disclosed, since a business paying half of a road to its own gate is a
procurement decision, not a civic one.

**Professional feasibility and costing.** Every mature programme inserts a
technical gate between idea and ballot: Peru's technical team and
SNIP/Invierte.pe viability; Madrid's preliminary feasibility filter (about 1,700
proposals to about 500 after support to about 350 after feasibility);
Barcelona's 1,982 to 823 technically validated to 184 on the ballot; Mexico
City's alcaldías rejecting four in ten; Uzbekistan's cost ceilings and the
Yuksalish recommendation of public expert review of design documents for
winners. The failure pattern is as consistent: costs are estimated by the
proposer or a desk officer, the winning project is then redesigned (Madrid's
ramp that became an elevator), the real price arrives at tender, and the project
stalls (Yuksalish found seven projects with no or partial funding, five with
insufficient funds to fix defects, three never contracted). **The costing step
is construction expertise, and it is currently unpaid, unaccountable and
invisible.** This is the first place Trustwork's consultations and quotes
belong.

**What works, from the record.**

- _One person, one vote, verified without surveillance._ Poland verifies by
  PESEL and SMS code and still had implausible votes until cities checked age
  distributions; Lviv's move to BankID (bank-verified identity) raised
  participation 40 per cent; Uzbekistan promised identity binding after SIM
  farms. The honest design is a verified-once, pseudonymous ballot: identity
  proven to the hub (or by a bank, a national eID or an in-person desk), the
  ballot stored unlinked, and the roll auditable in aggregate (one vote per
  verified person per cycle). The Polish Ombudsman's rule, "prevent double
  voting while collecting the least personal data", is the right test.
- _Offline and assisted channels._ NYC's 2024 vote was 31,000 online and about
  109,000 on paper at sites and events; Paris used 505 ballot boxes plus online;
  Portugal and Uzbekistan used SMS; Makueni used 3,867 physical forums.
  Digital-only PB measures the connected.
- _Accessibility and age._ Paris from age 7; NYC from 11 regardless of
  immigration status; 13 languages in NYC. Youth and school PB (Boston,
  Portugal, Georgia, Poland) are the cheapest civic education there is.
- _Counting rules._ Kyiv's one vote per theme; Madrid's capped "no" vote;
  Paris's majority judgment; the Method of Equal Shares (Wieliczka and Aarau
  2023; in Aarau it selected 17 of 35 projects against 7 for the greedy method)
  (Harvard EconCS seminar,
  https://econcs.seas.harvard.edu/event/method-equal-shares-participatory-budgeting,
  primary/research; equalshares.net). Proportional rules reduce the "biggest
  school wins everything" dynamic that drives vote farming.
- _Anti-fraud as policy, not only code._ Uzbekistan's disqualification of
  coerced projects and dismissal of a principal did more than any CAPTCHA;
  Wrocław's prosecutor referral did the same. A platform should make the audit
  trail available to the city and to the public (aggregate anomalies by hour, by
  age band, by referrer) and let a rule be enforced: a project whose votes are
  shown to be bought or coerced is removed, not only the votes.
- _What none of them do._ Every platform above ends at a status label ("in
  study", "in progress", "completed") set by the administration. None holds the
  tender, the contract, the site photographs, the acceptance act or the
  contractor's record. Paris publishes an open dataset of stages; Makueni runs a
  separate tracking system with citizen queries; Mexico City publishes alcaldía
  progress reports through the IECM. The build itself is outside the civic tool.

## 5. The weakest link everywhere: delivery

**The pattern.** Votes are fast; concrete is slow; the administration that must
deliver is not the one that promised.

- _Porto Alegre:_ over a thousand chosen projects remained on a long to-do list
  by the 2010s (Juniper Civic, _(secondary)_); WRI names "government commitment
  to implement" as one of four conditions.
- _Paris:_ of 612 projects voted since 2014, 263 were complete in 2018 (Time Out
  Paris, _(secondary)_); criticism of "slowness of implementation" led to the
  2020–2021 rework; the city now publishes a stage-by-stage dataset.
- _Madrid:_ delays and frustration; some winners "turned out not to be
  feasible"; 182 approved proposals discarded after 2019; move to biennial
  cycles; typical build 2–8 years for the three cases Consul showcases.
- _Barcelona:_ budget cut from 75 to 30 million euros explicitly so projects
  could finish within the term.
- _New York:_ 23 per cent of Queens PB projects complete since 2012 with over
  USD 7 million tied up (TimesLedger via QNS, 2016, _(secondary)_);
  implementation "up to five years after funding" (council aide, _(secondary)_);
  the median NYC capital project takes over four years and library and cultural
  projects about seven (Citizens Budget Commission and Center for an Urban
  Future, _(secondary)_); a council member's op-ed: "PB's not to blame, NYC's
  capital process needs reform" (Norwood News, _(secondary)_).
- _Saratoga Springs (NY):_ council declined to release USD 50,000 already
  allocated to PB (WAMC, December 2025, _(secondary)_).
- _Philippines BuB:_ 27.8 per cent completed, 7,928 dropped (section 2.4).
- _Mexico City:_ alcaldías executing late or not at all; residents judging 2022
  projects unfinished; committees in conflict (IECM report via Capital CDMX;
  Expansión).
- _Peru:_ approved pro-poor projects "not always carried out" (McNulty 2013).
- _Uzbekistan:_ a 2022 state audit found violations worth UZS 5.6 billion across
  257 Initiative Budget projects (Kun.uz, https://kun.uz/ru/news/2022/11/25/...,
  _(secondary)_); Yuksalish's August 2023 monitoring of **135 projects found 45
  (a third) with shortcomings**: 16 of poor quality (single-glazed doors where
  double were specified, uneven finishes, substandard kindergarten bedding), 12
  unfinished, 7 with funds not allocated or partial, 5 with no money to fix
  defects, 3 never contracted, a school gym 65 per cent done a year after
  funding, used transformers bought as new (Anhor.uz,
  https://anhor.uz/ru/news/budget-projects, _(secondary, NGO monitoring)_).
  Yuksalish's June 2025 water monitoring (not PB-specific) found 23 of 67
  "completed" sites unfinished and 9 of substandard quality, and district
  offices that "had not kept such records" (Kun.uz,
  https://kun.uz/en/news/2025/06/12/clean-water-projects-in-uzbekistan-face-major-implementation-failures,
  _(secondary)_). Yuksalish's recommendations read like a Trustwork feature
  list: public expert review of design documents, strict deadlines for design,
  tendering, contracting and commissioning, better cost calculation, and
  acceptance only with the facility head and public representatives signing a
  handover act.

**Where citizens verify completion, and what the evidence says about it.**

- _Makueni (Kenya):_ PMC sign-off is a condition of contractor payment; a public
  tracking system takes citizen queries. The World Bank calls it a model; the
  academic evaluation says accountability progress is "not uniform".
- _Dominican Republic:_ community works committees by law (art 10, Law 170-07),
  with monthly reviews and semi-annual accountability; compliance disputed.
- _Mexico City:_ an elected oversight committee per project verifies "correct
  use of money, progress and quality"; the IECM reports conflicts between
  execution and oversight committees as a cause of delay — a warning that two
  volunteer committees without a shared record fight.
- _Peru:_ Comités de Vigilancia with a legal mandate and no budget or technical
  support; "formality" is the common verdict.
- _The randomised evidence (Olken 2007, Indonesia, ~600 village roads, *Journal
  of Political Economy* 115: 200–249,
  https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:115:y:2007:p:200-249,
  primary/peer-reviewed):_ raising the probability of a professional audit from
  4 to 100 per cent cut missing expenditure by about eight percentage points and
  was cost-effective; **grassroots monitoring alone had little average effect**,
  reducing missing wages but not missing materials, and worked only where
  free-riding and elite capture were limited. Engineers independently measured
  quantities to detect what was missing. The lesson is not that citizens cannot
  verify; it is that **citizen verification needs a professional measurement to
  compare against and a consequence attached to the finding.**

**Verdict on the thesis.** The evidence supports it with a condition. PB
platforms stop at the vote; delivery fails for reasons that are procurement and
construction reasons (feasibility, costing, contracting, supervision,
acceptance, payment), and the programmes that do best (Makueni) put a verified
citizen act between the contractor and the money. A tool that carries the
project from vote through tender, frozen quote, agreement, staged acceptance
with evidence, counterparty confirmation and a reputation record would fill
exactly the documented gap. The condition is Olken's: citizen confirmation must
sit beside professional measurement (an engineer's bill of quantities, a sealed
quote, an inspector's act) and must trigger something (payment, a defect record,
a reputation signal). Confirmation without consequence is tokenism with
photographs.

## 8. What it means for Trustwork

The civic mission is a direction of the book, not yet designed in the
repository; nothing below is built. These are the concrete
shapes the research suggests for a PB module and the "loop of public trust",
each tied to existing Trustwork parts.

1. **Make the build the unit, not the vote.** The civic object is a _public
   project_ that lives from idea to acceptance and carries, as versions, the
   idea, the feasibility note, the cost range, the ballot result, the budget
   line, the tender (or the OCDS release imported by PROC-002), the agreement,
   the execution records and the acceptance. Reuses: Projects and plans (plan
   versions), execution records (delivery, work record, acceptance, defect,
   remediation, obligation), the
   `claimed → evidence_attached → confirmed | disputed` state machine, and the
   hub exchange's `procurement_notice` kind.
2. **Professional costing before the ballot, as a consultation.** A proposed
   idea becomes a scoped consultation for a local estimator, engineer or
   architect (the "From plan to people" journey), who returns a feasibility note
   and a cost range with a method version. The estimator is named, paid by the
   hub operator or municipality, and earns reputation from how the final quotes
   land against the range. Reuses: consultations and tasks, the reputation
   builder, "truth is labelled" (a resident's estimate, a professional's
   estimate and a sealed quote look different).
3. **Sealed-until-deadline tenders for PB projects.** The civic tender uses ADR
   027: quotes frozen once sent, sealed until the deadline, no late
   undercutting, the buyer sees who answered but no price. The winning quote
   becomes the agreement both sides can prove identical. This is the honest
   answer to Madrid's "turned out not to be feasible" and Yuksalish's "never
   contracted".
4. **A citizen act between the contractor and the money (the Makueni rule).**
   Staged acceptance of each milestone closes with an inspector's act, site
   photographs with dates and grid references, and a **resident confirmation**
   by named members of a project committee elected in a public forum — a
   separate record from the contractor's statement and the inspector's. The hub
   exposes a payment-release signal to the municipality's own finance system
   (Trustwork never holds money). Olken's condition: the resident confirmation
   compares against a professional measurement (bill of quantities, inspector's
   act), so a confirmation is an attestation of an observed fact, not a vote of
   thanks.
5. **Reputation from evidence for contractors, institutions and officials'
   offices, never for residents.** A contractor's PB record (on time against the
   agreed baseline, defects, remediation, acceptance) feeds the existing
   reputation snapshot with method version and sample counts. An institution (an
   alcaldía, a county department) gets a public page of its public projects with
   the same labels. **No reputation of a citizen from civic acts, ever**; the
   ballot is unlinkable; participation is never a signal about a person.
6. **Ballot design as data, not code.** The module supports approval, knapsack
   and equal-shares counting, district envelopes, one vote per theme, and a
   need-weighted floor, configured per hub; it stores a verified-once
   pseudonymous ballot (identity proven to the hub or by the country pack's eID
   or bank identity, then unlinked) and publishes aggregate anomaly audits by
   hour, age band and channel. A rule lets the operator remove a project whose
   votes are shown to be coerced or bought. Paper and assisted votes are entered
   by staff under the same roll.
7. **Co-financing as a first-class, disclosed term.** Resident and business
   contributions are recorded as obligations with in-kind valuation at a
   published rate, capped as a share of winners, optional, and never a condition
   for the poorest areas. A business contribution is shown on the project page
   as what it is.
8. **The dropped project has a page.** A winner that cannot be built gets a
   stated reason, a date, the person responsible by role, and a substitute
   chosen by the same rule. Nothing disappears (supersession and reversal append
   history).
9. **Open data by default, OCDS-shaped.** Every public project's stages publish
   as a feed the city can put on its own portal, mapped to OCDS planning,
   tender, award, contract and implementation with the `ocid` as external
   identifier; Paris's stage dataset and Makueni's PMTS show the demand. Private
   deal data (a contractor's floor, a negotiation) stays private; only public
   projections federate.
10. **Education built in.** School and youth PB cycles run on the same module
    with small sums; the magazine publishes a sourced "what was promised, what
    was built" edition per cycle; courses for project committee members (how to
    read a bill of quantities, how to witness a hidden-works acceptance) carry
    certificates checkable by number. This is "education of society" with a
    budget attached.
11. **Country packs carry the law.** Poland's 0.5 per cent, Peru's Law 28056 and
    DS 142-2009-EF, DR's Law 170-07 committees, Mexico City's arts 117 and
    130–132, Kenya's PFM Act s. 125, Korea's Local Finance Act art 39,
    Uzbekistan's decrees and cost ceilings become sourced facts in packs, so the
    module's stages and committee roles are configured from law, not hard-coded.
    Uzbekistan already has a pack; Kenya, Peru, DR, Mexico and the Philippines
    are launch-research countries.
12. **Neutrality and non-surveillance tests.** Launch gates for a civic hub add:
    the ballot cannot be linked to a person by any role including staff; no
    resident score exists in the schema; the operator is a lawful municipality
    or civic body; no restricted place; and the operator has accepted that
    evidence is published whether it flatters or not.

## 9. Key findings, sourced

1. Participatory budgeting began in Porto Alegre in 1989; by 2019 more than
   11,000 processes were recorded worldwide; in 2017 Porto Alegre suspended it
   (WRI 2018; PB World Atlas 2019).
2. Where PB lasted more than four years, Brazilian municipalities spent more on
   health and sanitation and lost fewer infants: one to three per thousand live
   births (Gonçalves, _World Development_ 2014; Touchton and Wampler, _CPS_
   2014).
3. The idea travelled as a conversation and arrived without its teeth: the
   meetings spread, the binding link from meeting to budget usually did not
   (Baiocchi and Ganuza, _Politics & Society_).
4. Peru made PB law for every government in 2003; twenty years on it increased
   pro-poor spending and quality of life, and is still called a formality by the
   people who studied it longest (Abbott, McKiernan and McNulty 2024; McNulty
   2019).
5. Poland obliges its 66 largest cities to decide at least 0.5 per cent of
   spending by civic budget (Act of 11 January 2018, art. 5a). Scotland's
   councils put 1.3 per cent of their budgets under participatory budgeting in
   2022/23 (COSLA via Scottish Government FOI 2025).
6. In spring 2023 Uzbekistan's Initiative Budget drew 16.13 million votes for
   1,666 winning mahalla projects (Gazeta.uz 2023; the press called it about a
   third of the adult population, a share that does not match the arithmetic
   and is not repeated here). It also drew vote buying at a dollar a vote, SIM farms and
   teachers sent door to door; in 2025 the ministry disqualified coerced
   projects and called compelled voting forced labour (Kun.uz 2023, 2025).
7. A civic monitor found a third of 135 Uzbek Initiative Budget projects
   finished with defects, unfinished or never contracted (Yuksalish via
   Anhor.uz, 2023). The Philippines completed 27.8 per cent of 59,684 Bottom-up
   Budgeting projects in three years and dropped 7,928 (DILG and PIDS via
   BusinessMirror 2016).
8. In New York, a project residents voted for can take four to five years to be
   built; a council member wrote that PB is not to blame, the city's capital
   process is (Norwood News; Citizens Budget Commission).
9. Madrid cut its EUR 100 million participatory budget in half in 2020,
   discarded 182 approved projects and moved to two-year cycles so the
   administration could evaluate and build what was chosen (Consul Democracy
   2026; Democracy Technologies). Barcelona cut 75 million to 30 so that the
   projects could be finished within the term (betevé 2020).
10. In Makueni County, Kenya, a contractor is paid only after a committee of
    residents elected in a public forum has approved the work (World Bank,
    _Making Devolution Work for Service Delivery in Kenya_).
11. In Indonesia, raising the chance of a professional audit from 4 to 100 per
    cent cut missing money on village roads by eight points; community
    monitoring alone did little except where elites could not capture it (Olken,
    _Journal of Political Economy_ 2007). Citizen confirmation needs a
    measurement to check against and a consequence attached.
12. The Method of Equal Shares, first used in Wieliczka and Aarau in 2023, chose
    17 projects in Aarau where the usual count chose 7, so that more
    neighbourhoods got something they had voted for (Harvard EconCS; University
    of Warsaw).
13. The leading participation platforms are free software under AGPL-3.0,
    governed by associations and foundations and listed as Digital Public Goods:
    Decidim (2023), Consul Democracy (2024), Polis (verified 2026). None of them
    follows a project past a status label.
14. Co-financing by residents and business, the mark of "initiative budgeting",
    raises ownership and can exclude the poor; in Kyrgyz villages the
    contribution requirement kept the poorest out of both the decision and the
    choice (_Development in Practice_ 2011).
15. Every programme that died — Porto Alegre 2017, Brazil's 80 per cent fall
    after 2016, Bottom-up Budgeting 2016, Lisbon 2021 — died when a government
    changed or a capital budget shrank; what survived was written into law,
    measured in public, and left a record.
