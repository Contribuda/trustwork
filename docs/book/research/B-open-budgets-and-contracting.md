# B. Following public money into concrete: open budgets, open contracting, infrastructure transparency, citizen monitoring, and the reputation of contractors and institutions

Research note for the Trustwork Book, first edition, November 2026. Working evidence, not the book's position.

Feeds chapters 13 and 20. Date of access for every source: 2026-11-04 unless
stated. Evidence levels: **primary/official**, _(secondary)_, _(vendor/self
claim)_. Nothing here is legal advice; every legal point is a question for
counsel. Naming an organisation records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

## Summary

1. The latest Open Budget Survey is **OBS 2025** (10th edition, 82 countries,
   documents through 31 December 2024, released in regional launches from spring
   2026; the global report was still "coming soon" on 2026-11-04). The global
   average transparency score stays at **45/100**; public participation rose
   only from 15 to **17/100**. The 2023 round covered 125 countries; six of our
   fifteen countries (United States, Colombia, Mexico, Georgia, Azerbaijan,
   Uzbekistan) are not in the 2025 round and keep their 2023 scores.
2. Participation is the weakest link everywhere: the best of our countries
   (Dominican Republic 50, Kenya 44, Ukraine 41, Peru 39, Philippines 37) still
   fall far short of what IBP calls adequate; Nigeria scores 7, Azerbaijan 11.
3. Open contracting has a standard (OCDS 1.1.5), an infrastructure extension
   (OC4IDS 0.9.5, Apache-2.0, built by OCP and CoST), a registry of **136
   datasets**, and a credible but mostly advocacy-sourced evidence base (more
   bidders, fewer amendments, lower prices). Eleven of our fifteen countries
   publish OCDS; the Philippines, Armenia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan do not.
4. The Philippine flood-control scandal of 2025 is the clearest recent proof of
   the book's thesis: 9,855 projects worth PHP 545.64 billion, **421 "ghost"
   projects among about 8,000 inspected**, a contractor family holding nine
   licensed companies, and a licensing register (PCAB) that renewed those
   licences until the Senate hearings. Paper registers did not protect anyone;
   field evidence did.
5. The widely quoted "10–30 percent of public construction investment is lost to
   mismanagement and corruption" is a **CoST estimate from 2012**, not a
   measurement; the IMF's 2015 figure of about 30 percent lost to
   public-investment _inefficiency_ is the better-sourced number. The book
   should quote both with their status.
6. CoST (founded 2012) has about 19 active members, a 40-item Infrastructure
   Data Standard, an independent "assurance" review and an Infrastructure
   Transparency Index (Uganda 20.8 → 32.26 between 2021 and 2024; Ghana 35.76 in
   2025); only nine members ran a structured scrutiny process in 2021–2025.
7. Citizen monitoring works when it is institutionalised, funded and followed up
   (Andhra Pradesh's independent social audit society, Ukraine's DoZorro feeding
   state auditors, Makueni's elected project committees) and fails when it is a
   reporting channel without consequences (LAPOR! complaints about corruption,
   NOCOPO's eight compliant ministries in 2018, recovery of 15 percent of
   flagged MGNREGA money).
8. Geo-tagged, dated photographs of public works are already state practice at
   scale (about 30 million MGNREGA assets on India's Bhuvan portal; Kaduna's
   2-km "Eyes and Ears" app; Peru's INFOBRAS, which counted 2,428 stalled works
   worth S/44.3 billion in mid-2025).
9. Monitoring is dangerous: CHRI counts 82 right-to-information users killed and
   166 assaulted in India since 2005; the Philippines still has no
   whistle-blower law. Protection of monitors is a design requirement, not a
   footnote.
10. The EU AI Act bans social scoring of people by AI (Article 5(1)(c),
    applicable since 2 February 2025); the EU anti-SLAPP Directive protects
    public participation from abusive lawsuits. Trustwork's "no public score,
    evidence only, explained and appealable" rule is already on the right side
    of this line and must never move.

## 1. Open budgets

### The Open Budget Survey: what the latest round says

The International Budget Partnership's Open Budget Survey is the only
independent, comparative, fact-based measure of budget transparency,
participation and oversight. Each country page of the **2025 round** states:
10th edition, 82 countries, 218 questions, documents and events through 31
December 2024, global average transparency **45** and global average public
participation **17** (primary/official: e.g.
https://internationalbudget.org/open-budget-survey/country-results/2025/peru and
.../2025/philippines, IBP, 2026). IBP's survey landing page on 2026-11-04 said
results were released in five regional launches "to give more space for regional
context", with the global report "coming soon" (primary:
https://internationalbudget.org/open-budget-survey). Press coverage dates the
first release to 15 April 2026 _(secondary: Manila Bulletin 2026-07-01,
https://mb.com.ph/2026/07/01/2025-open-budget-survey-going-beyond-the-numbers;
Arab News Pakistan)_. Regional averages given on the 2025 pages: Latin America
and the Caribbean transparency 57 / participation 26; Sub-Saharan Africa 38 /
16; Asia-Pacific 49 / 18.

The **2023 round** (9th edition, 125 countries, published 29 May 2024) had the
same transparency average, 45, and a participation average of 15; secondary
summaries give legislative oversight 45 and supreme-audit oversight 62
_(secondary: https://fiscaltransparency.net/obs-2023/; Freedom Forum Nepal,
https://freedomforum.org.np/obs23-nepal)_. IBP treats 61 as the threshold for
"sufficient" transparency, so the world average has been "insufficient" for a
decade.

The shrink from 125 to 82 countries is a fact the book should state plainly;
IBP's own explanation (regional launches) does not say why fewer countries were
covered, and the reason could not be verified.

**What participation scores look like.** A participation score measures whether
the executive, legislature and audit office give the public formal, inclusive,
recorded ways to take part during formulation, approval, execution and audit
(pre-budget consultations, participatory budgeting, public hearings, citizen
input to audits). Scores above 40 are rare; in our set only the Dominican
Republic (50), Kenya (44), Georgia (44 in 2023), Ukraine (41), Peru (39) and the
Philippines (37) exceed it. Kenya's 44 reflects the constitutional duty of
public participation and county forums; the Dominican Republic's 50 is the
highest of the fifteen. The pattern in numbers:
governments publish more than they listen.

### GIFT principles

The Global Initiative for Fiscal Transparency published ten **High-Level
Principles on Fiscal Transparency, Participation and Accountability** in 2012;
the UN General Assembly recognised them in resolution 67/218 (December 2012),
encouraging member states to intensify efforts on transparency, participation
and accountability in fiscal policy. Principle 10 asserts the public's right to
direct participation in fiscal policy. GIFT added the **Principles of Public
Participation in Fiscal Policy** in 2016 after public consultation, and an
expanded version of the High-Level Principles in 2018 (primary: GIFT PDF
https://fiscaltransparency.net/documents/GIFT-EHLP-9Feb18.pdf; GIFT pages
https://fiscaltransparency.net/detailed-explanation-of-the-high-level-principles/
returned a server error on 2026-11-04 and were read through search excerpts;
_(secondary)_ for the principle-by-principle text). The participation principles
(accessibility, openness, inclusiveness, respect for self-expression,
timeliness, depth, proportionality, sustainability, complementarity,
reciprocity) are a usable checklist for how a hub runs a public consultation on
a built project.

### Citizens' budgets and budget portals

A citizens' budget is a plain-language version of the budget; IBP counts it as
one of eight key documents. Beyond documents, the practice that matters for
Trustwork is the **execution** portal: Brazil's Portal da Transparência (CGU,
launched November 2004; record traffic in 2024; about 15,480 datasets)
_(secondary: JML/CGU repost,
https://jmlgrupo.com.br/conteudo/cgu-portal-da-transparencia-registra-recorde-de-acessos-em-2024/;
CGU knowledge base)_; Ukraine's E-data platform (spending.gov.ua,
openbudget.gov.ua), launched 15 September 2015, which the government says
produced 76 million documents for 39,880 budget administrators in seven years
(primary/official: Ministry of Finance
https://mof.gov.ua/en/news/ministerstvo-finansiv-zapustilo-oficijnij-vebportal-vikoristannja-derzhavnih-koshtiv;
Cabinet of Ministers
https://www.kmu.gov.ua/news/minfin-za-7-rokiv-roboty-platformy-ie-data-bulo-stvoreno-76-mln-dokumentiv);
Uzbekistan's openbudget.uz, which runs the national "Initiative Budget" vote
(see §4).

### Open Government Partnership

OGP (founded 2011) is the forum in which most open-budget and open-contracting
commitments are made. Its 2025–26 annual report (April 2025–March 2026) says 15
national and 48 subnational members submitted new commitments in the period, 36
subnational members for the first time; 86 percent of OGP countries implemented
commitments; Thailand joined in January 2026; 175 Open Gov Challenge commitments
from 42 national and local members had been accepted by April 2026
(primary/official PDF
https://www.opengovpartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/OGP-2025-26-Annual-Report.pdf,
figures read through search excerpts; the PDF could not be parsed directly). The
total membership count could not be verified (the members page could not be
reached); older OGP pages say 78 countries. OCP states that "over 50 governments"
have an open contracting programme, mostly through OGP commitments _(vendor/self
claim: https://www.open-contracting.org/impact/evidence/)_. Of our fifteen, all
except Uzbekistan, Azerbaijan (withdrawn/inactive) and Rwanda (not a member, to
general knowledge; not verified) have been OGP members at some point; the
United States' status after 2025 should be checked before the book states it.

## 2. Open contracting

### The standards

The **Open Contracting Data Standard** (OCDS, current version 1.1.5) is "a
common data model for publishing contracting information" at every stage
(planning, tender, award, contract, implementation) with documents attached
(primary: https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/). **OC4IDS** (Open
Contracting for Infrastructure Data Standards, version 0.9.5, Apache-2.0) links
project-level disclosure based on the CoST Infrastructure Data Standard with
contract-level OCDS data, across identification, preparation, implementation and
delivery; it was developed by the Open Contracting Partnership and CoST
(primary: https://standard.open-contracting.org/infrastructure/latest/en/).
OC4IDS is the natural schema for a Trustwork "public-works record": one project,
many contracts, one timeline.

### The evidence on impact

OCP's evidence page is the best single collection, but most entries are OCP's
own stories or partner reports; independent academic work is thinner
_(vendor/self claim unless noted:
https://www.open-contracting.org/impact/evidence/)_:

- **Ukraine (Prozorro).** OCP: 97.7 percent of bidding businesses are SMEs (2021
  data); perceived corruption halved and unique suppliers up 45 percent in early
  years. Official 2024 figures from the Ministry of Economy: competitive
  procurement rose from UAH 616.8 billion (2023) to **UAH 841 billion (2024)**,
  contracts signed UAH 789.5 billion, and average bids per tender rose from 2.03
  to **2.38**; defence-sector competitive procurement grew from UAH 60 to 168.4
  billion over 2023–2024 (primary/official:
  https://www.kmu.gov.ua/news/popry-povnomasshtabnu-viinu-sfera-publichnykh-zakupivel-iak-nikoly-dynamichno-rozvyvaietsia-nadiia-bihun;
  Ministry of Economy news pages). Cumulative "savings" of about USD 9.8 billion
  since launch are a USAID/press figure _(secondary)_; annual savings totals for
  2024–2025 could not be verified. Prozorro's technical lesson is that **all
  data is open by default and civil society gets the same API as the state**.
- **Colombia (SECOP II).** Among entities using SECOP II, direct contracts fell
  6 percent and average bidders rose 17 percent (21 percent for open tenders),
  2019–2020 _(self claim, OCP 2020)_. SECOP II publishes daily OCDS through a
  JSON API and CSV on datos.gov.co (primary:
  https://operaciones.colombiacompra.gov.co/transparencia/estandar-ocds).
- **Paraguay (DNCP).** Contract amendments fell from 19 percent to 3 percent;
  school-funding transparency raised the share of neediest schools reached from
  under 20 to over 80 percent _(self claim, OCP/Digiwhist)_. A registered
  randomised trial (210 of 317 projects) tests MapaInversiones' effect on
  project delivery; results were not found (primary registration:
  https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/9787).
- **Moldova (MTender).** Real-time OCDS; 14.5 percent overall medical
  procurement savings and 19 percent on HIV medicines; USD 40 million monitored
  in real time _(self claim, OCP 2021)_.
- **Nigeria.** Budeshi (PPDC) linked 10,000+ contracts worth NGN 226 billion
  since 2014 _(self claim)_. The federal NOCOPO portal was promised at the 2016
  London Anti-Corruption Summit; in July 2018 **only eight ministries complied**
  with the circular to upload records; a redesign on OCDS is scheduled for
  January 2026 to December 2029 (primary/official: OGP commitments
  https://www.opengovpartnership.org/members/nigeria/commitments/NG0033/ and
  NG0016; _(secondary)_ OECD OPSI).
- **Kenya.** PPRA publishes daily OCDS from the Public Procurement Information
  Portal; Makueni County was an early subnational publisher (registry). No
  independent impact study found.
- **Philippines (PhilGEPS).** Not an OCDS publisher. The New Government
  Procurement Act (RA 12009) took effect in August 2024 and its implementing
  rules in February 2025; the modernised mPhilGEPS is committed to publish OCDS
  from the annual procurement plan to award (primary/official: OGP commitment
  PH0066
  https://www.opengovpartnership.org/members/philippines/commitments/ph0066/;
  GPPB Resolution 09-2025). A 2026 KPMG note says RA 12009 removed the criminal
  penal clauses that RA 9184 carried _(secondary:
  https://kpmg.com/ph/en/insights/2026/07/accountability-by-design-how-digital-tools-can-close-the-procurement-gap.html)_;
  counsel should check.
- **Mexico.** CompraNet (since 1996) was replaced in 2025 by the Digital Public
  Procurement Platform (ComprasMX); the 2025 public-works reform cut the
  direct-award ceiling from 30 to 20 percent of budget _(secondary: Garrigues,
  Mondaq; official:
  https://www.proyectosmexico.gob.mx/en/compranet-has-been-transformed-into-compras-mx/)_.
  IMCO found direct awards and restricted invitations reached 43.3 percent of
  spending in 2020, the first year they exceeded tendered contracts _(secondary:
  IMCO)_. Nuevo León reports competition up 25 percent since 2017 _(self claim
  via OCP)_. Mexico's distinctive strength is the two dozen state transparency
  institutes publishing OCDS.
- **Peru.** OSCE became **OECE** (Organismo Especializado para las
  Contrataciones Públicas Eficientes) when Law 32069 took effect on 22 April
  2025; it publishes SEACE data in OCDS daily (primary:
  https://data.open-contracting.org/en/publication/135; _(secondary)_ Andina,
  Gestión). An IGC study cited by OCP found districts with anti-corruption
  monitoring spent 51.39 percent less on public-works execution than comparable
  districts _(secondary)_.
- **Dominican Republic.** DGCP began OCDS publication in April 2020; contracts
  to smaller women-led businesses rose from 10 percent (2012) to 26 percent
  (2019); a 2025 law reserves a share of MSME purchases for women-led firms
  (sources disagree on 5 or 10 percent) _(self claim, OCP; secondary press)_.
- **Rwanda.** Umucyo e-procurement launched 2016 (mandatory from July 2017); an
  RPPA snapshot reported 19,850 tenders published and 16,976 contracts awarded
  through the portal; RPPA publishes daily OCDS; a parliamentary hearing found
  13 tenders worth RWF 31 billion processed with a closed bidding window
  _(secondary: The New Times; primary registry
  https://data.open-contracting.org/en/publication/145)_.
- **Georgia.** Ge-GP (2010) made every tender electronic; bidder identity is
  hidden until bid opening to limit collusion; the World Bank called it a
  success story; TI Georgia in 2013 called it "a leading, illustrative example";
  the State Audit Office audited its shortcomings in 2016 _(secondary: World
  Bank blog
  https://blogs.worldbank.org/governance/story-behind-georgias-e-procurement-success;
  TI Georgia 2012 report)_.
- **Elsewhere.** Slovakia: almost 8 percent of the public checked at least one
  contract online in a year, implementation under USD 1 million _(secondary: TI
  Slovakia 2015)_. EU-wide: each additional item of tender information lowers
  single-bid risk; single-bid contracts cost over 7 percent more _(secondary:
  Digiwhist)_. Costa Rica: MapaInversiones projects showed +18 percent financial
  and +8 points physical progress after three months _(secondary: IDB)_.
  Colombia's MapaRegalías: +7.996 percentage points execution efficiency
  _(secondary: IDB)_. The IDB made MapaInversiones **open source in May 2025**,
  now in 14 countries (primary/official:
  https://www.iadb.org/en/blog/modernization-state/public-administration/investmentmap-now-open-source).

**Honest reading.** Open contracting reliably increases _visibility_ and often
_competition_; its effect on _corruption_ and _quality of the built asset_
depends on who uses the data and whether anyone can act on it. Ukraine's own
history shows this: in DoZorro's first year more than 3,000 violations were
reported but only five fines followed _(secondary: Mind.ua 2017)_.

## 3. Infrastructure transparency

### CoST, the Infrastructure Transparency Initiative

CoST was launched globally in 2012 with World Bank support after a three-year
pilot in eight countries (primary:
https://infrastructuretransparency.org/about-us/). Its approach has four
features: **multi-stakeholder working** (government, industry, civil society in
one group), **disclosure** (purpose, scope, cost, implementation data),
**assurance** (an independent review team checks the disclosed data, finds gaps
and good practice and translates it into plain language) and **social
accountability** (media, civil society and academia use the findings) (primary:
https://infrastructuretransparency.org/our-approach/). The **CoST Infrastructure
Data Standard** requires 40 proactively published data points across the project
cycle; OC4IDS adds the contract layer. CoST reports **100,244 projects
published** using IDS or OC4IDS, and since 2020: 15,260 people trained, 112
government actions, and USD 76.78 billion of public infrastructure investment
"made more transparent" _(self claim, homepage)_.

**Members.** CoST's news categories list Afghanistan, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El
Salvador, Ethiopia, Guatemala, Honduras, Malawi, Mozambique, Panama, Tanzania,
Thailand, Timor-Leste, Uganda and Ukraine at national level, and Bogotá, Buenos
Aires, Jalisco, Puerto Rico, Sekondi-Takoradi (Ghana) and West Lombok
(Indonesia) at sub-national level; the Dominican Republic joined as an
affiliate member in 2021, the first Caribbean member, and applied for full
membership in October 2024 with the procurement directorate (DGCP) leading
(primary: https://infrastructuretransparency.org/members/, status labels are
not given on the page;
https://infrastructuretransparency.org/news/dominican-republic-joins-cost-as-first-caribbean-member/);
the 2025 plans also mention Nigeria and Kaduna State, Ghana and Indonesia. An
FCDO-commissioned evaluation (April 2026) says **nine of 19 active members**
undertook a structured scrutiny process in 2021–2025 _(secondary: FCDO IATI
document S30056657)_. Of our fifteen countries Ukraine and the Dominican
Republic are national members; Ghana (Sekondi-Takoradi), Nigeria (Kaduna),
Colombia (Bogotá) and Mexico (Jalisco) appear sub-nationally or in plans.
Kenya, Peru, the Philippines, Rwanda and the Caucasus countries are not
members.

**Infrastructure Transparency Index (ITI).** A country score built from
disclosure, assurance and institutional practice across sampled procuring
entities. Known results: Uganda 20.8 percent (2021) → **32.26 percent (2024)**
(primary/official: Uganda Ministry of Finance knowledge centre,
https://development.finance.go.ug/knowledge-centre-reports/uganda's-infrastructure-transparency-index-2025);
Honduras 47.51 (2021, beta deck); Costa Rica's third ITI (2025) covered 30
institutions and 87 percent of the public-works budget _(secondary: Delfino, El
Mundo CR)_; Ghana **35.76 percent** (December 2025), called "not encouraging" by
its own programme _(secondary: B&FT)_. The ITI is the closest existing thing to
an "institution delivery record" for public works, and it is still in beta in
most places.

### How much is lost, and how solid the number is

- **"10–30 percent of investment in publicly funded construction is lost through
  mismanagement and corruption"** is attributed to **CoST, 2012** by an OECD
  presentation (2019) and repeated by OGP ("could reach USD 5 trillion a year by
  2030") _(secondary: OECD slides via World Bank PPP site; OGP story
  https://www.opengovpartnership.org/stories/three-simple-steps-to-deliver-better-value-infrastructure-through-your-ogp-national-action-plan/)_.
  CoST's former chair Chrik Poortman describes it as a business-as-usual
  scenario estimate, not a measurement _(secondary: IHRB interview)_. CoST's own
  site today cites instead the **IMF (2015)**: about 30 percent of the potential
  value of public investment is lost through _inefficiency_ (primary/official:
  IMF, "Making Public Investment More Efficient",
  https://www.imf.org/external/np/pp/eng/2015/061115.pdf, via CoST about page).
  Charles Kenny's World Bank working paper 4271 (2007), the usual academic
  anchor, gives **no monetary loss figure**; its argument is that the largest
  cost of construction corruption is poor-quality infrastructure with weak
  returns and unfunded maintenance (primary:
  https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/bec8ccfc-9da0-507b-a5e1-770228deafae).
  OECD's statement that 57 percent of foreign-bribery cases involved public
  contracts is primary/official (OECD Foreign Bribery Report 2014, via OCP).
- **Verdict for the book:** quote "10–30 percent" only as "CoST's 2012
  estimate", quote the IMF's "about 30 percent lost to inefficiency" as the
  measured-model figure, and say that the Philippines' 2025 audit (421
  non-existent projects among about 8,000 inspected, roughly 5 percent by count,
  and PHP 100–118.5 billion of losses on about PHP 545 billion of projects,
  roughly 20 percent by value) lands inside that range (see §4).

### Debarment and cross-debarment

The World Bank's Listing of Ineligible Firms and Individuals is public, updated
every three hours, and distinguishes debarred from cross-debarred entities and
"other sanctions" (reprimands, conditional non-debarment). The **Agreement for
Mutual Enforcement of Debarment Decisions** (9 April 2010, effective 1
July 2011) binds the World Bank, ADB, EBRD, IDB and AfDB: a debarment longer
than one year by one bank is enforced by the others (primary:
https://www.worldbank.org/en/projects-operations/procurement/debarred-firms; the
one-year threshold is from the agreement text, _(secondary)_ here). ADB
cross-debarred 87 firms and 9 individuals in 2024 _(secondary: Mayer Brown,
Nov 2025)_. National registers also exist (US SAM exclusions; Philippine GPPB
blacklisting; Kenya PPRA debarment; Ukraine's register of unreliable suppliers).
For Trustwork these are **sourced facts about a company**, never a Trustwork
judgement: "debarred by X from date to date, grounds Y, source Z".

### Contractor performance registers

- **United States, CPARS.** Every federal contract above the threshold gets a
  past-performance assessment; contractors can comment and request review.
  Problems documented by inspectors: a DoD IG review found **84 percent of
  assessments** had ratings, narratives or descriptions short of requirements;
  GAO sustained protests where agencies relied only on CPARS summary charts and
  will not hear direct challenges to a rating _(secondary: POGO, SmallGovCon
  summaries of DoD IG and GAO decisions)_. Lesson: a performance record needs
  narrative evidence and an appeal route, or it degrades into unused
  percentages.
- **Kenya, NCA.** Contractors are registered in categories NCA1 (unlimited) to
  NCA8 (small works), under the National Construction Authority Act 2011;
  registration fees from KES 100,000 down to KES 5,000; about 18,000 contractors
  registered at one count _(primary: https://www.nca.go.ke/local-contractors;
  secondary press)_. It is a _capacity_ classification, not a performance
  record; no public performance evaluation was found.
- **Philippines, PCAB.** Licences by category (AAAA to E). PCAB revoked the
  licences of **nine related companies** on 1 September 2025 (Board Resolution
  075) after Senate hearings established that they had bid against each other
  for flood-control contracts; senators asked why PCAB had renewed the licences
  _(primary: PCAB Board Resolution 075; Senate hearing record; press reports
  secondary)_. A classification register that cannot see that nine
  "competitors" share one owner is a captured register.
- **Peru, RNP and INFOBRAS.** The supplier register (RNP) is capacity-based.
  INFOBRAS, run by the Contraloría, records every public work with progress and
  photographs; on 30 June 2025 it counted **2,428 stalled works worth S/44,297
  million**, 71.8 percent of them local-government works; officials who fail to
  update it face sanctions (primary/official: Contraloría via gob.pe;
  _(secondary)_ Infomercado, Caretas). A year-end 2025 press figure of S/67,139
  million could not be verified against the Contraloría.

## 4. Citizen monitoring and social audits

### India: MGNREGA social audits and the Andhra Pradesh model

Under the Audit of Scheme Rules 2011 every state must have a **Social Audit Unit
independent of the implementing agency**, funded with 0.5 percent of the
previous year's MGNREGA spend _(secondary: Drishti summaries of MoRD rules)_.
Andhra Pradesh created the **Society for Social Audit, Accountability and
Transparency (SSAAT)** in May 2009 (primary: CAG performance audit 2013,
https://cag.gov.in/uploads/download_audit_report/2013/Andhra_Pradesh_MGNREGA_report_5_2013_chapter_5.pdf).
The AP method: trained village social auditors verify muster rolls and works
door to door, then a **mandal-level public hearing** with mandatory attendance
of implementing officials. Findings: Afridi and Iversen (2014) found audits
detected more irregularities over successive rounds but had little effect on
reducing easy-to-detect malpractice, because follow-up and punishment were weak;
they recommend time-bound, enforced consequences _(secondary: NCAER India Policy
Forum 2014,
https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nca:ncaerj:v:10:y:2014:i:2014-1:p:297-341)_.
Nationally, only three states have audited 40 percent or more of villages (AP
49.7, HP 45.32, Telangana 40.5) and in 2021–22 **INR 171 crore was flagged, INR
26 crore (15 percent) recovered** _(secondary: Drishti/Hindu)_. The OGP's
annotated bibliography concludes that _relatively independent_ social audit
units are more participatory and better at follow-up (primary/official: OGP
2022,
https://www.opengovpartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/Social-Audits-in-Service-Delivery-An-Annotated-Bibliography.pdf).

**GeoMGNREGA.** Since an MoU of 24 June 2016 between the Rural Development
Ministry, NRSC (ISRO) and NIC, every completed asset is geo-tagged with GPS and
**two photographs** by enumerators, moderated by a state nodal officer and shown
publicly on the Bhuvan portal; about 30 million assets were tagged by NRSC's
undated count (primary: https://www.nrsc.gov.in/readmore_geomnrega;
_(secondary)_ Swarajya 2018 count 29.1 million). This is the largest existing
field-evidence register of public works and the direct precedent for a Trustwork
public-works record.

### Philippines: Check My School and Sumbong sa Pangulo

**Check My School** (ANSA-EAP with the Department of Education, from 2011)
combined an online school-data portal with offline volunteer validation; the
World Bank case study found the offline strategy "largely effective" and the
online strategy "more challenging" (primary/official: World Bank 2012,
https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/23031). **Sumbong sa Pangulo**
(August 2025) published the list of flood-control projects and took citizen
reports: 1,148 reports and 823 feedback submissions in its first three days
_(secondary: Philstar)_. Context: 9,855 flood-control projects worth PHP 545.64
billion, July 2022–May 2025 _(secondary: SunStar)_; by 9 October 2025 the
public-works secretary reported **421 ghost projects among about 8,000
inspected** _(secondary: Gulf News)_; the Department of Finance put losses at up
to PHP 118.5 billion for 2023–2025 _(secondary: The Star)_. The **Independent
Commission for Infrastructure** (Executive Order 94, 11 September 2025, chaired
by a retired Supreme Court justice) filed a 125-day report on 6 February 2026,
handed findings to the Ombudsman and DOJ and closed on 31 March 2026;
publication of its final report was left to those bodies _(secondary: Manila
Bulletin, Inquirer, PTV)_. Tens of thousands marched on 30 November 2025
_(secondary: AFP via EWN)_. The scandal is the book's best cautionary scene: the
data existed (PhilGEPS notices, DPWH lists, PCAB licences, COA audits) but
**nobody had tied the money to the concrete with field evidence**.

### Indonesia: LAPOR!

SP4N-LAPOR! is the national complaint system (since 2012): 1,543,465 reports by
November 2019; 718 institutions connected by 2026 (primary/official: OGP
commitment ID0058; _(secondary)_ RRI 2026). A 2020 thesis found weak
institutional responses to corruption complaints make it ineffective against
abuse of authority _(secondary: Flinders University)_. A channel without an
accountable responder is a suggestion box.

### Brazil: Portal da Transparência and the Observatório Social

Brazil's federal portal (2004) is the reference for line-item spending
transparency. The **Observatório Social do Brasil** network, about 150 volunteer
observatories in 17 states (2020), monitors municipal tenders in person; it has
claimed about BRL 4 billion in savings over four years, but its own reports say
savings are rarely counted and indirect effects are hard to attribute _(self
claim and secondary: OSB reports, Gazeta do Povo)_. Value for Trustwork: the
model of **local, standing, non-partisan volunteer bodies** attached to a city.

### Ukraine: DoZorro

DoZorro (Transparency International Ukraine, 2016) is the citizen layer over
Prozorro: a portal, 19+ regional NGOs, risk indicators and later a
machine-learning model that flags suspicious tenders. Figures: 30,000+ tenders
flagged worth about USD 4 billion in three years, violations fixed in 14
percent, 100,000 monthly users _(secondary: OCP 2020)_; earlier counts of 21,000
flagged, 30 percent resolved, 59 criminal charges and 198 sanctions _(secondary:
OCP evidence page)_; the state anti-corruption agency's 2025 digests credit
DoZorro monitoring with UAH 87 million saved in September and UAH 133 million in
July 2025 (primary/official: NACP digests https://nazk.gov.ua/en/october-2025/,
.../august-2025/). The design lesson is the **feedback loop to an authority with
power** (the State Audit Service, the Antimonopoly Committee, procurers
themselves), and the publishing of outcomes per complaint.

### Kenya: Makueni

Makueni County reserves at least 25 percent of its development budget for
participatory budgeting, runs a tiered process (one account: 3,867 meetings,
over 350,000 participants, a 1,000-person county forum) and lets citizens
oversee works through **elected Project Management Committees**; the World Bank
credits this with better accountability but notes committees focus on single
projects and that many felt processes were "open to abuse" (primary/official:
World Bank "Making Devolution Work for Service Delivery in Kenya" and PB pilot
note
https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/471371592554290653/pdf/Participatory-Budgeting-in-Kenya-Piloting-New-Techniques-for-Project-Monitoring.pdf).
The Controller of Budget's FY 2024/25 review shows the institutional
counterpart: counties spent KES 123.76 billion of a KES 218.99 billion
development budget, **57 percent absorption**, against 78 percent overall; only
twelve of 47 counties exceeded 70 percent; West Pokot reported zero development
spending for the year _(secondary: Nation, The Star, AllAfrica summaries of the
CoB report)_.

### Nigeria: Kaduna "Eyes and Ears"

Kaduna State's 2015 app lets citizens locate public projects within 2 km and
report on them; Ondo, Adamawa, Plateau and Enugu have looked at the model
(primary/official: OGP story
https://www.opengovpartnership.org/stories/citizen-eyes-and-ears-on-the-government-kaduna-nigeria/).

### Uzbekistan: the Initiative Budget

Since 2019 Uzbekistan runs a national participatory-budget vote on
openbudget.uz: in the first 2023 season, more than 16 million votes on 33,680
initiatives, 1,666 winners; in 2024, 1,772 winners funded with over UZS 2.1
trillion; a 2025 season received 22,623 proposals _(secondary: Gazeta.uz,
Kun.uz, Zamin.uz; UNDP)_. Critics describe vote mobilisation by local officials
and schools _(secondary: Global Voices 2023)_. Note A covers PB in
depth; for this note the point is that **Uzbekistan has the vote but no OBS
score and no OCDS**, so the budget line and the tender behind each winning
project are not open.

### Community scorecards: the evidence and the warning

Björkman and Svensson's randomised trial in 50 Ugandan communities (QJE 2009)
found community monitoring of clinics cut child mortality and raised
utilisation; a 3ie pure replication verified the results; the scaled-up
Posner–Raffler–Parkerson trial found weaker effects and **no evidence that
citizen monitoring was the channel** _(secondary: 3ie, CEGA, Journal of
Development Studies 2019)_. The honest conclusion: monitoring plus information
plus a provider who can be sanctioned works; monitoring alone is unproven.

### What makes citizen verification credible and safe

Across these cases the credible designs share: (1) an **independent, funded
body** that convenes the audit (SSAAT, CoST assurance teams); (2) **field
evidence with provenance** (GPS, timestamp, two photographs, who took them); (3)
a **public hearing or public outcome** per finding; (4) a **named authority with
a duty to respond** within a time limit; (5) **published follow-up** (resolved /
not resolved / why). The unsafe designs expose the monitor: CHRI's tracker
counts **82 RTI users killed and 166 assaulted** in India since 2005
_(secondary: Front Line Defenders citing CHRI; earlier CHRI count 74,
https://humanrightsinitiative.org/in-the-news/for-74-indian-activists-the-punishment-for-seeking-the-truth-has-been-death)_;
the Philippines has **no national whistle-blower protection law** and relies on
an ad-hoc witness programme; the Senate's May 2026 partial report recommends one
_(secondary: Gulf News, BusinessWorld)_. In the EU, Directive 2019/1937 protects
whistle-blowers and Directive 2024/1069 (in force 6 May 2024, transposition due
7 May 2026, infringement proceedings opened against 14 states on 15 July 2026)
protects public participation against abusive lawsuits (SLAPPs) _(secondary:
EAPIL, ANSA)_.

## 5. Reputation of contractors and institutions from evidence

### What exists

- **Capacity classification** (Kenya NCA1–8, Philippines PCAB AAAA–E, Peru RNP,
  South Africa CIDB grades): says what a firm _may_ bid for, not how it
  performed.
- **Performance assessment** (US CPARS; some national "contractor performance
  reports"): written by the client, often late, incomplete or inflated;
  appealable only indirectly.
- **Debarment and sanctions lists** (MDBs, SAM, national blacklists): binary,
  lagging, legally heavy, but reliable as _facts_.
- **Procurement analytics / red flags** (DoZorro, Budeshi, IMCO's
  corruption-risk index, Digiwhist): statistical indicators, useful for
  attention, not for judgement of a person.
- **Asset registers with evidence** (GeoMGNREGA, INFOBRAS, MapaInversiones): the
  only ones that look at the _concrete_.
- **Institutional indices** (OBS, CoST ITI, PEFA): they rate the _system_, not
  the official, and they are periodic.

### What fails

- **Paper ratings** without evidence or narrative (CPARS percentages).
- **Captured registers** that cannot see common ownership or that renew licences
  on paper (PCAB and the nine related firms).
- **Channels without consequences** (LAPOR! corruption complaints; NOCOPO's
  eight compliant ministries; MGNREGA's 15 percent recovery).
- **Scores that travel across contexts** (a traffic fine affecting a tender),
  which is exactly what the EU AI Act prohibits for AI systems: Article 5(1)(c)
  bans AI "evaluation or classification of natural persons or groups… based on
  their social behaviour or… personal characteristics" where the score leads to
  detrimental treatment in unrelated contexts or disproportionate to the
  behaviour; applicable since 2 February 2025 (primary:
  https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/).

### How a fair, explained, appealable record could work

Trustwork's reputation model already has the pieces: every claim passes
`claimed → evidence_attached → confirmed | disputed → superseded | reversed`;
confirmation is a recorded decision by an authorised actor with evidence;
importing a document is not confirmation; appeals are decided by a different
person; absence of evidence is "unknown, not failure"
(`docs/engine/execution-and-reputation.md`). Applied to public works:

- **Contractor record** = the set of public contracts (OCDS awards and
  contracts) linked to execution records (milestones, staged acceptance,
  defects, remediation), each with field evidence, the client's acceptance act,
  and — new — **citizen observations** that are themselves claims with evidence,
  confirmed or disputed by the inspector or the contractor. Public view shows
  facts and their status, never a composite score. Debarments appear as sourced
  facts with dates and grounds.
- **Official's record** is _not_ a Trustwork object. People in public roles
  appear only as **signatories of documents** (an award notice, an acceptance
  act) that are already public records in the source system. Trustwork never
  rates an official; it shows which document they signed, with the source.
- **Institution delivery record** = planned versus executed, promised versus
  delivered, built only from public, sourced data: budget lines (open budget
  portals), procurement (OCDS), project status (OC4IDS/INFOBRAS-type registers),
  audit findings (SAI reports), and the hub's own confirmed completions. Kenya's
  county absorption rates (57 percent development absorption in FY 2024/25),
  Peru's stalled-works count, Costa Rica's ITI by institution, and the
  Philippines' project lists are the kind of input. Each number carries its
  source and date; the page says what is missing.
- **Fairness mechanics**: right of reply on every page (a contractor or an
  institution can attach a response and evidence); a dispute freezes the public
  display of the disputed observation pending resolution; a different person
  decides the appeal; methods are versioned and published; nothing is bought.

## 6. Safeguards

1. **Personal data of officials and citizens.** OCDS itself carries personal
   data only in contact-person fields; OCP recommends a national-level privacy
   impact assessment rather than one per tender _(secondary: Portland PIA form;
   OCP 2017,
   https://www.open-contracting.org/2017/02/23/getting-personal-business-mind/)_.
   Trustwork hubs should ingest officials' names only as they appear in the
   public source, not enrich them, and never build profiles of officials;
   citizen monitors' identities are protected by default (pseudonymous
   submissions, identity known to the hub's trust reviewer only, no public
   attribution without consent).
2. **Defamation.** Publishing "the contractor failed" is an allegation;
   publishing "acceptance act of [date] records defects X; remediation not
   confirmed as of [date]; contractor's response: …" is a sourced fact with
   reply. The latter is the only form Trustwork publishes. Hubs operate under
   national law; the EU anti-SLAPP Directive and national equivalents are
   counsel questions per country pack.
3. **Political neutrality.** The same methods apply to every administration; the
   record is a time series that does not reset at elections; hubs do not publish
   during electoral silence periods where law requires; no endorsements, no
   rankings of politicians.
4. **Licences.** OCDS publications carry their own licences (often CC-BY or open
   government licences); OC4IDS is Apache-2.0; MapaInversiones is open source
   since May 2025; GeoMGNREGA imagery and INFOBRAS data have national terms.
   Every sourced page cites licence and date (existing rule in
   `discovery-and-outreach.md`).
5. **Retaliation.** Where there is no whistle-blower law (the Philippines today)
   a hub must assume that a named monitor can be hurt. Design: anonymity
   options, no geolocation of the _reporter_ (only of the asset), delayed
   publication windows, partnership with existing protection programmes, and the
   operator's duty to stop a campaign that endangers people.
6. **Why Trustwork must never become a state scoring tool.** A state that can
   read a citizen's observations, votes and complaints as a profile has a
   social-credit system. Trustwork's invariants already forbid it: no public
   score, private stays private (including from staff), reputation only from
   evidence tied to transactions and roles the person chose, one representative
   agent per person under revocable mandates, data stays on the hub's node. The
   civic layer adds one more: **citizens are never the object of a record; only
   works, contracts, budgets and the organisations and companies that deliver
   them are.** The EU AI Act's Article 5(1)(c) is the external benchmark.

## What it means for Trustwork

What exists in the engine's designs and is reused: PROC-001/PROC-002 (import of public
notices and awards) and PROC-003 (OCDS tender adapters for launch countries) in
the procurement design; sourced pages with field-level provenance and "created
from an open source" labelling (`discovery-and-outreach.md`); the execution and
evidence model with staged acceptance, photographs "with dates and grid
references", defects and remediation, counterparty confirmation, disputes with a
different decider (`execution-and-reputation.md`); REP-001 reputation toolkit
and passport; sealed-until-deadline RFQs (ADR 027) as the private-sector
analogue of sealed public bids; the tender desk for suppliers; country packs
with sourced legal facts; events, learning, the magazine; agents under mandates;
hubs and the mesh with public projections only.

1. **OCDS and OC4IDS ingestion as sourced pages (extends PROC-003).** Each OCDS
   release becomes a sourced _tender page_ and, on award, a sourced _contract
   page_ attached to the supplier's company page ("awarded by X on date, value,
   source, licence"). Each OC4IDS project becomes a sourced _public-works page_
   with its contracts beneath it. Pages stay labelled "from an open source";
   nothing on them is a Trustwork judgement. Launch-country adapters: SECOP II,
   OECE/PeruCompras, PPRA, DGCP, RPPA, BPP + states, INAI + states, PPA Ghana,
   Prozorro, SPA Georgia. Gaps to say plainly: Philippines (until mPhilGEPS
   OCDS), Armenia, Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan.
2. **A public-works record with field evidence and citizen confirmation (new
   entity, a direction of the book).** A public-works page gains an evidence timeline:
   milestones from the source system; the contractor's own execution records if
   the contractor is on the hub; **citizen observations** (geo-tagged,
   timestamped photographs of the asset, GeoMGNREGA-style, two photos minimum,
   with the reporter's identity held by the hub's trust reviewer and not
   published); inspector or client confirmation as the authorised decision;
   status per observation (received / acknowledged / confirmed / disputed /
   resolved) published, DoZorro-style. Observation guidelines come from CoST's
   assurance practice (what to look at: scope, cost, time, quality) and the
   hub's building-practice courses.
3. **Institution delivery records (new, a direction of the book; only from
   public data).** For
   a municipality, ministry or agency: budget planned versus executed by year
   (from the open-budget portal), projects promised versus completed versus
   stalled (from OC4IDS/INFOBRAS-type registers and hub-confirmed completions),
   audit findings (SAI reports), participation events held (public hearings, PB
   rounds). Every figure sourced and dated; missing data shown as missing; no
   composite score; right of reply for the institution; time series across
   administrations.
4. **No people are scored.** Officials appear only as signatories in public
   documents; citizens never appear as records; contractors are companies with
   sourced facts and confirmed evidence; the reputation passport remains the
   person's own toolkit. Add an invariant test: no public projection may contain
   a derived score for a natural person, and no observation may carry reporter
   geolocation.
5. **Protection of monitors (new policy facet).** A country-pack fact on
   whistle-blower and witness protection (law, body, source) and a hub setting
   that forces pseudonymous observations where protection is weak. Observations
   may be embargoed until the inspector responds. Hub operators have a
   documented duty to suspend a campaign that endangers people.
6. **Civic agents under mandates.** A resident's agent can watch a public-works
   page, prepare an observation from the resident's photographs, draft a
   question to the procuring entity under the resident's name, and remind about
   hearing dates; a contractor's agent can attach acceptance acts and responses;
   a municipality's standing representative (ADR 010 decision 9) can answer
   observations. All under the existing mandate and capability registry; nothing
   is sent without a person.
7. **Learning and events.** Courses for citizen monitors (how to read a bill of
   quantities, what a stalled work looks like, how to photograph for evidence)
   and for small contractors (how to bid on SECOP II / Prozorro / Umucyo; the
   tender desk already plans this); events that are public hearings and site
   visits; magazine pieces that follow one budget line into one building.
8. **Institutions whose published data a hub reads.** The instruments above
   are _data sources and methods_, not partnerships: the national procurement
   authority (OCDS publisher), the supreme audit institution, the CoST national
   programme where one exists, the IBP country researcher's organisation, local
   chapters of Transparency International, the ANSA/Check-My-School type
   networks, IDB's MapaInversiones team in Latin America, and the city's own
   transparency office. Naming an organisation records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.
