Following public money into concrete

The world has built standards for publishing budgets and contracts, programmes for disclosing infrastructure projects, and registers of millions of photographed public works. Each sees one stretch of the road. The loop closes only when a budget line, a contract, a photograph of the finished work and a confirmation by someone who can be held to it sit on one page, with every source named. This chapter says what exists, what it taught, and what Trustwork will do with it. The public-works record and the institution's delivery record are a direction of this book; the import of open procurement data is designed in the engine and not yet built.

Open budgets: publishing more than listening

The Open Budget Survey of the International Budget Partnership is the only independent, comparative measure of budget transparency, participation and oversight. Its 2025 round, the tenth, covered eighty-two countries and documents through the end of 2024; the global average for transparency was forty-five out of a hundred, where sixty-one counts as sufficient, and the average for public participation seventeen, up from fifteen in the 2023 round that covered 125 countries. Of the fourteen countries of this book that the survey covers, counting the 2023 round for those absent from 2025, the Dominican Republic listens best, with a participation score of fifty, and Nigeria least, with seven. Participation, in the survey's sense, means formal, inclusive, recorded ways for the public to take part in formulation, approval, execution and audit. Almost nowhere does it exist at the execution stage, which is the stage where a building is or is not built.

The principles that say it should exist are old: the Global Initiative for Fiscal Transparency's high-level principles, recognised by the United Nations General Assembly in December 2012, assert the public's right to direct participation in fiscal policy. The practice that followed is mostly documents and execution portals such as Brazil's, which has published spending since 2004, and Ukraine's, launched in 2015. A portal shows that money moved. It does not show what it became.

Open contracting: the standard that worked

The Open Contracting Data Standard is a common data model for publishing contracting information at every stage, planning, tender, award, contract and implementation, with documents attached and everything about one process linked by one identifier. Its infrastructure extension, built by the Open Contracting Partnership and CoST and released under an open licence, adds the project level: one project, many contracts, one timeline. The partnership's registry listed 136 datasets as of November 2026. Nine of the fourteen countries of this book publish in the standard nationally (Colombia, Peru, Kenya, the Dominican Republic, Rwanda, Nigeria, Mexico, Ghana and Georgia), as does Ukraine, and the United States through one city; the Philippines, Armenia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan do not yet, though the Philippines has committed to.

The evidence that openness changes behaviour is real, and most of it comes from the people who promote it. Ukraine's system is the best documented: competitive procurement grew from 616.8 billion hryvnia in 2023 to 841 billion in 2024, under full-scale war, and the average number of bids per tender rose from 2.03 to 2.38, by the economy ministry's own figures. Its technical lesson is that all data is open by default and civil society gets the same interface as the state. Colombia reports more bidders and fewer direct contracts where its second-generation system is used; Paraguay reports contract amendments falling from nineteen per cent to three. A fair reading is that open contracting reliably increases visibility and often competition; its effect on corruption and on the quality of the built asset depends on who uses the data and whether anyone can act on it. Ukraine shows that too: in the first year of its community monitoring layer more than three thousand violations were reported and five fines followed; years later, with the monitors feeding the state audit service and publishing the outcome of each complaint, the anti-corruption agency credits them with tens of millions of hryvnia saved in a single month. The difference was not the data. It was a named authority with a duty to respond and a published result per finding.

Infrastructure transparency: the forty data points

CoST, the Infrastructure Transparency Initiative, was launched in 2012 with World Bank support after a three-year pilot in eight countries. Its approach has four parts that Trustwork takes as a specification: a group of government, industry and civil society working together; disclosure of forty data points across a project's life; an independent assurance review that checks the disclosed data and translates its gaps into plain language; and social accountability, meaning that media, civil society and academia use the findings. Its Infrastructure Transparency Index scores a country's procuring entities on disclosure, assurance and practice: Uganda rose from 20.8 to 32.26 per cent between 2021 and 2024; Ghana scored 35.76 in December 2025, a result the country's business press headlined as "not encouraging". The index is the closest existing thing to a delivery record for public institutions, and it is in beta almost everywhere; an evaluation in 2026 found that nine of CoST's nineteen active members had run a structured scrutiny process in the previous five years.

Evidence of the concrete

Photographing public works with a date and a location is not a new idea. India has done it at a scale nothing else approaches: since an agreement of June 2016 between the rural development ministry, the space agency's remote sensing centre and the national informatics centre, every asset completed under the rural employment guarantee is geo-tagged with two photographs, moderated by a state officer and shown on a public map; the centre's count is about thirty million assets. Peru's audit office runs a register of every public work with progress and photographs; it is the register that counted the stalled works of chapter 11, and officials who fail to update it face sanction. The Inter-American Development Bank's investment map, which shows project progress to the public and whose pilots report faster physical and financial execution, was made open source in May 2025 and runs in fourteen countries.

The Philippine flood-control scandal (chapter 11) showed the difference. The registers that failed were registers of paper: a licence renewed, a notice posted. The records that worked were records of evidence: an inspector on site, a resident with a phone, an auditor with a map.

A portal shows that money moved. Evidence shows what it became.

Social audit: what makes resident verification credible

Resident verification has a long record and a clear verdict. In India every state must have a social audit unit independent of the agency that spends the money; Andhra Pradesh created the first in 2009, trained village auditors to verify muster rolls and works door to door, and held public hearings at which the implementing officials had to sit. The audits detected more each round. Their effect on behaviour was weak, because follow-up and punishment were weak: in 2021/22, of the money flagged nationally, fifteen per cent was recovered. Indonesia's complaint system has taken more than a million and a half reports and is judged ineffective against abuse of authority because institutions do not answer. Nigeria's open-contracting portal, promised in 2016, had eight compliant ministries in 2018. A channel without an accountable responder is a suggestion box.

Across the cases that worked, five things recur. An independent, funded body convenes the audit. Field evidence carries its provenance: location, timestamp, two photographs, who took them. Each finding gets a public hearing or a public outcome. A named authority has a duty to respond within a time limit. And the follow-up is published: resolved, not resolved, why. The Indonesian roads study of chapter 12 adds the sixth: a professional measurement to compare against. Scorecard trials in Ugandan clinics point the same way: monitoring alone is unproven; monitoring with a measurement and a consequence is not.

What Trustwork will do, said exactly

Three layers, with their status. The first exists in the engine's designs for the industry and is reused. The second and third are new to the civic engine and not yet designed in it. The first civic product of the San Diego hub is to be this evidence layer, a "works of the city" built from open permits and capital lines, and not a vote.

Sourced pages from open procurement data (designed, not built). The engine's discovery design already treats a procurement portal as a source, shows a public tender as a "public procurement notice" on the needs board and never as a member's need, and builds project pages only from public notices, permits and awards, linked to companies by their registration identifiers, never naming homeowners or private addresses. The procurement design names the Open Contracting Data Standard as its exchange format; adapters for the launch countries' portals are an open work package. Every such page is labelled "from an open source" with the portal, the notice, the licence and the date; nothing on it is a Trustwork judgement. The civic engine adds scope: each award becomes a sourced contract page beneath the supplier's company page, and each infrastructure project a sourced public-works page with its contracts beneath it.

A public-works record with field evidence and resident confirmation (not yet designed). A public-works page gains a timeline: milestones from the source system, the contractor's own execution records if the contractor is a participant, and the client's or inspector's confirmation as the authorised decision. Residents add observations: dated, geo-referenced photographs of the asset, two at minimum, in the manner of India's register, with the observer's identity held by the hub's trust reviewer and never published. Each observation passes through public states, received, acknowledged, confirmed or disputed, resolved, as Ukraine's monitors publish theirs, and what to look at comes from CoST's assurance practice. A confirmed observation that contradicts a reported milestone changes no score by itself; it opens a case for a person, and the case and its outcome are on the page.

An institution's delivery record (not yet designed). For a municipality, a ministry or an agency, a page that answers one question with public data only: what was promised, budgeted, built and accepted, and when. Budget planned against executed by year, from the open budget portal. Projects promised, completed and stalled, from the national works register and from completions the hub has confirmed. Audit findings, from the supreme audit institution. Participation events held. Kenya's county absorption rates, Peru's stalled-works count and Costa Rica's transparency index by institution are the kind of input, each with its source and date. Chapter 15 says why this is a record and not a rating, and why no official is ever its subject.

Civic agents, under the same mandates. A resident's agent, a contractor's agent and a municipality's standing representative work on these pages under the same registry and mandates (chapters 5 and 8); nothing is sent without a person.

One budget line, one building

This is how the layers meet, told as the system would run it, with roles and without numbers. The place is any district whose law provides for participatory works and whose procurement authority publishes in the open standard.

A school roof, from the budget line to the signature
  1. January
    the hub's data steward · the civic hub

    The district's approved budget is published on the national portal. The hub's import, under the operator's grant, links the capital line for the school roof to the public project page that the previous year's vote created. The page now shows the idea, the costed note, the ballot result and the budget line, each with its source.

  2. March
    the procurement officer · the district

    The tender is published on the national portal in the open standard. The hub imports the notice as a sourced page under the project and shows it to the contractors who opted in to roofing in the region. Their agents draft bids; people submit them on the official portal, because the engine never submits a bid.

  3. July
    the inspector and the project committee · on site

    The award was imported in May and the winning company, a participant, linked its agreed baseline and stages to the project. Now the trusses are up and the hidden fixings are about to be covered. The contractor records the stage with dated, placed photographs; the district's inspector signs a short act; three committee members, who took the hub's course on witnessing a hidden-works acceptance, confirm what they saw against it.

  4. August
    a resident · the neighbourhood

    A resident photographs water pooling on the new roof after the first rain and submits an observation through their agent. The hub's trust reviewer holds their identity. The observation is public as "received", then "acknowledged" when the district's representative answers from the record. A defect is opened against the stage.

  5. October
    the district's finance office · the municipality

    Final acceptance: the inspector's act, the committee's confirmation, the remediation accepted. The hub sends a payment-release signal to the district's own finance system, and the district pays through its own rails. The contractor's record and the district's delivery record each gain one dated, sourced line.

The people who look

Monitoring is dangerous, and a design that cannot protect the monitor is not a design. The Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative's tracker counts eighty-two people killed and 166 assaulted in India since 2005 for using their right to information; the figures are from a secondary report of the tracker. The Philippines has no national whistle-blower protection law and relies on an ad-hoc witness programme; its Senate recommended one in 2026. The European Union protects whistle-blowers by a directive of 2019 and, since 2024, protects public participation against abusive lawsuits in cross-border cases. The difference between those legal environments must be a fact in the country pack, with its source, and it must change how a hub behaves.

So the public-works record is built around the observer's safety. An observation is pseudonymous by default: the hub's trust reviewer knows who made it and the page does not. It carries the location of the asset and never the location of the reporter. Where protection is weak, the pack forces pseudonymity, the operator may embargo observations until the inspector responds, and a resident may submit through an institution with standing, a legally recognised oversight committee, an auditor or a monitoring organisation, so that no individual is exposed. Publishing "the contractor failed" is an allegation; publishing "the acceptance act of this date records defects; remediation not confirmed as of this date; the contractor's response follows" is a sourced fact with a reply, and that is the only form a hub publishes. Officials' names are ingested only as they appear in the public source, never enriched, never made into a profile. And the operator has a written duty to suspend a campaign that endangers people, which no setting can remove. None of this is legal advice; each country's defamation, data and protection law is a question for the operator's counsel, recorded in the pack.

One page on which the money and the building meet, with a person who can be held to each: that is what Trustwork proposes to build, from parts the industry already needs.

Open data about money is not evidence about buildings. One page, every source named, is.
