A society that knows how to build

A mason without a certificate and a town without a budget-literate public are two faces of one gap: the skill and the money are real, and the record and the understanding are missing. This chapter is about closing that gap from both sides, so that the people who build can prove what they know, and the people who pay can read what they are paying for. It takes what the world has learned about training self-builders, recognising informal skill, building safely after disasters and naming a culture of building, and says what Trustwork's learning, events and magazine become in the civic engine. The civic parts are a direction of this book, not yet in the engine's designs.

The skill is real; the record is missing

Construction is one of the sectors with the most informal work, and the International Labour Organization's work on the recognition of prior learning shows what that means for one person. In Jordan, construction workers, most of them Syrian refugees and Jordanians, were assessed against industry standards and received certificates that formally acknowledged what they already knew; one programme document counts 2,600 of them. Kenya finished a national policy on recognising prior learning after a pilot and planned to roll it out to trades including carpenters, masons and plumbers. Malawi piloted it and had assessed and certified more than five hundred artisans by August 2026. The ILO's office in Sri Lanka observed in 2017 that migrant construction workers return home with transferable skills and no certificate, and asked for an accessible, transparent assessment system. The pattern is the same in every case: the skill exists, the record does not, and the missing record is what keeps the worker informal, uninsured and underpaid.

The engine's answer exists in design and as a demo. A certificate on a hub is a public record anyone can check by its number, and it says plainly what it means: this person completed this course and passed its assessment, not a state qualification or a licence. The civic engine adds the next step, not yet designed in the engine: a recognition pathway, in which an assessor accredited by a training authority or a professional body, recorded with a source, assesses a worker's prior learning on the hub, and the hub issues a certificate that names the assessor and the standard. The certificate goes into the worker's reputation passport, a signed, revocable, holder-controlled credential in the W3C Verifiable Credentials format, a web standard since 2019 and in its 2.0 version since May 2025, compatible with Open Badges and mappable to Europe's learning model, so that it travels with the worker between employers, hubs and borders and cannot be lost or forged. No ministry or professional body recognises Trustwork certificates today; the book promises the standard, not the recognition.

How self-building changed where it changed

The programmes that changed how poor families build did not start from inspection. They started from advice, small credit and respected local people. A cement manufacturer's programme in Mexico and Latin America has, since 1998, combined materials on credit, technical advice and a savings schedule for families building incrementally, delivered through more than two hundred promoters from the communities themselves, most of them women; the company reports more than 650,000 families served by the end of 2022, a figure that is its own. An engineering non-profit working in Nepal after the 2015 earthquakes reports more than 24,000 houses built or strengthened, more than 52,000 homeowners who came to its technical support centres, more than 8,000 government engineers and construction professionals trained, and a public campaign of forum theatre and films that reached hundreds of thousands; the government approved and published its confined-masonry house design in 2017. Those figures are the organisation's own, and this book cites no independent evaluation of safety outcomes. Nepal's reconstruction itself was homeowner-driven by law: owners chose from approved designs, managed their own builders, and received a grant of 300,000 rupees in three tranches, released against inspected construction stages by engineers posted to municipalities; the reconstruction authority reported ninety-two per cent of private housing rebuilt by the end of its term in 2021.

Read together, the three say one thing. Culture travelled through people who were paid and respected for carrying it, a promoter, a mason-trainer, a municipal engineer, and it was tied to money released against evidence. That is the shape of Trustwork's plan-to-people journey: a self-builder gets a real plan, consultations with experts who earn income and reputation for giving them, and staged acceptance of each stage with a short act and photographs. The missing piece in the places the engine is needed most is the respected local master, and the engine gives that person a role, a record and a living: the mentor whose consultations are counted by outcome and whose students' certificates carry their name. Those lessons stay free and arrive on the plan step itself, for the reasons chapter 2 gives.

Where buildings fall

The collapses counted in Nigeria, and the causes the regulators name every time, appear in chapter 11. Investigations there often end in "unknown factors", and no audited breakdown of causes exists; each named cause is a record that was never made. The guild that keeps the count is a professional body trying to teach a society not to let its buildings fall. A hub in such a place is a learning institution before it is a marketplace: courses on stage certification and soil testing taught by the engineers who investigate the collapses, certificates for the workers who take them, a magazine that reports every collapse with sources, and execution records that make the missing record ordinary.

A culture of building, with a name

"Culture of building" risks sounding like a slogan, and it has a definition. In January 2018 the European ministers of culture adopted the Davos Declaration, which treats building as a cultural act and the whole built environment, infrastructure and public space included, as culture. In 2021 the Swiss Federal Office of Culture published a quality system that makes the idea measurable: eight criteria, each with an assessment questionnaire, and a quality statement required on all eight. The criteria are governance, functionality, environment, economy, diversity, context, sense of place and beauty. Social, cultural and emotional qualities stand on an equal footing with the technical, environmental and economic ones. The word for this is Baukultur, and the book keeps the German word because no English one carries the same weight.

Two things make this more than a European curiosity. The first criterion is governance: how a place is decided, by whom, and how openly. That ties the culture of building directly to the loop of public trust: a participatory project decided in public, costed by a named professional, built with evidence and confirmed by residents scores on governance before a brick is laid. And the eight criteria give a hub's magazine, events and courses an existing vocabulary to organise around, and a public-works page an optional short statement on each criterion, written by the proposers and the professionals who costed it, never scored. The system is European in origin, and a hub adopts it only if its operator chooses to: a tool to adapt, never a standard to impose.

Budget literacy and the youngest voters

A public that understands its budget is the precondition of the civic loop. The instruments are modest and proven. A citizens' budget is the budget in plain language, and the Open Budget Survey has scored its publication for years. Budget schools, short courses for residents and journalists on reading a municipal budget, are run by budget groups and universities in many countries; the practice is well attested and its outcomes uncounted, so the book describes it and does not measure it. The cheapest civic education there is turns out to be school and youth participatory budgeting: Paris lets residents vote from the age of seven; New York from eleven, regardless of immigration status; Boston has run a youth budget of a million dollars a year since 2014; Portugal's schools budget has run since 2017; a Council of Europe school programme in two Georgian cities reached more than nine hundred students, teachers and administrators. A twelve-year-old who has argued for a school yard, seen it costed, voted, and watched it be built, or not, has learned what a budget is better than any lesson could teach.

The engine's first ring is itself a literacy device: a reader who sees a named source and a visible correction every week learns what to expect from news, and what to ask of a budget.

What learning, events and the magazine become

The civic engine does not add a school to the hub. It gives the hub's existing learning, events and magazine a public audience and a public subject.

  • Courses for the people who confirm

    How to read a bill of quantities. What a stalled work looks like. How to photograph for evidence. How to witness a hidden-works acceptance. Short, practical, with a certificate checkable by number, for the members of a project committee, for residents who observe, for journalists.

  • Courses for the people who build

    Stage certification, soil testing, the local code's winter concreting, installing a manufacturer's system, taught by the manufacturer and labelled as such. How to bid on the national procurement portal, for the small contractor who has never won public work; the supplier bid desk is designed for exactly this.

  • Recognition of prior learning

    Not yet designed, and recognised by nobody yet; the page says so until it is.

  • Events that are hearings

    A public hearing on a budget is an event with a programme and a record; a visit to a half-built public work is a site visit. Registration, materials and attendance certificates work as they do for any event.

  • The magazine follows the money

    One sourced story per issue that follows a budget line into a building. A "what was promised, what was built" edition at the end of each participatory cycle, built from the public-works records, with the institution's reply. Every number sourced; every correction an edition.

  • Youth cycles on the same module

    A school's participatory budget runs on the same module with small sums, so that the first budget a person ever votes on is one whose result they can see built.

What stays human

Learning is the part of the engine where agents help least and people matter most, and the design keeps it that way. An agent can suggest a course from a plan step, draft a quiz from a standard, remind a learner, and tell a contractor which certificate a tender requires. It cannot assess prior learning; an accredited person does, under their name. It cannot teach a mason to feel when a mix is wrong; a master does, on site, and the hub gives the master a record. It cannot decide whether a course deserves accreditation; a professional body does, and the hub shows the decision with its source. And it cannot make a society want to build well. What a hub can do is make the people who already build well visible, paid and copied, and put the budget in front of residents in words they can read, early enough to argue. A society that knows how to build is one that has been let into the conversation about what gets built, and has learned, from its own projects, what the answers cost.

Teach the craft to the hands that build, and the budget to the people who pay.
