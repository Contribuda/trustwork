The future we are building for

Four views shape every decision in Trustwork: who will build the homes the next decades need and how; how people and their agents will work together in an industry and a city; what the state and society will expect of a record like ours, and what they must never be allowed to do with it; and what the engine must become in the next five years so that it is ready when the world arrives there.

Who will build the next homes

The homes the world needs in the coming decades will mostly be built by the people who live in them, in cities that are growing faster than their plans. We do not claim a global count; the sourced figures are enough. In Peru about seventy per cent of homes are self-built, by the Housing Ministry's own account. In Nairobi, about sixty per cent of residents are reported to live in informal settlements, and the city builds a fraction of the homes it needs each year. The same picture holds in Mexico, Colombia, Manila, Accra, Lagos and Kigali; chapter 20 gives the figures country by country.

This is the world Trustwork's plan-to-people was designed for. A family that will build its own house incrementally, as money arrives, does not need a marketplace; it needs a plan it can understand, advice from a professional it can afford for an hour, a mason whose record it can check, and a way to prove at each stage that the work was done, so that one day a lender or an insurer will take the risk alongside it. The programmes that changed self-building at scale had this shape, and none of them left a portable record that the family owned. The engine's job is to make that record a by-product of building well, in the family's own language, on a phone.

The other side of the same future is safety. Kenya's engineers are asking for a national building register so that anyone can check who designed and who approved a building. Florida answered a collapse that killed ninety-eight people with inspections at fixed ages and records posted online. The Dominican Republic discovered in April 2025 that it had no inspection code in law. Each asks for the same object: a public page per building with who designed, who built, who approved, who inspected and when. That page is Trustwork's sourced page by another name, and the engine can offer it to every country long before any vote is involved.

People and agents

The routine of an industry is moving to machines: finding, matching, comparing, scheduling, drafting, reminding, summarising, reconciling. What remains with people, and matters more than before, is judgement and responsibility: the decision to build, the acceptance of a stage, the professional's signature, the licensed act, the movement of money, the word given to a third party. Trustwork's model of the two is simple and strict. Each person has one representative agent, acting only under mandates they can read, change and revoke, and a company may have one standing representative with a named accountable person (chapter 8). It never commits on chat text; money and commitments need a confirmed mandate, a budget and the person's own confirmation.

There is no swarm, no panel of bots, no agent that acts for nobody. The future we expect is not agents replacing people in construction; concrete does not pour itself and a stage does not accept itself. It is an industry in which the paperwork that used to make trust expensive is done by agents under mandate, and the acts that make trust real are done by people under their own name.

In the civic ring the line is the same and sharper. A resident's agent can watch a public-works page, prepare an observation from the resident's own photographs, draft a question to the procuring office under the resident's name, and remind them of a hearing. A municipality's standing representative can answer observations. Nothing is sent without a person, and no agent votes, confirms, inspects or decides. The world is moving in the same direction where it moves well: cities that let a machine pre-check a building permit keep the decision with the reviewer, and Seattle's evaluation of 2026 found the pre-check caught most of what reviewers caught while reviewers kept the last word; Singapore announced in 2026 a registry of the AI agents its public service runs, so that each is known and accountable. People's expectations are plain and fair: in the OECD's trust survey of 2026, more than four in ten thought AI could make government serve them better, and fewer believed governments would keep it safe. An engine that puts agents in public processes must show its safeguards before it shows its savings.

What stays human

Decisions. Acceptance. Licensed acts. Money. The assessment of a skill. The inspector's measurement. The resident's vote. The council's budget. The editor's by-line. The appeal, decided by a different person. The frontier of what an agent can prepare moves every year; the list of what only a person can do in Trustwork does not.

The state and society

Trust in national government is a minority position across the OECD, thirty-nine per cent in 2024 and forty in 2026, and the largest gap in the data is between people who feel they have a say and people who do not: sixty-nine per cent of the first trust their government, twenty-two per cent of the second, in the 2024 round. A loop that closes, from a resident's idea to a finished work the resident can confirm, is a "have a say" machine, and it is the one thing in Trustwork that touches trust in institutions directly.

The rails are being laid. The United Nations' Global Digital Compact of September 2024 names open-source software, data and standards as digital public goods and commits to investing in them and in digital public infrastructure by 2030. Dozens of countries are building shared identity, payment and data-exchange layers; open contracting has a standard in which nine of the fourteen countries of this book and Ukraine already publish nationally, and one more through a city; participation platforms are free software recognised as digital public goods; a signed credential is a web standard. Trustwork does not build identity or payments; it plugs into them through adapters, and it intends to stand beside those platforms as a recognised digital public good itself.

The risks are as real as the rails, and they decide where the engine may go. Surveillance. A state that can read a resident's observations, votes and complaints as a profile has a social-credit system, whatever it calls it. Residents are never the object of a record; the ballot is unlinkable; a civic role carries no reputation. Capture. Open government can be lost as fast as it is gained: the United States, which co-founded the Open Government Partnership, withdrew from it by a letter of 15 January 2026, and Georgia, ranked first in the world for budget transparency in 2023, was permanently suspended from the same partnership on 13 April 2026 over restrictions on civic space, including its laws on foreign influence. So civic-space standing is a gate, not a score: a hub enables the civic ring only where a lawful operator can work and where the people who use the record are safe, and the gate is re-checked every year. Where it is shut, the construction hub runs alone, and chapter 20 says so. Exclusion. A digital-only vote measures the connected; a co-financing rule that rewards the neighbourhoods that can pay excludes the ones that cannot; a monitoring design that exposes the monitor gets people hurt. So paper and assisted channels count alike, co-financing is optional and capped, the poorest areas are served first, and the person who looks is protected by design.

Neutrality follows from all of this: Trustwork serves evidence, not parties, and chapter 15 says what that requires.

The five-year horizon

Trustwork's arc runs five years, and chapter 19 tells it year by year. The order is the point: construction first; the civic ring only with a consenting municipality and a lawful operator; standards before scale; an operators' association before the fourth operator; self-funding before the tenth country. Through all five years, still no score of residents, ever.

Agents will do the paperwork of trust. People will keep its acts.
