The missions we set ourselves

A mission says what the project is for, and why. There are seven. The first three belong to the construction industry, where the engine is designed to work first. The next three are the civic ring that grows out of it. The last is about the engine itself: that it runs anywhere and belongs to no one. For each we say why it is needed, what it looks like five years on, how we will know without ever watching an individual, and what stays human. The evidence for each lives in the chapter that treats it fully; here it is only named. The years follow the arc in Part VII; they are its shape, not dates.

1. Make every builder visible

Why. Most of the people who build the world have no record of what they can do. Informal work is 57.8 per cent of all employment worldwide, more than two billion people, by the ILO's count for 2024, and construction is one of the three sectors where it is most common. The skill is real and the certificate is missing. Chapter 14 shows what changes when that record is supplied.

Five years on. Every worker, professional and company on a hub has a lifelong profile that belongs to them and not to their employer, a portfolio backed by evidence and confirmations tied to real work, certificates checkable by number, and a passport that travels between hubs and across borders. In at least two countries, the hub is the place where an accredited body assesses an informal worker's prior learning and records it once. A young contractor in a regional city is found by the client who needs them, for reasons the matching explains.

How we will know. By counts, never by faces: certificates issued and checked, profiles that survived a change of employer, confirmations tied to real transactions, and, in consented research, whether participants feel more in control of their working life. We measure the record's reach, never a person.

What stays human. The assessment of a skill by an accredited assessor. The decision to publish a profile, by recorded, revocable consent. The confirmation of work by the counterparty who received it. Nobody is made visible against their will, and no agent confirms anything on anyone's behalf.

2. Make every build clearer and safer

Why. Where building is informal and inspection weak, buildings fall. Kenya's construction authority found 10,791 of 14,895 sampled buildings unsafe to occupy in a 2022 audit, as the press reported; Nigeria's count of collapses and the causes regulators name are in chapter 11, and every one of those causes is a missing record.

Five years on. Anyone who launches a project, a house on a lot in San Diego or a room added to a family home in Lima, has a real plan: phases, trades, permits and inspections, budget ranges, risks and the decisions ahead. Plan items become consultations with experts and tasks for trades, and hidden works are accepted stage by stage with dated, placed photographs before they are covered. Finished projects become shared knowledge with the owner's consent, so that a self-builder in Accra can read, in their own language, how someone else solved the same wall. Manufacturers' knowledge sits on the plan steps where it fits, never because anyone paid. When the build ends, its record continues as the family's house record (chapter 2), and the lesson for each step stays free (chapter 14).

How we will know. Plans approved and completed, consultations accepted, stage acceptances recorded with evidence, defects recorded and remedied, and each hub's open statistics on cost, completion time and defect rates by stage and building type, aggregated from consenting projects. Whether buildings fall less often is for the professional bodies that count collapses to say; we will give them better records.

What stays human. The plan is approved by its owner. The advice is given by a licensed professional under their own name. A stage is accepted by a person who was there. An inspector's act is an inspector's. The agent prepares, reminds, matches and reports; it never pours concrete and never signs for it.

3. Make every private build bankable and insurable

Why. Private construction has, almost nowhere, any tool for lending, because there is no standard; the diagnosis holds wherever one looks. The reason is the object, not the borrower: an unfinished house with no standard plan, no inspected stages and no record of its builder cannot be valued, lent against or insured. Chapter 10 makes the case in full.

Five years on. Every Trustwork build produces, as a by-product of the work being recorded, a standard, evidenced build file: the plan and its versions, permits, the named professionals, frozen quotes, inspection gates, field evidence, acceptances, changes and defects, and the contractor's record. Trustwork publishes the file's format as an open standard, so that many lenders and insurers can read one shape. With the borrower's consent, a licensed lender releases money stage by stage against accepted evidence and an independent inspector's act; an insurer prices from evidence rather than from guesswork. A family borrows and insures its own house as it rises.

How we will know. Build files completed to the standard; lenders and insurers that read it, counted as institutions, never as households; stage releases made on accepted evidence; and, over time, the defect and overrun statistics of financed builds against unfinanced ones. We will publish what did not work too.

What stays human, and what stays outside Trustwork. Trustwork never lends, never holds money and never decides who is creditworthy; licensed lenders, insurers and inspectors act under their own law, and Trustwork supplies the evidenced file, with the borrower's consent, and nothing else (chapter 10).

4. Let residents decide

Why. Participatory budgeting began in Porto Alegre in 1989 and has spread to thousands of cities. The projects residents choose are overwhelmingly built things, and its weakest link is always delivery: the vote is decided in a day, and the building takes years. Chapter 12 gives the evidence.

Five years on. Any hub can enable the civic ring under its country's law, with a consenting municipality or civic body. Ideas are co-designed and costed by a named professional before the ballot; any vote is held by a neutral keeper, the municipality or an electoral institute, one person one vote, with no link from ballot to person that any role, including ours, can follow; co-financing, the mark of initiative budgeting, is optional and capped, so that the poorest neighbourhoods are served first. Where the law does not allow it, or the civic space is closed, the construction hub runs alone, and we say so.

How we will know. Projects proposed, costed, voted, funded, contracted, built and confirmed, as a pipeline a resident can read; the share of winners accepted within the promised time; the share dropped, each with a stated reason; turnout as a share of all residents. We will never know who voted for what, and we build so that we cannot.

What stays human. The idea, the discussion, the vote, the council's budget, the inspector's act, the committee's confirmation. The engine carries the record from one human act to the next; it decides nothing and replaces no council, auditor or court.

5. Follow public money into concrete

Why. Governments publish more than they listen: the world's average budget transparency score has hovered around forty-five out of a hundred for a decade, and its average for public participation is seventeen, by the Open Budget Survey of 2025. And open data about money is not evidence about buildings. The Philippine flood-control scandal of 2025 showed it: every notice had been posted, and the works did not exist (chapter 11). Photographed evidence of public works exists, and India has kept it for a decade; what does not exist is a shared, portable record of it, joined to the budget line and the contract (chapter 13).

Five years on. Every public work in a hub's territory has a public-works record, from the budget line and the contract to the stages with field evidence, the inspector's act and residents' confirmations, with what remains unknown shown as unknown. Every institution that commissions works has a delivery record of what it promised against what it built, with the right of reply. A resident can see what their vote became.

How we will know. Public works with a complete record; resident confirmations compared against a professional measurement, because monitoring without a measurement and a consequence does little; responses by institutions to observations, and their time; and the gap, published, between what was promised and what stands.

What stays human. The inspector's measurement. The resident's observation, made safely. The institution's reply. The journalist's piece. We never rate an official as a person; people in public roles appear only as signatories of documents that are already public.

6. Raise the culture of building

Why. A society that understands its built environment and its budgets gets better buildings and keeps more of its money. "Culture of building" has a European definition and a published measure, governance first (chapter 14). Culture travels through people who are paid and respected for building well, not through posters.

Five years on. Every hub reads a magazine with named sources and accurate dates, in which a weekly piece follows one budget line into one building. Events are conferences, site visits and public hearings alike. Courses teach a mason the next trade, a committee member how to witness the acceptance of a hidden work, a journalist how to read a municipal budget, with certificates mapped, in at least one country, to a national qualifications framework.

How we will know. Courses completed and certificates checked; events held, as totals; magazine corrections, published; and, in consented research, whether readers say they understand their budget and their building better. We do not count what a person read.

What stays human. Teaching. Editing. Deciding what is worth a site visit. Writing the sentence that explains why the drain matters to the people who will never read a budget. Agents draft, summarise and remind; the by-line is a person's, and so is the correction.

7. Run anywhere, belong to no one

Why. An engine that helps the state and society cannot be owned by a company, captured by a government or dependent on one donor. Open civic projects live on diversified income and die on one funder: the Sunlight Foundation closed in 2020, Code for America's brigades lost their sponsor in 2023, the Open Collective Foundation dissolved in 2024, and USAID stopped implementing foreign assistance on 1 July 2025, taking much of the money that paid for open-government work. Decidim and OpenStreetMap survived by widening their base. Trustwork is already free software under the AGPL-3.0-only licence, and a careful reading of the Digital Public Goods Standard's nine indicators finds its gaps to be documentation and policy, not architecture.

Five years on. Hubs in ten or more countries, run by local operators under one charter that carries the invariants and the list of what we will never build; an operators' association independent of any one funder; Contribuda as maintainer on the terms of chapter 7; the engine recognised as a digital public good, or the gap list published; income to hubs from commissions, events, courses and services, never from ranking and never from data; and a public failure log.

How we will know. Independent operators; country packs maintained in their own country; an association budget published every year; a second implementation of the protocol; a charter every hub has signed.

What stays human. The decision to open a hub and to take legal responsibility for it. Counsel's review of each country's law. The association's vote. The maintainer's refusal to build what the charter forbids, even when a government asks.

The seven together

Read as one, the missions are a single sentence. Make every builder visible so that every build can be clearer and safer; make every build evidenced so that a bank and an insurer can trust it; let residents decide what to build with common money and follow that money into concrete with the same record; teach a society to read its buildings and its budgets; and keep the engine that does this open and owned by the people who run it. The civic ring is the same wall, one course higher.

What we promise is a record. What we hope for is a culture.
