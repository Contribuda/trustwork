Trust is built like a building

A wall is not bought. It is laid, one course at a time, each brick bedded in mortar on the course below, and anyone may walk up to it, look, tap it, and see whether it was done well. This is the central idea of Trustwork, and it shapes the engine, its mark and its civic ring: trust is built the way a building is built, from small accepted facts, and it can be inspected, repaired, and remembered.

Course by course

Every trade knows that a wall is only as good as its first course. In Trustwork the first course is evidence, the small accepted facts of the first chapter: each a brick, small enough to be checked by one person in a minute, hard enough to bear weight.

Acceptance is the mortar. A brick lying loose on the ground is a claim. It becomes part of the wall only when the counterparty, the person who received the work or the inspector who measured it, beds it in with a recorded decision. In the engine this is a state machine, not a metaphor: a claim is made, evidence is attached, and the claim is confirmed or disputed by someone with the standing to do so. A photograph is not acceptance. A model's summary is not acceptance. Only a person's recorded decision is, and it carries their name.

A reputation is the wall. It is nothing more than the courses laid so far, visible to anyone who cares to look: how many, over what period, of what kind, by which method they were counted, with what excluded and why. Nobody can buy a wall. You can only lay it. Nobody can lay it for you in a night. And a wall has three properties that a score does not. It can be inspected: every brick is still there to be looked at, with its evidence. It can be repaired: a bad course is not painted over but taken out in the open, with a note saying what was wrong and what replaced it, and the appeal is heard by someone other than the person who laid the judgement. And it remembers: a course laid ten years ago under a different employer is still yours, because the wall belongs to the person who built it.

A score is a photograph of a wall from far away; it tells you there is a wall and nothing about the bricks.

The mark, read

Trustwork's mark is three blue squares. It has no official meaning; what follows is one reading of it. Set on a two-by-two grid, the three squares are three courses already laid and one place still open. We read the laid squares as the three who build with trust today: the person who builds, the company that delivers, and the public that commissions with common money. We read the open square as the resident, the community, the next participant, the next hub, the next craft. It is not ours to fill. If the engine ever drew that fourth square itself, by scoring the resident or by deciding for the community, it would have stopped being what it is. The gap is the promise.

The loop of public trust

Lay the courses in a city instead of on a site and the wall becomes a loop. Public money becomes visible in the built environment, and every step from an idea to a finished public work is a brick that someone can accept or dispute. The loop below is the civic ring of the engine. It is a direction of this book, not yet designed in the engine's handbook; nothing in it is built; it is shown here because it is the idea the rest of the book serves.

Three things about the loop matter more than its shape. First, the evidence weighs as much as the vote: votes are fast, concrete is slow, and the programmes that hold put a verified act between the contractor and the money, as Makueni County in Kenya does by paying a contractor only after a committee of residents has approved the work. Second, confirmation needs a measurement to check against and a consequence attached, so the residents' confirmation sits beside an engineer's bill of quantities and an inspector's act, and it triggers something: a payment signal, a defect record. Chapter 12 gives the evidence for both. Third, the loop records institutions and companies, never residents. The ballot is unlinkable to the person by any role, including ours.

One year, step by step

This chapter follows one year in one municipality, because a year is the rhythm of a public work. The place is a mid-sized municipality in the Dominican Republic, and the choice is deliberate. Dominican law has required every municipality to run a participatory budget since 2007, with a works committee for each project, and every tender is published as open data; chapter 20 explains why the arc places the first full loop there.

Everything that follows is told as the system will run it. No municipality has agreed to anything; the people are roles, not persons; no figure is given because none is known. The vote is the municipality's, held under its law; the hub supports the record around it.

A barrio, a drain, a budget year
  1. January
    a resident · a barrio on the edge of town

    The street floods every rainy season where the storm drain was never finished. The resident has photographed it from the doorstep for years. This time their agent, under a mandate they gave it, knows from the country pack that the participatory-budget calendar opens this month. It drafts the idea in their words, attaches the dated photographs, and asks whether to submit. They change a sentence, and submit.

  2. February
    neighbours and the planning office · the community assembly

    The assembly meets in the school, with the municipality's planners present. The drain and the broken culvert under the same street are merged into one idea; the hub's page records each version and who proposed it, from the assembly's own minutes. Nobody votes yet. Nobody is scored.

  3. March
    a civil engineer · registered with the national college, paid by the municipality

    The idea becomes a consultation. The engineer walks the street, measures, and returns a note: what the drain needs, a cost range, and what could not be seen without opening the ground. It is labelled as a professional estimate with the engineer's name and method, and the engineer's record will later show how the real price landed against the range.

  4. April
    the section assembly and the open council · the vote

    The costed ideas are voted on under the municipality's own rules, one person one vote. The hub runs no ballot and holds no roll; it shows the result as a sourced page from the municipality, with the counts and the method, and nothing about who voted. The drain wins.

  5. May
    the council and the barrio · the budget line

    The approved plan enters the municipal budget, as the law requires, and the drain's page links to the budget line. The barrio elects its community works committee: five neighbours, shown on the page by role, who will watch that the work follows the plan and the money.

  6. June
    the procurement office · the open tender

    The tender is published through the national procurement office and imported as a sourced page on the drain's record. Four contractors answer. The winning bid becomes an agreement both sides can prove identical.

  7. July
    the contractor · a small firm from the next town

    The firm's page was sourced from procurement data months ago; now its owner claims it, with evidence. The company's agent, under a standing mandate with a named accountable person, lays out the stages from the agreement and the engineer's note, each with the evidence it will need before acceptance.

  8. August
    the foreman and two committee members · the drain bed, before it is covered

    Before the pipe is laid, the foreman records the bed with dated, placed photographs against the drawing, and two committee members stand in the trench and attach their own observation. The municipality's inspector measures the next morning and signs the act. Three records, three authors, one stage. Then the concrete covers it, and the record is all that remains of what was underneath.

  9. September
    the inspector and the committee · a defect

    The culvert's headwall is not to the drawing. The inspector records a defect, the committee agrees, and the firm's owner confirms a remedy under their own name. Three weeks later the remedy is accepted. The defect stays on the record, and so does its remedy. Nothing is painted over.

  10. October
    the works committee · the confirmation that releases money

    The surface is finished before the rains. The inspector's final act, the contractor's completion statement and the committee's confirmation, checked against the engineer's bill of quantities, sit as three records on one page. The hub sends one signal to the municipality's finance system that the stage is accepted, and the municipality pays through its own channels. Trustwork has held no money at any point.

  11. November
    the hub's editorial office · lessons

    The magazine follows the budget line into the drain, every source named and the defect included. The works committee becomes the maintenance committee, as the law provides, and its members take a short course on witnessing hidden works. The contractor's record shows one public work, on time, one defect remedied; the municipality's delivery record shows one project promised, budgeted, built and accepted, with dates.

  12. December
    the resident · the same doorstep

    It rains, and the street does not flood. The resident photographs it anyway and attaches it to the drain's record as a resident observation. Their agent mentions that the calendar opens again in January, and that the stair to the upper street has been on their list for years.

Each month of that year rests on a design choice: the public project, not the vote, is the unit; costing is a paid consultation; tenders are sealed and agreements frozen; every stage carries three records by separate authors; Trustwork sends a payment signal and never moves money; records are kept for companies and institutions, never for residents; the law comes from the country pack; and the person who looks is protected. Chapters 12, 13 and 15 set out each choice.

The loop is not electronic voting, not a government's scoring of its residents, and not a replacement for councils, auditors, inspectors or courts. Chapter 15 sets out these lines.

Nobody can buy a wall. You can only lay it.
