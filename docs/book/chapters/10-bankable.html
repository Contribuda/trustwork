A build a bank can trust

A family that builds its own house almost nowhere can borrow for it, and almost nowhere can insure it while it rises. This chapter says why, what has worked in the places that solved parts of the problem, and how Trustwork will turn the record it is designed to keep of a build into a file a licensed lender and a licensed insurer can read. Trustwork never lends, never holds money and never advises on credit. It supplies the standard and the evidence. The records this chapter builds on exist in the engine; the build file itself is a direction of this book, not yet in the repository's designs.

Where a private build is financed at all, there is a standard record of the plan, money released in stages against an inspection, a warranty or defect insurance tied to those inspections, and a title that can carry a lien. Where any of the four is missing, the family pays cash, builds in bursts as money arrives, and carries every risk alone.

The problem is the object, not the borrower

Most housing in the developing world is built by its owners, in stages, from savings, remittances and small loans. The World Bank's stocktaking of housing in Sub-Saharan Africa says that "most housing is built incrementally" and that in 2011 only three per cent of the region's population was eligible for a conventional mortgage. Kenya, a country of more than fifty million people, had 30,015 residential mortgages outstanding in 2023, and 284,472 microfinance loans. Roughly ninety per cent of Ghana's housing stock is produced informally, by a 2011 estimate. In Nigeria 71.4 per cent of landlords held no title in a national survey of 2018. UN-Habitat's World Cities Report of 2026 concludes that "conventional housing finance systems have generally failed to engage with the reality of incremental upgrading." Nor is the problem confined to poor countries: in the United States, 176,932 single-family homes were started in 2024 on the owner's own land, 17.5 per cent of all single-family starts, and lending for them exists only through a specialised product with its own rules.

Lenders refuse not because the borrower is poor but because the object is unreadable. Read any working instrument from the inside and the same gaps appear, each a missing record rather than a missing person.

  • No valuation of an unfinished asset. A lender needs an "as completed" value and a way to re-check it as the building rises.
  • No standard plan and budget. Stage payments, against interim valuations or a fixed schedule of costs, need a plan broken into stages.
  • No reliable builder. Where no contractor record exists, nobody can be prequalified.
  • No inspection of stages. Without an inspector, the lender can pay nothing until completion, which the owner cannot pre-finance, or pay in advance and hope.
  • Change without a record. A loan may rise "only to cover documented increased construction costs".
  • Title. The lien is the collateral; without a registrable title there is no mortgage, only a personal loan.
  • Informal income. Where most work is informal, the answer is rarely a twenty-year mortgage but a sequence of small loans, each secured by the stage it finances, which again needs the stage recorded.

Insurers refuse for the same reasons and one more: nobody records defects. A private build with no history of who built what and what was fixed has no actuarial history. France solved this by law, making defect insurance compulsory for builder and owner in 1978 and producing decades of claims data; Britain through a warranty provider that inspects key stages during the build and then insures the house for ten years. Both depend on a record kept while the house was being built, not reconstructed afterwards.

What worked elsewhere

The instruments differ in law and language and agree in shape. In the United States a construction-to-permanent loan covers the build and the mortgage that follows: the appraisal is of the house "as completed", the lender inspects "prior to issuing draws" and holds back undrawn funds, and the loan converts only on a completion report, lien waivers and a certificate of occupancy. In Britain a self-build mortgage pays at named stages, "wall plate level", "wind and watertight", "first fix and plastering", after the lender's valuer has seen the work, and the lender expects a ten-year warranty whose provider inspected the build. Germany wrote the schedule into law for homes bought from a developer: up to seven instalments, thirty per cent after earthworks begin, forty per cent of the balance at the shell, five per cent on completion. France made insurance the licence to build: a constructor must hold ten-year liability cover before opening the site, and if no insurer will quote, a tariff office sets the premium.

The countries Trustwork looks at first have pieces of the same machine. Peru pays a family a housing bond of 32,100 soles to build on its own registered, lien-free plot, through a registered technical entity that signs the contract, submits plans and budget, triggers the disbursement and has 120 days to finish: the state standardised the role, and nobody has yet standardised the record. Mexico's housing fund lets a worker build on land they own with money released in stages, as the press describes it; the Philippines' housing fund releases construction loans in tranches after inspection, as a law firm's commentary describes it. Kenya has a mortgage refinance company that sets eligibility criteria for the lenders it refinances, and a deep housing microfinance sector lending small, short sums for a room or a roof. Uzbekistan built a mortgage market in five years by building its records first, a refinancing company, a housing price index and a mortgage database, and the company had supported more than twenty thousand mortgages by January 2025.

One precedent matters more than the rest, because it shows a standard turning into a bank product. The International Finance Corporation's EDGE certificate asks for twenty per cent less energy, water and embodied carbon than a local baseline, audited by a third party. By February 2023 it had certified 357,000 housing units, in a programme active in eighty-nine countries; Colombia went from no certified new build in 2017 to more than twenty-seven per cent of new build by 2023; a South African bank launched the country's first green mortgage on 807 certified homes. The certificate did not lend. It made the object legible, and lenders priced what they could read. The Open Contracting Data Standard did the same for public contracts. Nobody has done it for the private build itself.

A bank does not need to trust the builder. It needs to read the building.

The bankable build file

A build file is a consented, versioned projection of one project for one reader: a lender, an insurer, a guarantor. It contains only the fields that reader needs, each traceable to a record the engine already keeps; it is not a new database but a projection with an audience the owner adds. Most of what it draws on is already built in the engine's API or designed; what is new is the lender as an audience, the inspector who signs inside it, and the open standard that lets every lender read it the same way. The file has versions like a plan. A lender reads version seven, decides, and the record remembers that it decided on version seven. Nothing in a file is editable; a correction is a new record and a new version. A reader with no consent does not get a refusal; it gets nothing, as if the file did not exist.

At completion the file becomes the first chapter of the family's house record (chapter 2), which a lender or insurer later reads only with the same consent.

Money released by evidence

The sequence is simple and every step of it is human. The owner approves a plan version with stage gates; the hub's stage template suggests the gates and the typical share of cost at each. The owner adds a licensed lender, and where the agreement uses one a licensed escrow provider, as a reader of the build file. The lender decides in its own systems. Work proceeds. The contractor files a work record with evidence, the inspector files an act, the owner files an acceptance. When all three exist for a stage, the engine composes a disbursement instruction: the agreement digest, the stage, the amount the agreement names, the three record references, the file version. The parties sign it; the licensed provider executes it; an unknown outcome goes to reconciliation, never to a blind retry. Lien waivers, retainage and deposit limits follow the country pack.

Trustwork is the clerk, never the cashier. That is a recorded decision and a tested rule of the engine: it never holds, pools or moves participants' funds. Where no licensed provider exists, the engine records obligations and evidence while money moves outside it, and the file is still worth having to a lender who pays by its own transfer.

Inspectors, valuers and insurers

The missing participant in every instrument above is the person who stands in the trench. An inspector on Trustwork is a person or firm with a verified credential, a mandate on this project, a narrow capability (read the shared stage and attach an act; never edit the plan, accept work or see a budget the owner has not shared) and a reputation of their own: acts filed on time, acts later contradicted by a confirmed defect, appeals heard. Their agent may schedule visits and draft the act; the person signs it. A valuer works the same way. Peru's registered technical entities, the community construction management teams of a federation of the urban poor in Durban, and the American lender's draw inspector are the three archetypes; each country pack says which licence the role needs.

An insurer added as a reader sees what a British warranty provider sees: who built, which stages were inspected and by whom, which defects were found and fixed. Its certificate, builder's risk during the works, latent defects after them, catastrophe cover where the hazard is wind, water or ground, becomes a credential with an expiry, checked like a licence, and over time the hub's open statistics give it the frequency data it lacks. Trustwork never ranks insurers, never takes a commission on a policy, and never says "buy this".

A hub's open statistics

A loan officer approving a draw for "foundations complete" needs to know whether that stage in this town usually costs what the budget says; an insurer needs the frequency of roof defects by building type. So each hub publishes, per period, region, building type and stage, the range of cost per square metre and of duration, the share of stages passed at first inspection, the defect rate by category and the frequency and cause of change. Only from projects whose owners consented to contribute; only cells above a minimum sample, checked against re-identification in small towns; never a project, a person, a company or an address; published with the method version under an open licence, and offered to the national statistics office as a contribution, not a replacement. This is the bridge to the civic ring: the same cells that tell a lender what a roof should cost tell a town what a school roof should cost, and a budget line outside the hub's range is a question worth asking in public.

An open Build File Data Standard

One lender reading one hub's export is a pilot. Many lenders reading one format is a market. Trustwork will publish a Build File Data Standard: a schema and a vocabulary for the sections above, with stable keys across versions, crosswalks to open building classifications, a mapping to the open contracting standard where a build is public, fixtures in every engine language, and a validator. Its licence will be open, so that any lender, insurer, inspector's application or national programme can read and write it without Trustwork. Trustwork owns none of the data, only the stewardship of the schema, which it can hand to a neutral body when one exists. The standard is what makes "many lenders read one format" true, as EDGE made "many banks read one certificate" true.

How we will act

Step by step, in the arc's order, and everywhere with the same rule: Trustwork is the record, never the regime. Naming an organisation in this chapter records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

First. Publish the first draft of the open standard, and add to the engine the parts the table shows as missing: the inspector and valuer roles, the stage closure record, the disbursement instruction and the consent trail for a reader. Today there are no work packages for them.

Then pilot where a staged, inspected disbursement already exists in law or practice, so the hub supplies the record to a regime that already wants it: Peru, where registered technical entities already sign the contract and trigger the bond, and where Spanish, an engine language still awaiting native review, is the reference hub's second language; Mexico and the Philippines, whose housing funds already release money in stages; Kenya, where a refinance company sets criteria for many lenders and housing microfinance is deep; and the San Diego hub, where an owner building an accessory dwelling unit under a construction-to-permanent loan already lives with draw inspections. Nigeria and Ghana come second: the need is greatest and the title base weakest, so they begin with evidence and statistics, not finance. Uzbekistan, Rwanda and the Dominican Republic follow as their programmes are studied.

Readers, not partners. A refinance company matters most: if the build file enters its eligibility criteria, every lender it refinances reads it. Housing microfinance institutions with incremental products, national housing funds that already disburse in stages, the development finance institutions behind them and inspection bodies follow; insurers are described by kind. Contact is inbound: a licensed institution that wants to read the standard asks. Chapter 21 describes them.

The lines we keep

The law around housing finance is close on every side, and the design stays on the information-only side of it until counsel says otherwise. Trustwork never lends and never holds money; that is a recorded decision and a tested refusal in the payment layer. It never gives credit or insurance advice: the agent may explain what a build file contains and what lenders generally ask for, and may never recommend a lender, a product or a sum. It never acts as a credit intermediary: the European mortgage credit directive defines one as a person who "presents or offers credit agreements" or "assists consumers by undertaking preparatory work", and excludes those "merely introducing"; Trustwork hands the owner a file, and the owner hands it to whom they choose. It never distributes insurance: the European distribution directive counts a ranked comparison with a way to buy as distribution and excludes "the provision of information on an incidental basis"; so a hub may list licensed insurers without ranking, remuneration or a button, or not at all. And it takes no remuneration from lenders or insurers for consumer housing finance, in any country: United States law forbids "any fee, kickback or other thing of value" for referring settlement-service business, and Contribuda takes no remuneration from lenders or insurers at all. Business finance is a different matter for a hub, and the engine's product specification draws the line: a hub may list licensed trade-credit and project-finance partners for companies, with any referral fee disclosed before a referral is made; consumer lending only through partners licensed for it; and payment never changes ranking, matching, reputation or what a family is shown.

Three more lines. Trustwork never produces a credit score or a "bankability score"; it produces a file, and the lender decides. Consent is specific and revocable; revocation stops the next read and cannot unsend what a lender lawfully received. And a build file may never become a condition a public subsidy programme imposes on families who do not use Trustwork; the standard is open precisely so that it never has to be.

Questions for counsel, per country, before any launch

Whether a build-file export offered to licensed lenders is "merely introducing" or an arranging activity under that country's law; how that country treats a disclosed referral fee from a licensed business lender, since for consumer housing finance there is none; the licensing of the disbursement provider; electronic signatures on acts and instructions; inspectors' liability; the data-protection basis for each shared field and each published aggregate. Nothing in this chapter is legal advice.

What stays human

Approving a plan version. Accepting a stage. Signing an act or a valuation. The credit decision, in the lender's own systems; underwriting, in the insurer's. Consenting to share, and to contribute to statistics. Agents compile the draft file for the owner's review, schedule the inspector's visit and prepare the instruction for human signature. They never approve, inspect, decide, sign or consent.

How we will know

Projects with a complete build file, and no stage marked inspected without an inspector's record. The share of stages with an independent act before a tranche moved. Cost variance and defect rates per stage. Lenders and insurers that read the standard, counted as institutions; refinance companies that cite it. Zero funds held, zero fees from lenders or insurers for consumer credit, zero findings of unlicensed activity. And what owners say at completion, in consented research: whether they borrowed, and at which stage. We will publish what did not work.
