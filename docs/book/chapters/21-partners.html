Who we build it with

Trustwork keeps the record and runs the gathering point. Everything else a hub needs in the world comes from partners: the operator who runs it, the municipality whose budget it follows, the lender who reads the build file, the body that certifies the mason, the manufacturer who explains the product, the provider who moves the money, the newsroom that reports, the funder who pays for a country pack, and Contribuda, which creates and maintains the engine. This chapter maps that network by kind and by country. Naming an organisation records its public work; it implies no contact, interest or agreement.

How to read this chapter

Two words describe engine integrations: connected means the engine really integrates something today, and planned means a work package names it and it is not integrated yet. Every organisation in this chapter is named for its public work, with a source in chapter 28. A documented API or a public register is not a partnership, and no organisation is called a partner unless an agreement exists and both sides have made it public. Contact begins with the organisation, through the addresses at the end of this book. Figures are quoted as reported.

Nothing is connected today; chapter 18 says what exists. What the engine reads from outside are public lists and open standards: the sanctions lists it screens against, the open contracting standard its adapters are designed for, the credential standards its passport is designed to issue. The partners are people and organisations, and they come later, one real case at a time.

Hub operators

The operator is the first partner in every country, and the only one without whom nothing happens. An operator brings local knowledge, legal responsibility and people; the engine brings everything else. The kinds are deliberately broad. Municipalities and public bodies

A civic partner is never named as a partner; it is a public institution whose public data a hub can read and whose consent a civic pilot needs. Chapter 20 names, country by country, the bodies that hold the budget, the procurement feed, the audit or the works register; every legal question about them is for counsel. Georgia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan appear there with the reasons their civic rings stay paused.

The financing ring

Chapter 10 asks for a build a bank can trust. The partners who make that true are licensed and regulated, and the engine never does their work: it supplies the standard and the evidenced build file, with the owner's consent, and records their acts. The lines of chapter 10 frame every row, and where a staged, inspected disbursement already exists in law or practice, the engine is the record, not the regime.

  • Readers of the file: lenders. Housing microfinance with incremental products (Kenya Women Microfinance Bank; Mibanco in Peru); national housing funds that already disburse in stages (Mexico's Infonavit and CONAVI; the Philippines' Pag-IBIG Fund; Peru's Fondo Mivivienda and the registered technical entities of Techo Propio); mortgage refinance companies that set eligibility criteria for many lenders (Kenya's KMRC, Nigeria's NMRC, Uzbekistan's refinancing company), the most influential readers, because a file in their criteria is read by every lender they refinance; construction-to-permanent lenders in the United States who already inspect before each draw.
  • Development finance behind them. IFC's housing-finance practice; the World Bank's housing-finance projects in Kenya and Rwanda; the Asian Development Bank's mortgage-market programme in Uzbekistan; the African Development Bank with Family Homes Funds in Nigeria (secondary).
  • Standards and certification bodies. IFC's EDGE certificate, which turned "green" into a bank product, and its Building Resilience Index; RICS and IVSC for the valuation of work in progress; the Open Contracting Partnership for schema practice; the European digital building logbook for interoperability.
  • Insurers and warranty providers. Latent-defect and warranty insurers (the United Kingdom's ten-year warranty schemes as the model), France's compulsory dommages-ouvrage insurers with a tariff office of last resort, builder's-risk carriers in the United States, catastrophe and micro-insurers in the Philippines and Kenya. These are named as kinds.
  • Inspection bodies and valuers. Peru's registered technical entities; municipal building control; licensed engineers' chambers; community construction management teams in federations of the urban poor (the Durban model, documented by UN-Habitat); independent inspectors and valuers as participants with their own reputation.
  • Data partners. The Centre for Affordable Housing Finance in Africa, housing-finance indicator networks and national statistics offices, for the open statistics a hub publishes and the loss data an insurer needs.
  • Precedents. Nepal's homeowner-driven reconstruction paid grants in three tranches against inspected stages; Germany's statutory payment schedule for homes bought from a developer fixes up to seven instalments by progress; Sindh's and Morocco's programmes paid by stage under pressure. These show the shape.

Open-contracting and civic-technology peers

These are the organisations that built the field the civic ring stands in. We treat them as peers and, where their platforms are in use, we interoperate rather than replace. None of them follows a project past a status label; that is the gap the engine fills, and the reason a peer is not a competitor.

Professional bodies, universities and training

The bodies that license, register and represent the people who build are the natural verifiers of a claimed page, reviewers of a seed guide and hosts of an event: the colleges of engineers and architects, the construction chambers, the contractor licensing boards and construction authorities, Nigeria's COREN and its Building Collapse Prevention Guild, and at home California's contractor licensing board. A licence from any of them is a sourced fact with an expiry; the engine never says "certified by Trustwork". Universities give a hub a neutral host, reviewers and research with consent; the national training services (SENCICO, SENA, TESDA, INFOTEP, Kenya's NITA, the Rwanda TVET Board, Mexico's competence certifier, San Diego's community colleges) give the informal worker the first rung of formality. Where a country runs recognition of prior learning, as Kenya has planned and Jordan has done for construction workers, the hub is a place where it can be recorded. No ministry or professional body recognises Trustwork certificates today; each is named here for its public work.

Manufacturers and suppliers as knowledge partners

As chapter 14 shows, a manufacturer's knowledge changed self-building when it reached the family as advice from a trusted person. On a hub, manufacturers and suppliers appear on the plan steps where they help, labelled as theirs, because they fit and never because they paid; their training pages and technical libraries are sources under licence; their experts answer consultations. The open, non-captive version of the promoter is the hub's mentor with a consultation record.

Licensed payment providers

Trustwork never holds money. A hub configures licensed providers behind the engine's payment port, and the first adapters for mobile money and instant payments are planned as a work package, not a connection. The rails in place, named for their public existence: card and bank transfers through licensed processors in the United States; M-Pesa in Kenya; MTN Mobile Money and the pan-African gateways Paystack and Flutterwave in Ghana and Nigeria; GCash, Maya and InstaPay in the Philippines; Bre-B in Colombia; Yape and Plin in Peru; SPEI in Mexico; Uzcard and Humo in Uzbekistan; the Dominican instant-payment system, planned by its central bank for 2027. Kenya is on the international list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring for money laundering (as reported), which licensed payment providers weigh.

Media

A hub's news is sourced, dated and corrected in public, and a newsroom that already works that way is the natural partner for editorial exchange, co-hosted events, and the magazine's version of the assurance report: one budget line followed into one building. Investigative newsrooms in the Philippines, the trade press of every launch country and the local outlets that covered San Diego's budget and housing decisions are the kinds of newsroom a hub would exchange with; their articles are sources under licence, never copied text. The hub is not a newspaper's competitor; it is the archive where a story's facts keep their sources.

Standards and public data we read

Not partners, but the ground the partners stand on: the Open Contracting Data Standard and OC4IDS; the CoST infrastructure data standard; W3C Verifiable Credentials, a web standard since 2019 and in its 2.0 version since 15 May 2025, with Open Badges 3.0 and the European Learning Model aligned to it, for the reputation passport; the Model Context Protocol for a person's own agent; OpenStreetMap under its open database licence for places; national digital identity systems reached only through licensed verifiers; the public sanctions lists the engine screens against; and the Digital Public Goods Standard (chapter 3). The engine will not call itself a digital public good until it is listed.

Contribuda

Trustwork is created and maintained by Contribuda, which gives the engine away under its licence, takes no licence or implementation fee and takes part only in the international layer, in a form to be decided (chapter 7). Nobody needs Contribuda to run a hub; its help, and its optional role as a tool for running a hub, follow Contribuda's own terms, and the transfer of the San Diego hub is described in chapter 23. Contribuda serves no one in a restricted place.

Every organisation that does its work well is welcome. A partnership begins with the first real case.
