A home that remembers

For most families the house is not one asset among several. It is the asset: the largest thing they will ever buy or build, and the thing they leave. It is built over years, lived in for generations, and it accumulates mistakes that nobody writes down, so each owner learns them again at their own cost. This chapter is the human centre of the book: a house record that continues after the build and passes with the home, learning from many homes that is free for everyone, and protection for the families who build with their own hands. The house record, the owner's adviser and the observatory are directions of this book, not yet designed in the engine's handbook.

Trustwork's part is practical. A house cannot remember by itself, a family cannot afford to learn every lesson alone, and nobody can insure or finance what has never been recorded. So the engine gives families the means to keep their house's memory, lessons that cost nothing to anyone who builds, the protection that today only buyers of professionally built homes enjoy, and, from many houses read together with consent, the statistics that let an insurer cover a house and a lender finance one.

The largest thing a family owns

The OECD, in a study of 2022, puts it in one line: housing is "households' largest lifetime investment and the majority of their wealth". Across OECD countries, owner-occupied housing is on average half of household wealth and more than sixty per cent of the middle class's. In the euro area, by the European Central Bank's household survey of 2021, 61.7 per cent of households own the home they live in, and its median value, 191,600 euros, is larger than the median household's entire assets. In the United States the Federal Reserve's survey of 2022 found the median homeowner worth 396,200 dollars and the median renter 10,400. Ownership is the line between two wealth worlds.

It is also what families leave. The British tax authority writes that estates under a million pounds "are likely to consist mainly of residential property and cash", and in a number of OECD countries most estates pay no inheritance tax at all, largely because of relief for close relatives and for the family home. The house passes, and the state steps aside to let it.

Where Trustwork is most needed it is harder. "Land and housing are the most important assets of the poor," the World Bank says, and the poor build them in stages, from savings and remittances, mostly without a drawing, a professional or a record. In Peru, by the Housing Ministry's own account, about seventy per cent of homes are self-built. The self-built house is savings, pension and security in one object, and it is the one large thing a family owns that nobody records.

What a house forgets

A house is built once and repaired for a century. The footing is poured in a season, the damp-proof course goes in on a morning, the roof is tied down or it is not; then the walls close and what was done well or badly disappears from sight. The leak appears three winters later, far from where the water enters. The crack opens over a decade, and nobody remembers that the ground was never tested. A house keeps no memory of its mistakes unless someone keeps it for it.

Yet the lessons are not mysteries. Buildings fail in the same ways everywhere, and mostly from water: in France, where claims under compulsory defect insurance have been counted since the 1980s, sixty-one per cent of insured defects in 2022 to 2024 were failures of water-tightness; in Japan, under a warranty scheme that ran from 1980 to 2008, water ingress was ninety per cent of all defects. After water comes structure. About eighty per cent of the deaths in the Kobe earthquake of 1995 were caused by collapsing houses, most of them built before the seismic code of 1981. The year a house was built largely decided whether it would fall on its family, and that year was written nowhere the family could read.

A house that remembers is a house that teaches.

One house, three generations

Told as the system will run it, in a hillside district of a Peruvian city. The people are roles, not persons; no figure is given because none is known.

One plot, one record, three generations
  1. The first year
    a young couple and a neighbourhood mason · a plot on the slope

    They launch the house on the hub, in Spanish. Their agent turns it into phases, trades, permits and budget ranges, and shows, before the first stage is paid, a short lesson on a footing on a slope. The mason takes the free course that goes with it; the certificate is the first the mason has ever held. An owner's adviser, a retired builder with a mandate from the couple, confirms the footing before it is covered. Three records, three authors, one stage.

  2. The twenty-fourth year
    the daughter · the same house, now hers

    The build file closed long ago; the house record went on, through a second storey and a damp stain under the stair recorded as a defect and then as a remedy. Her parents pass the house to her by deed, and the record passes with it, whole, with the mandates over it. She reads what her father never told her. When a licensed insurer covers the house, she grants it a time-limited read of the fields it needs. The insurer sees the record. It does not own it.

  3. The thirty-first year
    a buyer and the daughter · the deed

    The buyer reads thirty years of entries, each with the name of the person who confirmed it. The record changes hands at the deed, and the seller cannot edit the past. Because the family consented long ago, the next young couple on the slope read in their own language how a footing on this ground was done, and what went wrong when it was not.

The house record

Chapter 10 assembles the engine's record of a build into a file a licensed lender or insurer can read. But a build file ends at completion, and a house does not. The house record is what this chapter adds. The build file is its first chapter; then come the maintenance plan with its dated inspections, every repair and renovation with its quote and acceptance, the sale and the inheritance. A project becomes a house at completion, and the house keeps its record for as long as it stands.

Four places have already decided that a house should remember. Japan went furthest: a national council founded in 2010 sets the rules of its house history, a dwelling can receive a house ID that is inherited across owners, and by 2018 3.57 million dwellings had a history on file; a law in force since 2009 obliges the owners of certified long-life houses to keep maintenance records, and in the fiscal year 2024 it covered 39.3 per cent of new detached houses. The council's stated purpose is a move "from the age of building houses to the age of protecting, nurturing and handing them on". Flanders made its Woningpas free for every homeowner in 2018, one digital file per dwelling, more than three million of them by the operator's own count. France, by a law of 2021, requires a housing information record for every new home permitted from 2023, kept by the owner in an open format and handed to the buyer at the notarial deed. And the European Union, in the 2024 recast of its buildings directive, defined the digital building logbook in law for the first time.

One place shows the other side. England and Wales required sellers to supply a Home Information Pack from 2007 and suspended the duty in 2010, before repealing it, on research that more than ninety per cent of buyers wanted information about a home's condition and fewer than one in six trusted it when the seller provided it; experienced buyers preferred "an informal check by a tradesperson they already knew". A record is worth what its verification is worth. So every entry in a Trustwork house record shows who recorded it and who confirmed it, with the date and the digest of the evidence; an entry nobody confirmed is shown as the owner's word. A reader sees evidence levels, not a brochure, and no house score.

Learning from many homes

A house that remembers teaches its own family. Many houses that remember, read together with consent, teach a country. Every working defect observatory pairs a money event with a record event. France's compulsory decennial insurance produces claims, and since the 1980s the claims have fed a national database of defects by element and cause, used to plan prevention and to measure whether it worked. Denmark created a building defects fund for subsidised housing by law in 1986, financed by one per cent of construction cost, with inspections at one and five years and a legal duty to spread what it learns; by its own account, serious failures fell from more than a quarter of new buildings in the early 1990s to under eight per cent. And Alabama built a programme around a standard called FORTIFIED: an independent evaluator designates each house, a public register lists the designations, insurers must discount the wind premium for a designated home, as reported, and a state programme pays grants towards the work. In a study of more than forty thousand insured coastal properties after Hurricane Sally in 2020, the designated homes claimed between 55 and 74 per cent less often. A standard, a verified record of each house, a discount tied to the record and money at the moment of the work: every part of that mechanism is something a hub can hold.

So each hub, as chapter 10 describes, publishes aggregates from the projects whose families consented, with small cells suppressed and re-identification tested. This chapter adds the observatory: the same consented aggregates read over the life of the house and not only its build, so that a hub can see which roofs leak after ten years and which footings crack on which ground, and whether its own courses made a difference. For many of the countries in this book it could be the first defect statistics ever published for houses that families build themselves. Insurers can price what they can count; lenders can lend against what they can see; the next family builds knowing what the families before it learned. The family's own record is never the statistic.

Free, and good, for everyone

This chapter's promise has a word in it that costs money: free. The people who build most of the world's homes cannot pay for courses, and the evidence on what teaches them is clear. After Typhoon Haiyan in 2013 the government and the humanitarian shelter agencies in the Philippines agreed eight "Build Back Safer" messages for families and carpenters. The independent assessment of 2016 found that households had learned "by watching people they saw as the most skilled local carpenters"; that training for the wider community "was reported only to be effective when materials were distributed to participants in conjunction with the training"; and that, in one agency's words, "many can quote the key messages, price is the main barrier". One carpenter, well versed in the safer methods, said what every builder knows: "ultimately I build what people ask me to build." The owner gives the order, so the owner has to be the learner too. And people will learn in large numbers when the lesson is in their own language, practical and within their means: a Spanish-language course on building your own house safely, from Mexico's national university, had 159,540 learners enrolled as of November 2026.

So the rule, which chapter 14 carries further: the lessons a family and a builder need to build safely and to check a stage are free for everyone, in every engine language, and the certificate, checkable by number, is the record. A hub or a training body may offer other courses for a fee, as the learning design allows; the lessons that keep a house standing are never behind one. The lesson arrives where the evidence says it works: on the plan step itself, in the family's language, before the stage is paid, so that a family about to accept a footing is shown what to look at, and the mason about to pour it is offered the course and the certificate that follows. And the trusted local builder, the teacher people actually learn from, gains a record as a mentor whose students' certificates carry their name.

Protected

Where a family can buy protection, it rests on five pillars: a registered builder; a warranty or defect insurance that outlives the builder; inspected stages; protected deposits; and a body to take a dispute to. Japan learned what happens when one pillar is hollow. Its law of 2000 gave every new house a ten-year warranty for its structure and against water ingress; then a scandal of falsified structural calculations bankrupted a seller, and the owners "had to take out additional loans to buy a new unit on top of having already bought the one which was built based on the fake structure statement". Since 2009 every housing business in Japan must hold defect insurance or a deposit. A warranty was a promise; the law made it a funded one.

Now read the five pillars from the hillside. Every one of them presupposes a professional builder, a contract, a record of stages and an institution that can be found afterwards. Kenya's construction authority has registered contractors since 2011, and its regulations exempt "a skilled construction worker or construction site supervisor" from registration as a contractor, which is exactly the person who builds most Kenyan homes; such workers can be accredited, but the protections tied to a registered contractor do not reach the family that hires one. Denmark's compulsory building-damage insurance exempts housing that the consumers build themselves; Arizona's recovery fund pays the victims of licensed contractors only. The family that builds with a neighbourhood mason, pays cash by the week and keeps the plan in its head has none of the five pillars, in Nairobi and in Phoenix alike. This is not a judgement of the mason. It is a description of the record that was never made.

Trustwork cannot pass a warranty law and does not pretend to. What it gives is the part of protection that comes from a record, to the families who have nothing else. The plan carries the protection facts from the country pack at the step where the family chooses a builder: which register to check, what warranty applies, where a dispute goes; and where none exists, it says so plainly. Hidden works are accepted with dated, placed photographs before they are covered. An independent inspector and an owner's adviser, a person on the family's side who knows building, act as participants under a mandate from the family: they inspect stages, record defects and confirm acceptances, their reputation grows from evidence like anyone's, and they are paid by the family or by a programme, never by the builder. Money moves only through licensed providers the hub configures, against accepted evidence, and Trustwork never holds it. A dispute goes to the counterparty with the record in front of both, and then wherever the country's law sends it. And at the end there is a house record that exists, which is more than most families in the world have ever had.

The lines

The record belongs to the family: not to the hub, not to the builder, not to Contribuda, and not to any lender or insurer that has read it. It passes with the home by an explicit act at sale or inheritance, the seller cannot edit the past, and it can be exported in an open format so that nobody is locked in. Nothing in it is shared without the family's consent, and the hub's staff cannot read it except through the one door every private record has: a scoped, time-limited case grant approved by a second person, which the family can see. There is no house score and no family score. No resale premium is promised: no study known to us measures one. Lenders and insurers read with consent, for a stated period and a stated set of fields; they never own the record. Aggregates are published under an open licence and never sold. And no entry is ever marked verified without a named confirmer.

What stays human

Choosing the mason. Standing in the trench. Accepting a stage. Deciding what to share, and with whom. Teaching a daughter where the water comes in. Handing over the keys and the record. The agent drafts, reminds and compiles; it never approves, never accepts, never consents and never inherits. A house is a family's memory in brick. The engine only keeps the notes.

The record belongs to the family. The lessons belong to everyone.
